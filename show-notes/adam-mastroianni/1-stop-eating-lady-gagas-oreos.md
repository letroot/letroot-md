# Stop Eating Lady Gaga's Oreos

## Summary
[[Adam Mastroianni]] traces how "selling out" went from a cultural sin in the 1990s (Pearl Jam refusing music videos, punk's anti-consumerist ethos) to something fans now actively celebrate (Lady Gaga's *Chromatica* Oreos, Ice Spice's Dunkin' drink, a Goldman Sachs CEO DJing Lollapalooza). He attributes this shift to two forces: [[Poptimism]] (the idea that popular art deserves equal respect, which eroded critics' willingness to distinguish art from schlock) and what he calls the **[[Great Switcheroo]]** -- as it got harder to become rich, it got easier to become famous, so people now identify with celebrities as "temporarily unknown celebrities" rather than resenting them as they resent billionaires. His prescription: revive the stigma of selling out and the line between art and advertisement, without reviving the snobbish gatekeeping of old-school rockism.

## Key Insights
*   **[[Poptimism]]**: The critical stance that popular/commercial art is just as meritorious as any other art, originally a reaction against [[Rockism]] (the snobbish idea that only "authentic" guitar music counts). Poptimism's overcorrection removed critics' willingness to judge art quality at all, collapsing the line between art and entertainment.
*   **The [[Great Switcheroo]]**: The public has soured on billionaires (no rich person cracks 50% approval in YouGov polls) but warmed dramatically to celebrities (Lady Gaga: 97% recognition, 61% approval). As upward mobility through wealth has stalled, upward mobility through fame/virality feels newly attainable, so people root for celebrities as proxies for themselves.
*   **From Painful to Profitable Virality**: Early internet virality (Star Wars Kid, Numa Numa Kid, Afro Ninja) was a source of shame and bullying. Once monetization infrastructure existed (YouTube Partner Program, Stripe, Patreon), overnight fame flipped from something to escape to something to chase.
*   **Fandom as Unpaid Labor**: Stan armies (BTS Army, Beyhive, Swifties) frame promoting their favorite celebrity's commercial interests as "promoting our own voices," while functionally doing free marketing and enforcement work for record labels.
*   **The Line Between Art and Advertisement**: Mastroianni's actual complaint isn't that artists make or want money -- it's "commerce under the guise of art," where the emotional connection of art is used as a vehicle for brand deals, diluting the artist into "a credit default swap with a discography attached."

## Coach's Corner
**On Personal Branding & Selling Out:** As you build your SaaS and your own public presence, there's a version of this same trap: using genuine-seeming content (build-in-public threads, "authentic" storytelling) purely as a vehicle to sell a product, without any real substance behind it. Audiences today are more tolerant of overt commerce than ever, but the founders who build the most durable trust are the ones who keep a real line between "here's something true and useful" and "here's my pitch." Don't become a credit-default-swap with a Twitter bio attached.

**On Riding vs. Resisting Cultural Currents:** Mastroianni shows how a whole culture's norms (anti-consumerism to hyper-commercialism) can flip in 30 years. As a founder, don't assume the current growth tactics (creator partnerships, influencer marketing, meme marketing) are permanent norms -- they're artifacts of this specific "Great Switcheroo" moment. Build a business that would still work if audience tolerance for overt commercialism swings back.

**On the Great Switcheroo & Your Own Ambition:** The essay is really about where people direct their hope for upward mobility. If your own motivation is drifting toward "build an audience/followers" instead of "build a valuable product," notice that -- audience-chasing is competing in the same heavy-tailed, celebrity-driven attention economy Mastroianni describes, which is a much harder and less controllable game than building recurring SaaS revenue.

**On Maintaining Integrity as You Grow:** As your business grows, you will get chances to monetize your voice/brand in ways disconnected from the actual value you provide (sponsored content, affiliate pushes, "influencer" partnerships). Decide now, while the stakes are small, where your own line between art/craft and advertisement sits, so you're not negotiating it under pressure later.

## Action Items
*   [ ] **Audit your own "Oreos"**: List any part of your public content/marketing that is closer to pure commerce dressed up as authenticity, and decide if it matches the brand you actually want.
*   [ ] **Research [[Poptimism]] and [[Rockism]]**: Read further on this debate to sharpen your own thinking about quality vs. popularity, useful for how you evaluate your own product and content decisions.
*   [ ] **Write your personal "selling out" line**: Define, concretely, what kind of monetization of your name/brand you would and wouldn't do, before you're offered the deal.
