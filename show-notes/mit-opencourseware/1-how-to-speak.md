# How to Speak

## Summary

[[Patrick Winston]] argues that communication is not a soft accessory to technical ability: it is one of the main ways a person gets ideas valued and accepted. He presents a practical repertoire for starting, structuring, teaching, persuading, being remembered, and ending a talk. The lecture moves from the [[empowerment promise]] and repeated cycles through [[verbal punctuation]], careful use of time and place, boards and props, restrained slides, and the five S's of memorable work: [[symbols]], [[slogan]], [[surprise]], [[salient idea]], and [[story]]. For a founder, the central lesson is to treat every [[SaaS]] explanation as a designed experience that helps an audience understand, remember, and act.

## Key Insights

### The Communication Equation

* **Knowledge Beats Innate Talent**: Winston frames communication quality as a function of [[knowledge]], [[practice]], and [[talent]], with talent deliberately made small. A person with more domain knowledge and deliberate practice can outperform someone with more natural ability. This is an argument for building a personal [[armoury of speaking techniques]] rather than waiting to feel charismatic.
* **Ideas Need a Delivery System**: A strong idea can be ignored if its packaging is confusing. [[Communication]] is part of the work, not an afterthought; presenting an idea clearly increases the chance that people will value and accept it.
* **Build a Personal Repertoire**: Watch speakers you admire, diagnose what makes them effective, and collect techniques that fit your own [[personal style]]. The goal is not imitation but a reusable library of moves.

### How to Start: Empowerment and Promise

* **Start With Empowerment**: Do not open with a joke by default. At the start, the audience is still settling in, adapting to your voice, and putting away distractions. A joke is often too fragile at this point.
* **Make an Empowerment Promise**: Tell the audience what they will know or be able to do at the end that they could not do at the beginning. The promise answers the audience's implicit question, "Why should I spend this time with you?"
* **Promise a Concrete Change**: A useful opening promises a specific gain, not a vague claim that the talk will be interesting. In a founder's [[pitch]], promise a decision, diagnosis, insight, or capability the listener will leave with.

### How to Structure: Cycle, Fence, Punctuation, Question

* **Cycle the Core Idea**: Circle back to the central idea multiple times. Winston's version of "tell them what you will tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them" is not an insult to intelligence; at any moment, some portion of an audience is mentally fogged out. Repetition raises the probability that everyone catches the point.
* **Build a Fence Around the Idea**: Define what an idea is and is not. Distinguish it from adjacent ideas, competing approaches, and likely misunderstandings. A technical talk might say that one algorithm resembles [[Jones's algorithm]] except that Jones's is exponential while yours is linear.
* **Use Verbal Punctuation**: Number sections, announce transitions, and state where the audience is in the argument. Verbal landmarks create a seam in the talk and give distracted listeners a place to get back on the bus.
* **Ask a Well-Calibrated Question**: A question is another reset button. It must not be so obvious that people feel embarrassed answering, or so hard that nobody can respond. After asking, wait: roughly seven seconds of silence can feel long to the speaker but gives the audience time to think.
* **Use Landmarks as Navigation**: [[Signposting]], repetition, fences, and questions reduce the audience's cognitive load. A listener should always know the current section, the problem under discussion, and why the next move follows.

### Time and Place Are Part of the Talk

* **Choose Alert Time**: Around 11:00 a.m. is a strong lecture time because people are generally awake, not immediately post-meal, and less likely to be preparing for sleep. In a product demo or sales call, schedule when the buyer can actually attend rather than treating calendar placement as administrative trivia.
* **Keep the Room Bright**: Dim light signals sleep. Full lighting keeps the audience awake and lets the speaker maintain contact. Slides do not become more useful if the audience is looking at them through closed eyelids.
* **Case the Room**: Visit the venue in advance. Learn the sight lines, lighting, acoustics, display position, exits, audience size, and technical quirks. A [[rehearsal]] is partly content practice and partly environmental reconnaissance.
* **Match Room Size to Attendance**: A room should be populated enough to feel alive, not so large that a small audience looks absent. Context and perceived energy affect attention.
* **Design for the Actual Audience**: Time, place, and audience condition are communication variables. A good message delivered into an unsuitable environment is still a poor experience.

### Tools for Teaching and Informing

* **Use the Board for Teaching**: A [[blackboard]] or whiteboard is suited to informing, teaching, and lecturing. Its graphic quality makes it easy to draw relationships while its speed roughly matches the speed at which people can absorb an idea.
* **Give Your Hands a Job**: A board also gives the speaker a natural physical target. Pointing, writing, and moving through a diagram can prevent closed-off gestures such as hands in pockets or behind the back.
* **Exploit Physical Props**: A well-chosen [[prop]] can make an abstract relationship concrete and memorable. A bicycle wheel can demonstrate a counterintuitive mechanical idea; a pendulum and steel ball can make conservation of energy emotionally legible. The object is not decoration: it creates a model the audience can mentally manipulate.
* **Use Embodied Demonstration**: Physical demonstrations invite [[empathetic mirroring]]: the audience can imagine feeling the motion and constraints rather than only looking at a static picture. This is one reason a live object can teach differently from a slide.
* **Use Slides to Expose, Not Teach**: Slides are better for exposing ideas in a [[job talk]] or conference presentation than for walking novices through every step. Do not make a slide deck carry the entire lesson.
* **Keep Slides Sparse**: Remove background junk, unnecessary titles, logos, bullets, and excess words. Use large, readable type and plenty of [[white space]]. A slide should be a condiment to the speaker, not the main event.
* **Never Read the Slides**: The audience already knows how to read. Reading text that is visible on the screen divides attention and makes the speaker redundant.
* **Respect the One Language Processor**: People cannot fully read dense text and listen to a speaker at the same time. If the slide contains the important explanation, the audience will remember the slide rather than what the speaker says.
* **Avoid Laser Pointer Drift**: A laser pointer turns the speaker away from the audience and can make the talk feel like a tennis match. Put a static arrow or marker on the image instead.
* **Test the Minimum Font Size**: Print the deck or lay its slides out on a table. This exposes excessive density, insufficient air, and too much text. Small fonts are often a symptom of a talk trying to say too much.
* **Use a Rare Complexity Slide Sparingly**: An intentionally overwhelming slide can communicate complexity, but it is a one-off device, not a default visual language. A presentation full of such slides becomes unreadable.

### Informing, Inspiring, and Teaching How to Think

* **Open Informing Talks With a Promise**: An informative lecture should still tell people what they will gain. Information without a destination feels like a data dump.
* **Inspire With Passion**: Winston's survey found that students are inspired when someone tells them they can do it, helps them see a problem in a new way, or exhibits genuine passion. [[Passion]] is not theatrical hype; it is visible care for the problem and its implications.
* **Make the Coolness Visible**: A promise can include the surprising transformation the audience will witness, such as reducing a computation that would outlast the solar system to a result produced in seconds. The audience should feel why the subject matters before receiving all the mechanics.
* **Teach Through Stories**: Humans are [[storytelling animals]]. To teach people how to think, give them stories, the questions needed to interrogate those stories, mechanisms for analyzing them, ways to combine them, and methods for evaluating reliability.
* **Treat Thinking as Model Evaluation**: Good education trains people to ask what happened, why it happened, what evidence supports it, what constraints shaped it, and whether the story is reliable. This applies to [[product analytics]], customer research, and founder narratives.

### Persuading Through Job Talks and Technical Talks

* **Establish Vision Quickly**: In a faculty job talk, Winston says the audience may give a candidate only five minutes to show both [[vision]] and evidence of having done something. The practical translation is to make the first five minutes count.
* **Define Vision as Problem Plus Novel Approach**: Vision is not a grand adjective. It is a problem that matters paired with a new way of approaching it. Explain who cares, what is not yet understood, and what changes if the problem is solved.
* **Situate the Work**: Place the work in [[time]], [[place]], and [[field]]. Explain what has been tried, where the gap is, why the gap matters, and how the work fits a larger movement.
* **Show the Work as a Series of Steps**: To demonstrate that you have done something, enumerate the steps required to realize the vision: specify behavior, enumerate constraints, implement a system, and demonstrate it. You do not need to claim that every possible step is complete; you need to make the path and your contribution legible.
* **Use a Sandwich Structure**: A technical talk can open with the problem and vision, develop the approach and evidence, and conclude by mirroring the opening with explicit contributions.
* **Practice With Naive Listeners**: Colleagues who already know the work will hallucinate missing explanations into the presentation. A supervisor may be a poor debugger for this reason. Practice with people who do not know the material and invite unusually direct criticism.
* **Practice for Flack, Not Comfort**: A practice session should reveal where the story breaks, where terms are unexplained, and where attention disappears. Friendly reassurance is less useful than feedback that helps you survive a real audience.

### Getting Recognized: The Five S's

* **The Five S's Create Handles**: Winston's "Winston star" is a memory architecture for helping good work become recognizable. The elements are [[symbol]], [[slogan]], [[surprise]], [[salient idea]], and [[story]].
* **Symbol**: Give the work a visual or verbal emblem. A distinctive diagram, object, name, or image lets people point to the idea and retrieve it later.
* **Slogan**: Compress the work into a phrase that provides a handle. A good slogan is short enough to repeat and specific enough to distinguish the contribution.
* **Surprise**: Identify the counterintuitive result or expectation reversal. In Winston's example, a system learns something definite from one example rather than needing a million examples.
* **Salient Idea**: Choose the one idea that sticks out. A thesis with too many equally prominent good ideas can become impossible to summarize. Salience is about memorability and distinctiveness, not merely importance.
* **Story**: Tell how the work was done, how it works, and why it matters. A story gives the symbol, slogan, surprise, and salient idea a causal sequence.
* **Package Ideas Without Distorting Them**: Recognition is not vanity or empty personal branding. Ideas are like children going into the world; packaging them responsibly helps them be judged for their actual value rather than lost through neglect.

### How to End

* **End With Contributions**: The final slide should be titled [[Contributions]], not "Conclusions," "Questions," or "Thank you." The audience cares about what you did, what changed, and what they should remember.
* **Keep Collaborators Visible Earlier**: Credit collaborators on the opening slide or where their work is discussed. A final slide containing a giant list of names can make the speaker's own contribution unclear.
* **Keep the Contributions Slide Up During Questions**: The slide should continue working while the audience asks questions and leaves. It is valuable real estate, so use it to reinforce the work.
* **Signal Completion Without Apologizing**: A bare "thank you" is legal but weak because it can imply the audience stayed only out of politeness. Use a clear convention, a final joke when appropriate, a benediction, or a salute to the audience.
* **Salute the Audience**: A strong close can acknowledge the audience's time and place, say what was stimulating about the exchange, and express a genuine reason to return. This ends with relationship and respect rather than an apology for taking time.
* **Design the Last Second**: The last spoken sentence and the last visible slide are part of the product. Decide the desired final feeling—clarity, urgency, confidence, gratitude, or possibility—and build the ending toward it.

## Coach's Corner

### On Your SaaS Message

For [[Daniel]]'s early-stage SaaS, the immediate application is to stop explaining the product as a feature inventory. Build an [[empowerment promise]] for every important audience:

* For a prospect: "In the next ten minutes, you will see how to remove [[pain point]] X without adding another manual workflow."
* For an investor: "By the end, you will understand why this painful workflow is becoming urgent, how we reach the first narrow segment, and what evidence says the wedge can expand."
* For a teammate: "After this walkthrough, you will know the one user outcome we optimize for and the trade-offs we will not make."

That promise becomes a filter. If a slide, feature, metric, or anecdote does not help deliver it, move it out. Your early-stage advantage is not having the most information; it is being able to make the value proposition legible before the listener's attention expires.

### On Founder-Market Fit and Positioning

Use the [[fence around an idea]] to make your category precise. State what your product is, what it is not, who it is for, and which adjacent alternatives it should not be confused with. A useful positioning sentence has this shape: [[Product]] helps [[specific customer]] achieve [[specific outcome]] in [[specific context]], unlike [[alternative]], which requires [[meaningful trade-off]].

Then cycle it. Say the positioning in the homepage headline, the first minute of a demo, the opening of a sales call, the first paragraph of an outbound email, and the close of a case study. Repetition across contexts is not boring when the audience is encountering your idea at different moments. It is [[message-market fit]] being built.

### On Marketing and the Five S's

Your marketing needs its own five S's. Pick a symbol that can travel through your [[landing page]], product UI, demo, and social posts. Create a slogan that a customer can repeat without your help. Find the surprise in the product: perhaps a workflow that appears to require a team can be reduced to a few minutes, or a hidden cost is much larger than buyers assume. Select one salient idea rather than presenting six competing differentiators. Finally, tell the customer story from painful before-state to measurable after-state.

Do not confuse this with inventing a gimmick. The symbol and slogan should make the real mechanism easier to remember; the surprise should be supported by evidence; the story should preserve the customer's experience. This is [[ethical marketing]] through better retrieval, not manipulation.

### On Demos and Product Education

Apply the tool distinction directly: use a live product, diagram, or physical metaphor when you are teaching how the system works; use sparse slides when you are exposing the market, evidence, or strategic argument. In a demo, let the audience see the sequence at the speed they can absorb it. Narrate the user's decision, not every click.

Before a demo, draw the workflow on a board: trigger, user action, system response, outcome. This can expose unnecessary complexity before you build another feature. Use one prop or visual model for the hardest concept. If your product has a difficult data flow, animate or sketch the transformation rather than putting a paragraph of architecture on a slide.

### On Sales Calls and Persuasion

Treat the first five minutes of a [[sales call]] like a job talk. Establish the customer's important problem, show that you understand the context, state your novel approach, and provide early evidence that you have done something. Do not wait until minute twenty to explain why the prospect should care.

Ask calibrated questions and leave room for answers. A question that is too broad produces polite noise; one that is too obvious produces no insight. Ask about the current workflow, the cost of delay, the failed workaround, and the moment the pain becomes urgent. Then wait long enough for the customer to think. Silence is not a failure in a discovery call; it is part of the instrument.

### On Customer Research and Story Reliability

Winston's approach to teaching people how to think is a useful [[customer research]] discipline. Every customer story is a hypothesis, not a fact. Ask: What exactly happened? What evidence supports it? Was the outcome caused by the product or by a confounding change? How often does the problem occur? Which part of the story is memorable but unrepresentative?

Build a small repository of stories tagged by customer segment, problem, trigger, workaround, outcome, and evidence quality. Use these stories to improve onboarding, positioning, and roadmap choices, but do not let one vivid anecdote override a pattern in the data. This is how you combine [[qualitative research]] with [[product analytics]].

### On Investor and Team Presentations

Open an investor or team presentation with the problem and the promise, not your company history. Within five minutes, make the vision and the evidence legible. Situate the product in time, place, and field: why this market now, which customer segment first, what alternatives exist, and what has changed in the environment.

Use the sandwich structure: problem and vision, approach and proof, explicit contributions and next milestone. Replace generic conclusions with concrete contributions such as: a validated segment, a repeatable acquisition channel, a retention improvement, a workflow that customers complete faster, or an experiment that killed a bad assumption. Keep collaborators and thanks in the body or opening; keep the last slide for what the company has learned and built.

### On Founder Practice and Feedback

Create a deliberate [[communication practice]] loop. Record one five-minute explanation of the product each week. Watch it once for content, once for structure, and once with the sound off for body language and visual clutter. Give the recording to a friend who does not know the product and ask them to write down the promise, problem, customer, mechanism, and next action. Any answer they cannot recover is a fence, cycle, or punctuation problem.

Do not practice only with your cofounder or closest technical teammate. Familiar people fill in gaps because they already know the story. Recruit a naive listener, a skeptical buyer, and a technically capable but context-free friend. Ask them to be uncomfortable and specific. The goal is not to feel polished in rehearsal; it is to discover where the audience falls off the bus.

### On Productivity and Attention

The opening rule about laptops is a reminder to design for [[attention management]]. When you are presenting, remove competing channels. When you are building, do the same: write the promise for the work session, close unrelated tabs, and define the one output that proves the session happened.

Use verbal punctuation in your own week. At the start of a work block, state the outcome. At the midpoint, name what is done and what remains. At the end, record the contribution. This turns a vague founder day into a sequence of visible cycles and makes it easier to resume after interruptions.

### On Confidence and Recognition

You do not need to become a naturally magnetic speaker. Knowledge of the customer, practice with the message, and a small set of reliable techniques are enough to create meaningful improvement. Confidence should come from evidence that your message survives unfamiliar listeners, not from repeating it privately until it feels comfortable.

Recognition is useful because an unremembered product is difficult to refer, recommend, or buy. Give your work a handle, but make the handle honest. A memorable name, phrase, diagram, or customer story should lead people back to a real outcome. Your goal is not to be famous for its own sake; it is to prevent useful work from being ignored.

## Action Items

* [ ] **Write three empowerment promises**: Create one each for a prospect, an investor, and a teammate; keep each to one sentence and a measurable audience outcome.
* [ ] **Draft the product fence**: Write what [[Product]] is, what it is not, who it serves first, and how it differs from the two most common alternatives.
* [ ] **Find the five S's**: Choose a symbol, slogan, surprise, salient idea, and customer story for the current SaaS wedge.
* [ ] **Rebuild the opening**: Rewrite the first five minutes of your demo or pitch so the problem, vision, approach, and early evidence are clear before feature detail.
* [ ] **Cut the deck**: Remove dense text, background decoration, redundant titles, and slides that merely repeat your script; test every remaining slide for readable type and generous white space.
* [ ] **Create a contributions slide**: End the deck with what the company has learned, built, and proven, rather than a generic conclusions or thank-you slide.
* [ ] **Run a naive-listener rehearsal**: Present to someone unfamiliar with the product and ask them to reconstruct your promise, customer, problem, mechanism, and next step.
* [ ] **Record a weekly explanation**: Capture a five-minute product explanation and review it for structure, verbal punctuation, distracting gestures, and unnecessary words.
* [ ] **Schedule discovery at high attention**: Move important customer conversations to times when the buyer is alert and protect the call from avoidable environmental distractions.
* [ ] **Build a story evidence bank**: Store customer stories with the trigger, before-state, intervention, outcome, and evidence quality; review the patterns before making roadmap claims.
* [ ] **Practice calibrated questions**: Prepare five discovery questions that are neither obvious nor impossible, and deliberately leave seven seconds of silence after the most important ones.
