# This Executive Coach Works With The Top 0.01% & is Sharing Her Secrets | Dr. Julie Gurner

## Summary
Dr. Julie Gurner is one of the most sought-after executive performance coaches in the country, with a two-year waiting list and a client roster of CEOs, founders, and billionaire types. In this conversation with [[Michael Easter]] on the 2% podcast, she shares the psychological tools she uses with the top 0.01% to unlock extreme performance. The discussion ranges from audacity and imaginary rules to channeling anger as fuel, the myth that money doesn't buy happiness, and why humility is often terrible advice. Dr. Gurner brings a unique perspective shaped by her early career working in a supermax prison, where she learned that a bad 10 minutes can ruin a life -- a lesson that translates directly to business decisions made under emotional pressure.

## Key Insights

- **The_Supermax_Lesson**: [[Impulse Control]]. Working in a level five supermax facility taught Dr. Gurner that a bad 10 minutes -- a moment of rage or impulse -- can permanently alter the trajectory of your life. This applies in business too: decisions made in fear or anger can be similarly destructive. The ability to sit with strong emotions without acting on them destructively is a skill she developed young and carries into every executive coaching session.

- **Audacity_as_a_Trait**: [[Audacity]]. The single trait Dr. Gurner sees most in people who reach the top 0.01% is audacity -- an unawareness that they couldn't try for something. While most people follow [[Imaginary Rules]] (you shouldn't go after that contract, you shouldn't be in that room), the audacious simply go for it. They take the side path -- a dirt road -- while everyone else is trudging through the crowded main path. The disposition is "what if it goes right" instead of "what if it goes wrong."

- **Imaginary_Rules**: Most rules people follow are artificial -- societal conventions about what you can and cannot attempt based on your station in life. The top 0.01% operate with a near-complete unawareness that these rules exist. Once you break an imaginary rule once and succeed, you prove to yourself you can do it again in other areas of life.

- **The_Repetitive_Reflex**: [[Strengths vs Weaknesses]]. High performers are not great at everything. They are exceptional at a few things and they put enormous force behind those strengths. They don't try to fix their weaknesses to average -- they lean into what is above average and push it to outlier outcomes. Example: [[Elon Musk]] is exceptional at company building and vision, but has [[Gwynne Shotwell]] handle operations at SpaceX because he recognizes he is not the best at day-to-day management.

- **Haunting_Agitation**: The feeling that needles at you, telling you that you could be doing more, should be doing more. Most people carry this and it goes unfulfilled. Dr. Gurner describes this as a tragedy. The saying: "what you don't pay attention to as a whisper will become a scream." Most dreams die not from impossibility but from unwillingness to make the sacrifices required.

- **Sacrifice_and_the_Plan**: Your life reflects the sacrifices you're willing to make for the thing you say you want. Most people say they want something but are unwilling to support it with action. There is always a plan: sit down, map out the finances, figure out what it would take. The people who succeed are the ones who actually follow through on the sacrifices -- living in small apartments while siblings buy homes, dealing with resentment, keeping the long view.

- **Money_Buys_Happiness**: [[Wealth and Happiness]]. Dr. Gurner pushes back on the popular narrative that money doesn't buy happiness. Money buys good healthcare, physical therapy, time with family, better food, optionality, and reduced hassle. Research supports that financial comfort increases happiness. The very wealthy optimize for hassle reduction everywhere -- private planes mean being home for dinner. If you're unhappy with money, you'll probably be unhappy without it.

- **Survival_Mode_After_Success**: [[Scarcity Trauma]]. Some successful people remain trapped in survival mode, afraid everything could disappear. JK Rowling told Oprah she still feared losing it all; Oprah simply said "I realize I'll always be rich." Poverty leaves a residue -- a nervous system awareness of potential loss. Getting beyond it requires examining the actual evidence rather than living in the fear.

- **Stoicism_vs_Emotional_Channeling**: [[Stoicism]] can be useful but suppressing negative emotions is often counterproductive. Anger, rage, and spite are enormous sources of energy that can be channeled into building empires. [[Michael Jordan]] used perceived slights as fuel. [[Jeff Bezos]] was laughed at publicly for wanting to go to space -- that kind of experience drives you. The key is channeling destructive energy productively rather than self-destructively.

- **Humble_is_Terrible_Advice**: [[Humility]]. If most people were more humble, they would disappear into the ground. Devaluing yourself does the world a disservice because people who would value what you bring can't find you. There is a difference between owning what you're great at and being arrogant. The carpenter who shows you pictures of his incredible millwork wins the job over the one who says "I'm not really that good."

- **Reframing_Obstacles**: Whatever challenge lies before you, reframe it. The way you frame something determines how you approach it. There are always multiple equally true ways to think about the same challenge -- some get you amped up and ready to tackle it, others make you feel defeated.

- **Leaning_Into_Quirkiness**: [[Conformity]] is the wrong path. If you push to fit in with everyone else, you become everyone else, and your outcomes won't differ. Quirky interests and traits are what make you memorable and create outlier outcomes. A massive weightlifter who is also passionate about vintage floor tiles and curtains is more interesting and more connected than one who only lifts.

- **Exposure_Open_Doors**: You can't see what's on the other side of the door until you open it. Dr. Gurner's entire career trajectory shifted when she drove through the Gold Coast of Connecticut in grad school and saw that wealth was not rare -- it was everywhere. Once you see what's possible, your range of vision expands. One client who couldn't afford conferences would pay to sit in the hotel lobby and meet people.

- **The_Farm_Life**: Having a high-tech internal world and stepping outside to 1910 Amish country provides a complete cognitive break. Nobody cares about technology or politics. Conversations are about peach crops and frost damage. It keeps you grounded in what actually matters -- community, being a good neighbor, looking out for each other.

## Coach's Corner

**On Audacity and Imaginary Rules:**
Daniel, this is the most important insight for where you are right now. As an early-stage SaaS founder, the biggest risk is not moving too fast -- it's following imaginary rules about what a founder in your position should or shouldn't attempt. The audacious founders Dr. Gurner describes don't ask permission. They don't wonder if they should go after the big contract or the enterprise deal. They just do it, and a percentage of the time it works. You are currently on the crowded main path, trudging through the same playbook every indie SaaS founder follows. The dirt road is right there. What would it look like if you stopped asking "what if it goes wrong" and started asking "what if it goes right"?

**On Leaning Into Strengths:**
The repetitive reflex insight is critical for a solo founder. You cannot be great at everything. You cannot even be good at everything. If you are above average at product and technical work, put enormous force behind that. Don't spend 80% of your time trying to become average at design or sales -- hire or partner for those. The separation between you and competitors comes from pushing your natural strengths to outlier levels, not from bringing weaknesses up to mediocre. Audit your week: how much time is going into things you're naturally gifted at versus things you're grinding through at a below-average level?

**On Channeling Emotional Energy:**
The stoicism point is relevant to the emotional rollercoaster of building a SaaS. There will be days of frustration, anger at bugs, rage at lost deals. The temptation is to suppress it, be stoic, push it down. Dr. Gurner says that's a mistake. That energy is fuel. The founders who build empires on spite and "I'll show them" energy are channeling anger productively. When a deal falls through or a deployment breaks, don't try to be calm about it. Get angry. Then channel that anger into shipping faster, fixing the issue harder, and reaching out to the next customer with more intensity.

**On Haunting Agitation:**
Pay attention to the whisper. If there's something you know you should be doing with your product or business and you keep postponing it, that agitation will only get louder. Dr. Gurner's advice: form a plan. Map it out. What would it actually take to do the thing? The cost is often less than you think when you break it down. Most dreams die because people never sit down and do the math on what the sacrifice would actually require.

**On Humility:**
As a founder, being humble about what you're building is often the worst thing you can do. If you know your product solves a real problem, own it. When talking to potential customers, investors, or partners, don't downplay what you've built. The carpenter who shows you pictures of his incredible millwork wins the job. The one who says "I'm not that good" doesn't. Your product may not be perfect, but you should be able to articulate what it does well with genuine confidence. That's not arrogance -- that's how people who need what you've built find you.

**On Exposure and Opening Doors:**
Your range of vision is limited by what you've been exposed to. If you're not connecting with other founders at higher levels, find ways to be in those rooms. The client who couldn't afford conferences but sat in the hotel lobby -- that's the energy. Go to the events, join the Slack groups, reach out to the people two steps ahead of you. Once you see what's possible, your own ambition recalibrates. You can't build toward something you can't see.

## Action Items

- [ ] **Identify three imaginary rules** you're following in your SaaS business (e.g., "I shouldn't reach out to enterprise customers yet" or "I shouldn't charge more than X"). Break at least one this week.
- [ ] **Audit your time allocation**: Track one week of work and categorize what went into natural strengths versus things you're below average at. Create a plan to offload at least 30% of the below-average work.
- [ ] **Reframe one current obstacle**: Take the biggest challenge you're facing right now and write down three equally true ways to think about it. Choose the framing that makes you want to tackle it.
- [ ] **Stop being humble about your product**: Write a one-paragraph description of what your product does exceptionally well, without hedging or downplaying. Use this in your next customer conversation.
- [ ] **Get exposure to the next level**: Find one room to be in this month -- a conference, a community, a conversation with someone two steps ahead of you. If money is an issue, get creative (lobby strategy).
- [ ] **Map the plan for your haunting agitation**: Whatever that thing is that you know you should be doing but keep postponing, sit down and actually map out what it would take financially, time-wise, and logistically.
