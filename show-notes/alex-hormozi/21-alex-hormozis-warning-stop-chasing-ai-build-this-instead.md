# Alex Hormozi’s Warning: Stop Chasing AI, Build This Instead!

## Summary
[[Alex Hormozi]] joins [[Steven Bartlett]] on [[The Diary Of A CEO]] for a wide-ranging conversation about building durable companies, using [[Artificial Intelligence]] without outsourcing judgment, and choosing reality, trust, and long-term execution over fashionable shortcuts. Hormozi argues that founders are often using AI to do low-value work faster, automating processes that are not their true bottleneck, and building products that the models will soon commoditize; the better question is whether the technology is producing more money or more durable customer value. The conversation then moves through [[Product-Market Fit]], retention, pricing, hiring, incentives, content moats, unscalable offers, risk, decision-making, relationships, grief, happiness, and the discipline of continuing to take the next visible step when the whole path is uncertain. The central message is that [[Focus]], [[Patience]], real-world proof, and a track record with customers remain powerful advantages even as intelligence becomes cheaper and content becomes abundant.

## Key Insights

*   **Do not confuse AI adoption with business progress**: [[Artificial Intelligence]] is useful when it improves the normal functions of a business, makes delivery faster, raises quality, or lowers costs in a meaningful way. It is not automatically useful because it is novel. The diagnostic question is brutally simple: **are you making more money now?** A founder can be “token maxing,” spending heavily on models, and still be no closer to solving the business’s actual constraint.
    *   Hormozi gives the example of a company paying roughly $11,000 per month for 11 [[Virtual Assistants]] doing data-cleaning work. It spent approximately $350,000 trying to replace them with an AI system, equivalent to more than three years of the existing cost, even though the work was not limiting growth. The company’s real problem was insufficient [[Demand]], not the process it automated.
    *   Automate inside the business before deciding to build an “AI business.” Do not assume customers care about the underlying technology. Customers care about the [[Outcome]], the solved problem, and the reduction of their pain.
    *   AI can accelerate a bad strategy. Faster execution is not progress when the direction, offer, customer, or bottleneck is wrong.

*   **The durable value of intelligence is judgment under stakes**: AI may produce recommendations, but people and organizations still need someone to own the decision, assume the [[Risk]], and receive the upside. An AI system does not currently pay taxes, own the [[LLC]], risk its money, or bear the consequences of a bad judgment. Value therefore remains in responsibility, taste, selection, accountability, and the willingness to make a consequential commitment.
    *   This explains why a real [[MrBeast]] video cannot simply be replaced by a synthetic copy. If the money, challenge, and consequences are not real, the audience loses the reason to care.
    *   [[Chess]] engines beat human players, but people still care about human competition. [[Formula One]] would lose much of its appeal if [[Lewis Hamilton]] were removed from the car and replaced with a machine. Humans want [[Stakes]], not merely optimal outputs.
    *   In a [[SaaS]] company, AI may generate code, analysis, or recommendations, but the founder still has to decide what product to ship, which customer to serve, what risk to take, and what promise the company is prepared to stand behind.

*   **Outsourcing difficult thinking makes the founder weaker**: Hormozi treats his mind as his most valuable current asset and does not want to dull it by delegating the hardest thinking to [[ChatGPT]], [[Claude]], [[Gemini]], or any other model. He tests models by asking the same obvious decision of several systems and finds that they can disagree or confidently agree with almost anything.
    *   Use AI as a research assistant, critic, simulator, implementation engine, and speed multiplier. Do not automatically use it as the final source of judgment.
    *   Preserve the work that develops your own [[Critical Thinking]], [[Clarity of Thought]], problem decomposition, taste, and decision-making. The point is not to reject AI; it is to avoid letting convenience eliminate the cognitive practice that makes you valuable.
    *   A founder should be able to explain why a decision is right before asking a model to help execute it. If the model disappears, the founder should still understand the problem, assumptions, trade-offs, and desired result.

*   **The foundation must change with the time horizon**: Hormozi’s block-tower exercise illustrates [[Long-Term Thinking]]. A tower built in five seconds requires a different foundation from one built over five minutes, five days, or five years. The height and durability you intend to reach determine the foundations, materials, processes, and compromises you must make at the beginning.
    *   The fastest route to a $10 million business is not necessarily the fastest route to a $100 million business. A one-person agency can reach $1 million quickly, but the systems, retention, talent, distribution, and infrastructure required for $100 million are different.
    *   Founders often want the speed of a one-story building and later demand the height of a hundred-story building. They then discover that the original foundation cannot support the new ambition and may need to take two steps back to rebuild it.
    *   [[Focus]] and [[Patience]] are enduring competitive advantages precisely because they are anti-human and anti-[[Social Media]]. A founder who spends a season on boring hiring, retention, infrastructure, and process improvement may look inactive while building a moat competitors cannot quickly copy.
    *   [[Amazon]] is a classic example. [[Jeff Bezos]] did not remain satisfied with an online bookstore; he built logistics, warehouses, delivery capability, and other infrastructure that became a durable competitive advantage.

*   **Retention is the hidden engine of scale**: A business that retains customers can grow by adding customers. A business that loses customers must continually replace its entire base just to stand still. This is the difference between compounding growth and a leaky bucket.
    *   Imagine two companies that each acquire 100 customers per year at $10,000 of annual revenue per customer. Company A loses all of its customers each year and must sell 100, then 200, then 300 new customers to report $1 million, $2 million, and $3 million. Company B keeps its customers and adds 100 new ones each year, reaching the same reported revenue with far less acquisition pressure.
    *   Company A’s apparent growth is fragile. Its [[Customer Acquisition Cost]] rises, its margins compress, and the business can crater when sales slow. Company B has [[Recurring Revenue]], a growing installed base, better economics, and a foundation that can be paired with stronger distribution later.
    *   Before scaling traffic, sales, or AI automation, prove that customers continue to want the product. [[Churn]], [[Renewal]], repeat purchase, usage, and expansion reveal whether the business is genuinely valuable.
    *   Hormozi’s advice to the $1 million founder trying to reach $10 million is often to stay longer in the [[Product-Market Fit]] phase and make sure customers keep spending, rather than trying to force another year of new sales through a leaky product.

*   **“I’m running out of time” is often a margin problem**: When an owner says they are overwhelmed, the symptom is lack of time but the root cause may be an offer with too much delivery for too little money. Thin [[Gross Margin]] prevents the founder from buying help, creating capacity, documenting processes, and investing in the next layer of the company.
    *   Diagnose the relationship between the [[Offer]], [[Price]], delivery terms, and labor required. If the offer is underpriced, more demand can make the business less healthy.
    *   A strong marketing and sales motion can be dangerous when it pours customers into a weak operation. Better acquisition does not repair bad economics or poor delivery; it accelerates exposure of the defect.
    *   Price based on the value and urgency of the customer’s desired outcome, not on the founder’s private estimate of what the work feels like to them.

*   **Do not price from your own wallet**: New entrepreneurs frequently undercharge because the work is easy for them or because they would not personally pay the asking price. This is “selling out of your own wallet.” The relevant question is what the customer is willing to pay for a valuable outcome they do not want, cannot do, or cannot do reliably.
    *   A mechanic may think fixing a car is simple because they have done it many times. The customer is not paying for the number of screws turned; they are paying to avoid the problem, regain mobility, and save time.
    *   Underpricing creates a vicious cycle: low margin forces the founder to do more work, the founder accepts every customer, capacity disappears, and there is no time or cash to hire and train.
    *   Higher prices can improve customer quality, margins, focus, perceived value, and the founder’s ability to deliver a superior experience. Price is not only a monetization variable; it is also a signal about who the offer is for and how seriously the business takes the result.

*   **Use the Van Westendorp pricing framework**: For a new offer, ask potential customers four questions: at what price would it be so expensive that they would not consider it; at what price would it be so cheap that it could not be valuable; at what price would it feel expensive but still worth considering; and at what price would it feel like a bargain.
    *   Plot the responses to identify the [[Point of Marginal Cheapness]], the point of marginal expensiveness, and the range where different purchase objectives make sense.
    *   Slice the responses by customer type, income, industry, or use case. The optimal price for a budget buyer may be very different from the optimal price for a wealthy enterprise customer.
    *   Use AI to process the survey data if helpful, but do not let the tool substitute for speaking to customers, interpreting context, or testing whether people actually buy.

*   **Replace the founder with a system, not a mythical unicorn**: Founders often search for one employee who will think exactly like them, perform every function, and care with the same intensity. That person rarely exists. Hormozi’s “unicorn” metaphor breaks the fantasy into a rhino with the horn, a horse with the horse-like qualities, and fireflies for the sparkle.
    *   Hire for distinct capabilities and combine people, processes, training, and management. Do not insist that one person possess every trait you accumulated over years.
    *   The founder’s job is to identify what the role must accomplish, define the standard, teach the shortest route to competence, and build feedback loops.
    *   “Nobody can do it as well as me” can conceal [[Ego]], insecurity, fear of responsibility, and the desire to feel needed. The business exists to serve customers; it is not responsible for satisfying the founder’s emotional need to be indispensable.
    *   An early hiring mistake Hormozi identifies is failing to hold a high enough standard. The person highest in a department should have the highest standard. If a department leader has a higher standard than the founder, that person should probably run the department.

*   **Start by making the amorphous concrete**: “Start a business” is too vague to motivate action. Hormozi reduces it to four observable steps: create an [[LLC]] or other entity, open a bank account, obtain a way to process payments, and ask a stranger to pay for something.
    *   The first dollar changes identity. A person moves from [[Wantrepreneur]] to entrepreneur when a stranger exchanges money for a result they provide.
    *   The first customer is more valuable than another month of consuming business content because it creates real data about selling, delivery, expectations, objections, and value.
    *   Break every intimidating project into the next visible action. Confusion and fear persist when the task remains abstract; action creates a surface for feedback.

*   **Fear lives in the vague; specificity creates options**: “I will fail” is not a useful forecast. Ask what failure would actually mean. Someone says no to an offer; one hundred people say no; the offer changes; expenses are reduced; a job is taken temporarily; a friend’s couch or a lower-cost living arrangement extends [[Runway]]. Specificity turns catastrophe into a list of manageable scenarios.
    *   Hormozi recommends planning [[Plan B]] in painful detail. The goal is not to eliminate risk, but to know what happens if the first attempt does not work.
    *   People often protect a short-term lifestyle and the appearance of success at the expense of a long-term goal. Lowering living expenses can buy the time required to learn.
    *   Entrepreneurship does not require knowing the whole mountain. The fog clears only as you walk; the responsibility is to take the steps visible in front of you.

*   **Choose your future over the audience’s version of you**: At the beginning of entrepreneurship, the central conflict is often not lack of information but fear of judgment. Someone’s version of the founder has to die: either the desired future self or the self shaped by family, friends, colleagues, and social expectations.
    *   Hormozi delayed leaving his job for roughly six months, considered a [[Booth MBA]], calculated its opportunity cost, read extensively, and repeatedly discussed starting a company before acting. He eventually drove halfway across the country before telling his father he had quit because he feared being talked out of it.
    *   He realized that a specific person in his circle might not view his $46 million [[Gym Launch]] exit as legitimate. Naming whose approval controlled the decision made the emotional trap visible.
    *   There is no certainty available before the first step. Staying on the current path can carry a 100% probability of not reaching the desired future, while moving creates a possibility, even if it also creates risk.

*   **Be willing to be visibly bad at the beginning**: Early content, early sales calls, early products, and early public work will often be cringe. The founder who protects a polished identity cannot collect the repetitions required to improve.
    *   “Embrace cringiness” is an operating requirement, not a motivational slogan. You will know you are bad; other people may know too; the question is whether you care more about the future you are building than preserving your current status.
    *   A small, embarrassing experiment is superior to a sophisticated plan that never meets a customer. [[Iteration]] converts embarrassment into capability.

*   **Freedom requires commitment**: People often fetishize independence, optionality, and keeping every door open. Yet the best parts of life and business require choosing one door and leaving the others behind. You cannot have every possible life.
    *   Unmade decisions can last indefinitely. A decision inside a feedback loop produces information; an unmade decision produces rumination.
    *   Define what “freedom,” “financial independence,” or “success” means in observable daily terms. What changes about your schedule, relationships, work, responsibilities, and health?
    *   The goal is not maximum options; it is a deliberate trade in favor of the option you value most.

*   **Reality is the moat in an AI-saturated content market**: As models make text, images, video, and derivative content cheap, generic tips and tricks become less valuable. The strongest moat is a combination of [[Reality]], reputation, demonstrated results, scarce access, and real stakes.
    *   [[Elon Musk]] is influential not merely because he can express business ideas but because he has built [[Tesla]] and [[SpaceX]]. [[Jeff Bezos]] is influential because of [[Amazon]]. [[Warren Buffett]] is influential because of [[Berkshire Hathaway]] and a long investment record. A person can repeat their words, but cannot instantly reproduce their evidence.
    *   In education, the more costly the consequences of being wrong, the more audiences rely on the credibility of the source. A beauty tip may carry modest risk; personal-finance advice carries more; business advice can threaten a company. Credibility must rise with stakes.
    *   Hormozi’s content advantage comes from doing what only he can do: bringing hundreds of real business owners together, hearing their live problems, and demonstrating judgment in an interactive setting. [[IRL]] experiences, live conversations, and real entrepreneurs with something to lose are difficult to synthesize.
    *   “Hard and scarce” are useful content filters. Hard production, hard access, hard-won expertise, and scarce experiences create more defensibility than commoditized commentary.

*   **If you have no outcome proof, document effort proof**: A beginner cannot honestly claim a decade of results. They can still document the work that produces future proof.
    *   Record the sales calls, experiments, customer interviews, product iterations, failed tests, and improvements. Show the evidence of effort and learning rather than pretending to be an authority.
    *   A useful format compresses time: summarize a day of calls, show the best objection you overcame, explain the result of a test, or document how a real customer problem changed the product.
    *   Trust grows when the audience can see a real process with real constraints, not a manufactured posture of certainty.

*   **Use the value equation to improve offers and content**: The value of an offer rises with the desired [[Outcome]] and the customer’s [[Perceived Likelihood of Achievement]]. It falls with [[Time Delay]], [[Effort]], and [[Sacrifice]]. Effort is what the customer must begin doing; sacrifice is what they must stop doing.
    *   Increase the value of a SaaS offer by improving the customer’s result, raising confidence through proof and onboarding, shortening time to first value, reducing setup work, and removing behavior changes that create friction.
    *   A $19 PDF may contain useful information, but a $3,000 coach can carry a higher perceived likelihood of achievement because the customer receives personal implementation support and accountability.
    *   For content, the viewer evaluates the expected outcome, the creator’s credibility, the time required, and the work required to apply the lesson. A shorter piece is not automatically better; it must make the value of consuming it clear.
    *   Speed is underused. A business that reliably produces the same outcome in half the time can often charge a premium. Customers discount distant outcomes, even guaranteed ones, because the present cost feels more real than a future benefit.

*   **Bet on what will not change**: [[Jeff Bezos]]’s useful strategic question is not only “what will change?” but “what will remain true in ten years?” Customers will continue to prefer lower prices, more selection, faster delivery, greater convenience, and higher confidence when those benefits are available.
    *   Build around enduring human preferences rather than a temporary technology label. AI can change how a service is delivered, but customers still want a valuable result, less risk, less waiting, and less effort.
    *   In a SaaS roadmap, separate technology-dependent advantages from stable customer outcomes. The former may be copied; the latter can guide durable positioning.

*   **Patience and consistency are invisible inputs**: People see the winning post, exit, launch, or finished company, not the years of repetition that made it possible. [[Compounding]] looks flat before it looks vertical.
    *   Hormozi describes working at 4 a.m. even when he did not need to, because consistency cannot be understood by watching only the output.
    *   Focus is also invisible: observers see the choices made, not the opportunities declined. Patience is invisible because the audience arrives at the beginning and the end, not for the middle of the marathon.
    *   More founders abandon the correct path than choose the wrong one. A slow result is not automatically evidence of a bad strategy.

*   **Know when to push and when to pivot**: Pivot when a fundamental assumption underlying the business has been disproved by customer and market feedback. Push when the original thesis remains valid but progress is slower, harder, or less glamorous than expected.
    *   Do not confuse a local failure with a global failure. A failed headline, episode, ad, sales call, or product iteration is information; it becomes a global failure only when you decide you are no longer in the game.
    *   Quit the specific [[Doggy Skateboard]] if repeated tests show that customers do not want it. Do not quit business, learning, or the process of finding a better problem.
    *   A founder goes earlier than the world’s available evidence. The practical test is whether even a small group of people is receiving scarce value. Hormozi noticed that friends asked when the next [[Diary Of A CEO]] episode would arrive; that early pull mattered more than broad public recognition.

*   **Start unscalable, then discover what can scale**: “That is not scalable” is a poor objection when the founder is broke and has not yet validated the value. Begin with the most absurdly valuable and viable version of the offer, even if it requires personal labor.
    *   Add one or two zeros to the desired price and ask what would need to be included to make it worth paying. A premium service may involve home visits, implementation, accountability, or unusually high-touch support.
    *   Unscalable work produces richer customer data, stronger relationships, better understanding of the problem, higher cash flow, and access to customers with more resources and sophisticated worldviews.
    *   After delivery, identify which elements are repeatable and can become software, training, process, or media. The scalable version should be extracted from observed reality, not imagined in a vacuum.
    *   This resembles [[Tesla]]’s progression from the expensive [[Roadster]] to the [[Model S]] and broader-market vehicles: begin with a premium product, use it to learn and generate cash, then work down toward a larger market.

*   **Behavior is shaped by conditions and incentives**: Hormozi’s [[Behaviorism]] frame asks what conditions make the desired behavior the easiest and most attractive option. Instead of saying that a person “cannot be made to drink,” arrange the environment so drinking the medicine is more likely: improve the taste, make it cold, place enjoyable food nearby, and connect it to a desired activity.
    *   The same logic applies to marketing, onboarding, employee behavior, and product adoption. Do not rely on motivation when the environment, instructions, timing, reward, and friction can be redesigned.
    *   People generally behave within their [[Incentive Structure]]. If employees are told to adopt AI but are rewarded only for completing their current jobs, their rational action is to protect those jobs. Change behavior by changing the incentives and reducing the cost of the new behavior.
    *   The [[Innovator’s Dilemma]] captures a related organizational problem: existing incentives can make a company rationally ignore disruptive opportunities even when leaders intellectually understand them.

*   **Persuasion is a map of gains and losses**: Hormozi’s simple persuasion grid considers what happens if a prospect does the desired action or does not do it. The four quadrants are more good, less good, more bad, and less bad.
    *   “Do this and you gain more good” and “do this and you avoid more bad” are the direct reasons to act. “Do not do this and you lose good” and “do not do this and experience more bad” describe the cost of inaction.
    *   Ethical persuasion should clarify real consequences, not manufacture fear. In a fitness offer, the positive case may include health and time with children; the negative case may include continued pain or fewer future experiences. The claims must be true and relevant to the buyer.
    *   The founder’s job is not to create demand from nothing. [[Gary Halbert]]’s framing is to channel existing demand toward the business by making the offer the most convenient, credible, and attractive option.

*   **Choose the customer before trying to scale**: At the beginning, a service business may accept money from almost anyone because survival and learning matter. Between roughly $1 million and $3 million, the founder often has enough data to choose a segment deliberately.
    *   One customer type may be easier to serve, pay more, retain longer, and create better referrals. Specialization allows the back end to standardize delivery and the front end to communicate a precise promise.
    *   “We do podcast marketing for everyone” is weaker than “we help doctors generate patients through podcasts.” The specific customer recognizes themselves and the business can build a repeatable solution.
    *   One offer for ten similar customers is more scalable than ten customized offers for ten unrelated customers. First survive broadly; then narrow intelligently.

*   **Look for durable, unfashionable markets**: Hormozi highlights opportunities connected to stable human needs and neglected work: AI-enabled services for small businesses, Reddit reputation management, clipping and distribution services, longevity and medical spas, peptides, wealth-related services, insurance, risk management, and businesses that customers need but do not find socially glamorous.
    *   “Boring” or stigmatized categories can carry less competition and a discount in perceived status, even when the underlying economics are strong. Waste management is not a fashionable dinner-party identity, but essential services can create significant wealth.
    *   Look where money is concentrated, not only where your immediate peers are. A founder surrounded by people without money may falsely conclude that no one can afford a premium offer. Moving upmarket requires credibility, proof, and better delivery, but can reduce client-management difficulty when the customer has a larger budget and the purchase is not their personal financial survival.
    *   Wealth-related services, insurance, appearance, longevity, and essential operations are examples of categories supported by needs that are unlikely to vanish because of AI.

*   **Move upmarket through proof and price**: A service price often reflects the maturity of the business and its owner. Delivering reliably creates demand; when demand exceeds capacity, price can rise. Higher prices then create margin to improve delivery, attract stronger talent, and serve better customers.
    *   Do not merely announce a higher price without increasing capability. The buyer must receive a result, certainty, speed, access, or experience that makes the price rational.
    *   Track record is the bridge to enterprise customers. Start with the accessible customer if necessary, but systematically collect proof, case studies, referrals, and outcomes that make the next market believe you can serve it.
    *   The goal is not to serve wealthy customers for status. It is to serve a segment whose economics let you create enough value, margin, and capacity to build a durable company.

*   **Treat hiring as a pipeline, just like sales**: Hormozi models the supply side of a company using the same stages as customer acquisition: application generation, application nurture, interviews, onboarding, retention, and employee ascension.
    *   If the company has a demand problem, improve the customer pipeline. If it has a supply problem, apply the same discipline to the talent pipeline: compelling job copy, fast follow-up, clear proof of the opportunity, structured interviews, role-play, onboarding, 30/60/90-day expectations, and career progression.
    *   Audit the messages candidates receive. A business may be horrified by its own job posting or the poor follow-up candidates experience, just as it would be horrified by sloppy outbound sales.
    *   Ask a strong employee what it would take for them to work at the company forever. You do not have to accept every answer, but the question reveals the conditions that support retention.
    *   Referral incentives should be proportional to the value of the hire. One business increased an agent referral payment from $500 to $25,000 after comparing it with roughly $250,000 of annual gross profit per productive agent; the company grew from about $10 million to $400 million.

*   **People are the highest-variance decisions**: Hormozi calls marrying [[Leila Hormozi]] his single best financial decision, not as a universal claim that relationships should be optimized only for money, but because her belief, operational strength, loyalty, and support increased the probability that he could keep going and operate a complex business.
    *   A partner can increase or decrease the likelihood of reaching a goal. That does not mean every person should leave a relationship that is not maximally supportive of their career. People may support a partner’s well-being, spirituality, family life, or identity in ways that are not directly career-oriented.
    *   Make the trade explicit. No person provides every function: coach, therapist, cheerleader, operator, best friend, and business partner. Demanding a perfect all-in-one partner is another unicorn search.
    *   Choose what you want before judging whether a relationship is “right.” The best decision for financial outcomes may not be the best decision for subjective well-being, and the most successful-looking life can still be lonely.

*   **Control inputs rather than demanding a specific output**: While discussing his future child, Hormozi wrestles with the limits of control. A parent can provide conditions, education, resources, skills, standards, and love, but cannot fully dictate the child’s personality, preferences, or final life.
    *   The plant metaphor is useful: you cannot force a seed to become a different species, but you can provide water, sunlight, soil, pruning, and protection. In business, define the destination while concentrating daily attention on controllable inputs.
    *   [[Jeff Bezos]]’s advice to stay out of the business of the output applies to parenting and company building. Set direction, then focus on the quality and consistency of the inputs.
    *   For a SaaS founder, inputs include customer conversations, product iterations, reliable releases, support quality, pricing tests, hiring standards, and distribution experiments. Do not use anxiety about the output as an excuse to neglect the next input.

*   **Death clarifies priorities but does not prescribe one right way to grieve**: Hormozi describes writing notes to himself about death, including the observation that a funeral quickly becomes a small event in other people’s lives. The point is not nihilism; it is to reduce the weight of other people’s judgment and remember that time is finite.
    *   He rejects the assumption that the amount someone suffers proves how much they loved a person. After his mother’s death, he tried to preserve the activities that helped him function: working, helping businesses, writing, and exercising. Continuing was his way of honoring the path she knew he had chosen, not evidence that he did not mourn.
    *   His framework for difficult seasons includes [[Fortitude]]—how much adversity can be tolerated before behavior changes; [[Tolerance]]—how far behavior falls; [[Resilience]]—how quickly one returns to baseline; and [[Adaptability]]—whether the person becomes better, unchanged, or permanently worse after the event.
    *   The practical lesson is to identify controllables during a crisis and take the next two visible steps. A bad season may last longer than a day, but progress can still be measured by chipping away at what can be done now.

*   **Emotional discomfort is not automatically a reason to change course**: Feelings are variable like weather: there are sunny days, rainy days, and rainy seasons. Hormozi uses the idea of a “bottom 10% day” to prevent temporary emotional states from triggering destructive decisions.
    *   If life is consistently wrong, change it. If today is bad, delay irreversible choices until the emotional weather changes. A bad day is not necessarily evidence that the marriage, company, product, or life direction is wrong.
    *   In business, founders can destroy a young company by changing the strategy, positioning, offer, and product after one discouraging day. Measure patterns and evidence, not one emotional datapoint.
    *   A good day may simply be one on which you feel bad but still do useful work. That is not suppression; it is refusing to let a temporary state own a permanent decision.

*   **Do not sell business as a guaranteed path to happiness**: Hormozi is comfortable promising quantitative business outcomes—more qualified people finding the offer, better monetization, faster cash collection, stronger offers—but refuses to promise that wealth or growth will produce happiness.
    *   [[Subjective Well-Being]] is influenced by temperament, genetics, relationships, meaning, health, and adaptation. Financial success can solve financial problems without supplying a universal answer to purpose.
    *   He describes himself as intensely interested in his life and proud of the work he has done, while acknowledging that he does not always know what happiness means or feel good about being himself. The honest answer is more useful than a motivational promise.
    *   Build a business because you value the work, autonomy, craft, service, impact, or chosen life—not because a revenue milestone guarantees a permanent emotional state.

*   **If superintelligent AI arrives soon, build proof and trust now**: Asked what he would do if AI became better, faster, and cheaper than humans at nearly everything, Hormozi would build as much real-world proof, customer track record, brand, trust, and distribution as possible.
    *   Reputation and trust are likely to remain valuable because customers still need to decide whom to believe and whom to hold accountable. Distribution may change in cost and form, but marketplace dynamics will remain.
    *   For a company, build a brand that is attached to real outcomes, not merely to the current technology. For a person, think deeply about what it means to be human and what makes life worth living if intelligence becomes abundant.
    *   The practical anti-AI moat is not hiding from technology; it is accumulating evidence, relationships, judgment, and customer trust faster than competitors.

## Coach’s Corner

**On the AI temptation and your early-stage SaaS:**

As an early-stage [[SaaS Founder]], your biggest danger is probably not that you are too slow to adopt AI. It is that you are using AI to avoid the uncomfortable work that would reveal whether your product deserves to exist. An AI agent can generate a landing page, rewrite your onboarding emails, create a prototype, summarize interviews, and produce a hundred feature ideas. None of that answers the crucial questions: who has the problem, how painful is it, what do they do today, what will they pay, and will they continue paying after the novelty wears off?

Use AI aggressively around the edges and cautiously at the center. Let it help you analyze interview transcripts, identify repeated objections, generate test variants, write code after you have specified the behavior, create support drafts, and find inconsistencies in your reasoning. But keep the hardest work for yourself: selecting the customer, understanding the problem, deciding what not to build, choosing the promise, and taking responsibility for the result. If AI vanished tomorrow, you should still understand your customer and your product better than anyone else.

Before automating a process, ask three questions:

1. Is this process actually constraining growth?
2. What measurable outcome will improve if I automate it?
3. Will the saved time or money be reinvested into demand, retention, or delivery?

If you cannot answer those questions, you may be using technology as [[Productive Procrastination]]. Talk to customers instead.

**On building a durable SaaS foundation:**

Decide what building you are constructing. If you want a lifestyle product with a small, profitable customer base, you can optimize for simplicity and personal control. If you want a venture-scale or $100 million company, you need different foundations: reliable infrastructure, a clear ICP, repeatable acquisition, retention, documentation, security, support, hiring standards, and a product architecture that can survive more customers than the founder can personally touch.

Do not pretend those are the same business. The first version of a product can be narrow and manual, but you should know which parts are temporary scaffolding and which decisions are foundational. A manual onboarding process is fine while you learn. A pattern of acquiring customers who churn immediately is not fine; it is evidence that your foundation is wrong.

Your durable SaaS moat will probably not be a feature that a frontier model can reproduce in a weekend. It will be a combination of customer trust, proprietary workflow data, deep integration into a customer’s operations, switching costs earned through real value, distribution, brand, and a track record of solving a painful problem. Build those assets deliberately.

**On retention before acquisition:**

Track retention before celebrating acquisition. A new signup is a hypothesis. Continued use, renewal, expansion, and referral are evidence. If the customer disappears after the first month, more traffic only increases the number of people you disappoint.

Create a simple retention dashboard. At minimum, know:

*   Activation: what observable event means the customer experienced first value?
*   Time to value: how long does it take to reach that event?
*   Retention: what percentage is still active after 7, 30, 90, and 180 days?
*   Churn reasons: what did departing customers say, and what did their behavior show?
*   Expansion: which customers buy more seats, usage, features, or services?
*   Referral: who would recommend you without being prompted?

When retention is weak, do not immediately blame marketing. The offer may be wrong, the customer may be wrong, the onboarding may be confusing, the product may not integrate with a real workflow, or the promised outcome may be too distant. Find the leak before you pour in more water.

**On pricing and selling to customers who can pay:**

Stop using your personal finances as the market’s price ceiling. If your software saves a business $100,000, prevents a costly error, generates revenue, or removes a full-time manual process, a $99 monthly price may be a sign that you do not understand the value—not proof that you are customer-friendly.

Interview customers about the economic value of the problem. Ask what the problem costs them in money, time, missed opportunities, stress, and risk. Then test a price that reflects a meaningful fraction of the value created. Use the [[Van Westendorp Pricing Analysis]] for directional evidence, but validate it with actual purchases and renewals.

Do not raise price as theater. Raise it alongside stronger proof, faster onboarding, better support, clearer guarantees, higher reliability, or a more specific customer promise. A narrow product for a narrow ICP can often charge more than a generic tool that claims to serve everyone.

**On finding your ICP:**

At the beginning, take enough customers to learn. You may need to serve several types of users because you have not yet earned the right to be selective. But treat each customer as research. Record who closes fastest, activates fastest, retains longest, creates the fewest support problems, gets the largest result, and refers others.

Then choose. “We help businesses manage data” is not a position. “We help multi-location dental groups reconcile insurance payments without adding back-office staff” is closer to a position. The second statement tells you who to interview, what workflow to understand, what proof to collect, and where to distribute the message.

The goal is not to trap yourself forever. It is to create enough focus that your product and marketing can become excellent for someone. You can expand after you own a beachhead.

**On unscalable customer development:**

You are likely too early to worry about scale. If ten customers pay you for a high-touch service wrapped around your software, that is not a failure of productization; it is an opportunity to learn. Personally onboard them. Watch the workflow. Sit in on their meetings. Fix the problem manually when necessary. Charge enough that the work is economically meaningful.

This does not mean becoming a permanent agency. It means using service as a laboratory. Every manual step is a question: does the customer value this, can it be standardized, can it be eliminated, can the software perform it, and what new insight did I gain by doing it myself?

The premium customer can teach you more than a large number of low-intent users. They expose the real stakes, demand reliability, and force you to improve the offer. Build the unscalable version that creates undeniable value, then extract the repeatable system.

**On your personal operating system:**

Fear is not a sign that you are uniquely unqualified. It is the default response to uncertainty. Make it specific. What exactly happens if the product fails? How much runway remains? What expenses can you cut? What skill can you sell? Who can you call? What experiment can you run before making a larger commitment?

Write the answers. A written [[Plan B]] is not a vote against your ambition; it is a way to stop your nervous system from inventing an apocalypse. Then take the next visible step: one customer conversation, one paid pilot, one onboarding improvement, one distribution test.

Also accept that your first public attempts will be embarrassing. Your first demo, cold email, sales call, product video, and landing page will not represent your final capability. You are not protecting a finished identity. You are building one. The price of looking inexperienced is much smaller than the price of remaining inexperienced.

**On decision-making and the cost of optionality:**

You cannot build every product, target every segment, learn every skill, and preserve every lifestyle simultaneously. A large part of founder stress is the accumulation of unmade decisions. Pick a direction with explicit trade-offs and create a feedback loop.

Write: “I want to have ___ happen by ___ because ___, and I am willing to give up ___ to pursue it.” Include the daily reality, not just the impressive headline. If the goal is a large company, say what that means for your schedule, relationships, health, and responsibilities. If the goal is freedom, define the freedoms you actually want.

Then review the choice at a predetermined interval rather than renegotiating your identity every bad day. Emotional discomfort is data, but it is not automatically a strategic conclusion. Look for repeated evidence that a core assumption is false. If the thesis is still intact and the discomfort is merely the cost of learning, keep moving.

**On incentives inside your SaaS team:**

If you want your team to adopt AI, do not send a motivational message and hope. Change the environment. Give them a defined problem, a safe place to experiment, training, examples, time, and a reward for achieving a useful result. If you measure only speed, quality may collapse. If you measure only activity, outcomes may disappear. Pair metrics.

For customer success, pair response time with resolution quality and retention. For sales, pair new revenue with customer fit and renewal. For engineering, pair shipping speed with reliability and customer impact. For recruiting, pair hiring volume with quality and 90-day retention.

Ask of every behavior: what does the person gain, what does it cost them, and what competing behavior does the current system reward? Most “people problems” become clearer when translated into incentives and friction.

**On hiring before you feel ready:**

If the business has real margin and you are still doing every task, the bottleneck may be fear disguised as standards. You do not need a clone of yourself. Define the smallest role that removes a meaningful constraint, write the standard, provide examples, and train the person.

Hire the component, not the unicorn. One person may be excellent at customer research; another may be strong at implementation; a third may manage operations. Your job is to build the system that makes those capabilities work together.

Do not lower the standard to fill the seat. A mediocre hire creates management debt, damages customer trust, and trains the rest of the team to tolerate weak work. It is often cheaper to spend more on exceptional talent than to keep trying to solve a high-value problem with low-cost labor.

**On content and brand:**

Do not compete with infinite AI content by producing more generic content. Build a reality moat. Share customer outcomes, product decisions, live experiments, failures, implementation details, and hard-won lessons. If you are early, your proof is effort: show the calls, builds, tests, and improvements.

Ask what only you can do. Perhaps you can access a niche community, observe a workflow competitors cannot see, gather proprietary data, demonstrate a live product, or tell the truth about a problem because you are experiencing it yourself. That specificity is more defensible than another list of ten productivity tips.

Your content should channel existing demand to your product. It should make the right customer think, “This person understands my problem and has evidence that they can help.” Trust compounds in the same way retention compounds.

**On relationships and the life you are actually building:**

Do not borrow a stranger’s definition of success. A business can become more valuable while your life becomes less aligned. Before optimizing for revenue, decide which relationships, health standards, family commitments, creative work, and forms of freedom are non-negotiable.

The Ferrari-and-Michelin-restaurant-alone versus battered-Camry-and-Denny’s-with-five-friends comparison is a useful warning: objective success and subjective enjoyment are not the same variable. Do not discover at the end of a decade that you optimized a scoreboard you never truly wanted.

At the same time, do not use uncertainty about ultimate happiness as an excuse to avoid meaningful work. Choose a direction, make the trade consciously, and keep checking whether the life being produced resembles the life you intended.

**On surviving the bad season:**

When lawsuits, health problems, grief, or other events create an overwhelming open loop, reduce the horizon. What can you do today? What can you do this week? Progress may be nothing more glamorous than answering one email, attending one appointment, exercising, eating, sleeping, helping one customer, or refusing to abandon every stabilizing routine.

Do not judge yourself by another person’s mourning pattern or recovery timeline. Preserve the behaviors that make you more capable of meeting the next day. Fortitude, tolerance, resilience, and adaptability are not abstract virtues; they are observable patterns you can strengthen by returning to controllables.

A bottom-10% day is allowed to be a bottom-10% day. It does not have to become a referendum on your company, your marriage, or your future.

**On the long-term AI scenario:**

If intelligence becomes cheap, your advantage will be the evidence that you used it responsibly to create outcomes. Build customer trust now. Collect case studies. Create a reputation for keeping promises. Become known for a specific result in a specific market. Establish distribution that is not dependent on a single algorithm.

The future does not reward the founder who guessed every technology trend correctly. It rewards the founder who built something customers trust, renew, recommend, and rely on. Build the brand and the proof while the market is still noisy.

## Action Items

*   [ ] **Audit your AI spend and experiments**: List every AI tool, workflow, agent, and project. For each, record the cost, hours saved, revenue influenced, quality change, and whether it addresses the current constraint. Stop or pause anything that is merely fashionable.
*   [ ] **Interview five paying customers about retention**: Ask why they bought, what first value looked like, what nearly stopped adoption, what they would miss if the product disappeared, and what would make them renew or expand.
*   [ ] **Define your durable foundation**: Write whether you are building a profitable lifestyle SaaS, a focused niche company, or a large venture-scale business. List the foundation decisions that differ across those paths.
*   [ ] **Choose one ICP for the next experiment**: Compare your customers by activation, retention, willingness to pay, support burden, outcome, and referrals. Select the segment with the strongest evidence and rewrite your homepage around its specific problem.
*   [ ] **Run a Van Westendorp pricing survey**: Ask the four pricing questions to at least 15 relevant prospects. Segment responses by customer type, then test a price based on value and margin rather than your personal wallet.
*   [ ] **Build a high-touch paid pilot**: Offer a small number of customers an unusually valuable, manually supported implementation. Charge for it, observe the workflow, document every step, and identify the parts that should later become product or process.
*   [ ] **Calculate your retention engine**: Create a weekly dashboard for activation, time to first value, 30/90-day retention, churn reasons, expansion, and referrals. Do not increase acquisition spend until the weakest retention leak has an owner and an experiment.
*   [ ] **Map your current bottleneck**: Write the one constraint that most limits growth right now: demand, conversion, delivery, retention, margin, talent, or founder capacity. Commit the next 30 days to that constraint only.
*   [ ] **Replace a founder task with a component hire**: Choose one repeatable task that does not require your unique judgment. Define the outcome, standard, examples, training plan, and review cadence. Do not search for a clone of yourself.
*   [ ] **Audit team incentives**: For each important behavior, write what the person gains, what it costs, and what the current compensation or evaluation system actually rewards. Pair activity metrics with quality or outcome metrics.
*   [ ] **Improve the talent pipeline**: Review job copy, response speed, interview questions, role-play, onboarding, 30/60/90-day expectations, career progression, and employee referrals as if they were a customer funnel.
*   [ ] **Document proof of effort**: Publish one honest case study or build log showing the problem, experiment, failure, feedback, change, and result. If you lack outcome proof, do not fake authority; show the work.
*   [ ] **Write your Plan B in detail**: Specify what you would do if the next offer, launch, or product failed: runway, temporary income, living costs, people to call, and the next experiment. Convert vague fear into options.
*   [ ] **Name the approval voices**: Write the specific people whose judgment you fear, what you think they want for you, and what you want for yourself. Decide whose approval should govern the next business decision.
*   [ ] **Create a push-versus-pivot rule**: List the fundamental assumptions behind your product. Define the evidence that would disprove each one and the date on which you will review the evidence. Do not pivot merely because progress feels slow.
*   [ ] **Define the life behind the revenue goal**: Write what a successful ordinary Tuesday looks like at your target business size. Include work hours, relationships, health, family, location, responsibility, and freedom. Check whether your current strategy produces that life.
*   [ ] **Protect your cognitive edge**: Choose the three hardest thinking tasks you will not outsource to AI. Use models to challenge and accelerate your reasoning only after you have formed your own view.
*   [ ] **Choose one enduring customer preference**: Build a product or feature around something customers will likely still want in ten years: lower cost, faster delivery, greater convenience, more selection, reduced risk, or higher confidence.
