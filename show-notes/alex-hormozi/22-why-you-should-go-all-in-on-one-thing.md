# Why You Should Go All In on One Thing

## Summary
[[Alex Hormozi]] argues that most great fortunes are built by taking one business, channel, or capability to an extreme before using the resulting cash, talent, and distribution to expand into adjacent opportunities. What looks like a diversified [[Business Ecosystem]] from the outside usually began as a single focused vertical, not as several under-resourced businesses launched at once. The central operating principle is to eliminate alternatives, feed the winner, and stay with the strongest vehicle long enough for [[Compounding]] and [[Leverage]] to work. For an early-stage [[SaaS]] founder, this is a warning against confusing optionality with progress: a narrower customer, problem, product, and distribution motion can create more value than several half-built ideas.

## Key Insights

*   **Most visible ecosystems began as one focused channel**: The ultra-successful company often appears to have started as a broad [[Ecosystem]], but the visible ecosystem is the end state. The underlying pattern is usually one thing done exceptionally well, followed by expansion funded by accumulated [[Cash Flow]], [[Talent]], [[Customer Trust]], and [[Distribution]].
    *   Do not copy the final shape of [[Amazon]], [[Acquisition.com]], or another large company while ignoring the sequence that produced it.
    *   A portfolio of products is an advanced-stage outcome. An early founder usually needs a [[Beachhead Market]] and a single repeatable path to value first.
    *   The relevant question is not “How many businesses could I own?” but “Which one can I make undeniable?”

*   **Focus means eliminating alternatives**: Hormozi defines [[Commitment]] as the elimination of alternatives. Focus is not merely liking one project best while keeping every other option alive; it is repeatedly choosing the main thing over attractive opportunities that could also work.
    *   Saying no is not only a subtraction. It is a renewed yes to the mission, customer, and business already chosen.
    *   The most dangerous distractions are not obviously bad ideas. They are the appealing ideas the founder could probably execute, which makes them feel like responsible opportunities rather than [[Opportunity Cost]].
    *   A decision without a boundary is not commitment. Set a review date and a measurable thesis, then protect the chosen work until that review.

*   **The “leave no money on the table” instinct can create mutant businesses**: Hormozi describes a roofing contractor doing roofs, general contracting, and building flips. Each adjacent activity may produce revenue, but the combined operation can become too operationally complex to manage well.
    *   The desire to sell a customer “one more thing” often hides a [[Demand Generation]] problem. When acquiring more customers feels difficult, founders try to extract more revenue from each customer by adding unrelated offers.
    *   Multiple half-developed businesses compete for the same founder attention, working capital, operators, and recruiting capacity.
    *   The result is a set of “mutant businesses” that feed one another in the founder’s mental model but lack a coherent operating system in reality.
    *   Before expanding the offer, ask whether the core business can become much larger through better acquisition, conversion, delivery, [[Retention]], pricing, or capacity.

*   **Feed the winner and starve the losers**: When one activity is already producing cash and has demonstrated leverage, the default use of additional resources is usually to make that activity stronger and more profitable.
    *   Cash from the winner should not automatically be diverted to an unproven idea merely because the new idea is exciting.
    *   Expansion can be rational when the expected return from a new vertical is higher than the return from further investment in the existing one. The comparison must be explicit rather than emotional.
    *   “We do not know it is a loser yet” is not enough. Every initiative consumes scarce resources while it remains unproven; use [[Expected Value]], learning velocity, and opportunity cost to decide what survives.
    *   Pruning is part of growth. Removing a branch can improve the health of the whole [[Tree]] by returning nutrients, sunlight, and attention to the highest-return branch.

*   **Compare mature momentum with new-project optimism honestly**: A founder may imagine that a new business will produce half as much in year one and match the current business in year two. That comparison is misleading when the current business is already in year three.
    *   The fair comparison is year five of the current business, with concentrated attention and resources, against year one or two of the new business.
    *   Early progress in a new venture is seductive because it is measured against zero. Mature progress can look slower because it is measured against an established base.
    *   This is the business version of [[Time in the Market Beats Timing the Market]] and, in strength training, [[Time Under the Bar]]: sustained exposure to the same vehicle can produce returns that are invisible during the early plateau.
    *   Do not abandon an apparently inferior vehicle before testing what focused iteration, better talent, and accumulated learning could make it.

*   **Focus has a real trade-off, not magical upside**: Concentrating on one thing means other projects, passions, or revenue streams may grow more slowly. Hormozi does not claim that diversification is always wrong; he insists that the founder understand the trade being made.
    *   Diverting time, money, people, and attention to a second plant means the main plant receives less of those resources.
    *   A passion project can still be worthwhile for identity, joy, relationships, or creative development. It should not be disguised as a neutral business decision.
    *   Write down what the secondary project costs the primary one in speed, profit, stress, and [[Founder Capacity]]. Deliberate trade-offs are healthier than accidental fragmentation.

*   **The woman in the red dress is the distraction that looks like opportunity**: Using the training scene from [[The Matrix]], Hormozi frames distraction as something attractive enough to pull the founder’s eye off the ball. The distraction may be a new market, new product, partnership, or identity rather than a literal temptation.
    *   Successful founders are not immune. Hormozi describes owning several gyms, a dental agency, a chiropractic agency, and the Gym Launch business while giving only a fraction of his time to the activity producing most of the money.
    *   The problem was not a lack of ambition. It was an inability to recognize that maintaining several identities was preventing the strongest business from reaching its potential.
    *   Build a [[Distraction Inventory]]: list every project, commitment, and “future possibility” currently claiming mental bandwidth, then identify which ones are genuinely strategic.

*   **The hardest part of focus is often a few difficult conversations**: Hormozi’s move toward concentrating on Gym Launch required three conversations he had been dreading. The limiting factor was not necessarily strategy or capability; it was the anticipated emotional cost of closing or changing existing commitments.
    *   Founders often call this a strategy problem because “I need to decide what to do” feels safer than “I need to tell someone what I have decided.”
    *   Write down the conversations that would unlock the next level. Name the person, the decision, the consequence, and the exact words required.
    *   Anticipation is often worse than execution. One completed conversation can create [[Momentum]] and make the remaining decisions feel survivable.
    *   A clean ending is kinder than indefinite partial commitment when partners, employees, or customers are relying on clarity.

*   **Focus is a repeated choice, not a one-time declaration**: Hormozi cites [[David Senra]]’s summary of billionaire interviews: the common word is focus. Going all in means choosing the same direction over and over, especially when a new option is temporarily more exciting.
    *   The founder must renew the choice when a competitor launches, a feature request arrives, a new technology trends, or a short-term result disappoints.
    *   Focus does not mean refusing all information. It means filtering information through the current thesis instead of allowing every interesting idea to rewrite the thesis.
    *   Create a weekly [[Founder Review]] that asks: What is the one constraint? What is the one outcome that matters? What did I say no to? What evidence would justify changing course?

*   **Scale the core before building verticals**: Once the core business has enough resources, adjacent products and verticals can be rational. Before that point, expansion usually weakens the source of the resources required for expansion.
    *   The sequence is: identify a painful problem, win a narrow customer group, make delivery repeatable, improve economics, accumulate proof, hire around the core, and only then test adjacency.
    *   A vertical should be funded by strength rather than used as an escape from unresolved weakness in the core.
    *   Expansion readiness requires more than revenue. Look for stable [[Unit Economics]], repeatable acquisition, healthy retention, operational capacity, and a leader who can own the new area without pulling the founder back into every detail.

## Coach’s Corner

**On choosing one thing for your SaaS:**

Daniel, the practical version of “go all in” is not that you must promise to build the same product for the rest of your life. It is that you should stop making your attention compete with several futures at once. At this stage, your scarce resources are not only money. They are customer understanding, founder energy, product taste, credibility, and the number of high-quality conversations you can have in a week.

Choose one narrow combination of [[ICP]], painful workflow, promised outcome, and distribution channel for the next 90 days. For example: one type of business, one recurring problem, one measurable result, and one way of reaching decision-makers. Your product may eventually serve more segments, but your learning will be much faster if every interview, feature, onboarding flow, and sales message teaches you about the same economic problem.

Write the decision in this form:

> For the next 90 days, I will help [[Specific Customer]] achieve [[Specific Outcome]] through [[Core Product or Workflow]], acquiring them primarily through [[Primary Channel]]. I will not build or market [[Named Distractions]] unless the review criteria are met.

This is a [[Focus Contract]], not a lifelong identity sentence. The point is to create enough continuity for evidence to accumulate.

**On distinguishing a core from a distraction:**

A feature belongs in the core when it helps the chosen customer reach the promised outcome, improves activation or retention, or strengthens your ability to deliver the same result repeatedly. A feature is probably a distraction when it targets a different buyer, solves a different problem, requires a different sales motion, or exists mainly because it is technically interesting.

Before starting any new initiative, score it against four questions:

1. Does it serve the same ICP?
2. Does it improve the same primary outcome?
3. Does it use the same distribution and operational capabilities?
4. Is its expected return higher than improving the current bottleneck?

If the answer is no to two or more, place it in a [[Later List]]. Do not keep reopening it every few days. A written parking lot protects good ideas without letting them steal today’s work.

**On finding your actual bottleneck:**

The roofing example is especially relevant to SaaS because founders frequently add features when the real issue is that too few qualified people know the product exists. Or they add acquisition channels when the product is not retaining users. Or they polish infrastructure while avoiding sales conversations.

Classify the current constraint as one of [[Demand]], conversion, activation, retention, monetization, delivery, talent, or founder capacity. Choose one primary constraint for the next month. Then make your calendar reflect it:

*   If the constraint is demand, spend focused time on customer research, outbound, partnerships, or a single content channel.
*   If it is conversion, observe calls, rewrite the promise, tighten the demo, and test proof and objections.
*   If it is activation, watch new users complete the first-value path and remove friction one step at a time.
*   If it is retention, interview churned users and improve the recurring workflow before buying more traffic.
*   If it is monetization, test packaging, pricing, usage limits, and value communication with real prospects.

A founder who works on every category a little can feel busy while allowing the binding constraint to remain untouched.

**On resisting the “leave no money on the table” trap:**

Do not add a second product merely because an existing customer could theoretically buy it. First ask whether the core offer could earn more by serving the same customer more deeply. There are usually several cleaner forms of expansion: increase the result, reduce time to value, improve reliability, add usage or seats, create a premium tier, or become more embedded in the customer’s workflow.

For your SaaS, adjacent revenue is most attractive when it reuses the same customer relationship, data, product surface, support capability, and distribution channel. A separate product for a separate persona may look like cross-sell but is operationally a new company. Treat it as one and require it to justify the cost.

**On building one valuable vertical:**

A vertical is not just a label on a landing page. It is a decision to understand one group’s language, workflow, economics, objections, compliance concerns, and definition of success better than a generalist tool can. Narrowing gives you a chance to make the product feel unusually complete for someone.

Review your current and prospective customers by:

*   Speed to first value
*   Frequency of the problem
*   Willingness and ability to pay
*   Retention and expansion potential
*   Ease of reaching the buyer
*   Support and implementation burden
*   Strength of the outcome you can prove
*   Likelihood of referral

Choose the segment with the strongest evidence, not the one that sounds most prestigious. A small group that urgently needs your product is a better starting point than a huge market that treats it as optional.

**On the hard conversations you are avoiding:**

Your next level may be behind a conversation: telling an early user that a custom request is outside scope, asking for payment, ending a side project, changing a partnership, declining an attractive contract, or admitting that a feature is not working. These conversations can feel like existential threats because they challenge your identity as a helpful developer.

Make a table with four columns: conversation, feared outcome, likely actual outcome, and next action. Prepare a clear and respectful script. Schedule the conversation instead of carrying it as an open loop. You do not need to feel fearless; you need to discover that discomfort is survivable and clarity is valuable.

**On commitment without recklessness:**

Going all in does not mean burning your runway, ignoring evidence, or refusing a sensible safety net. It means committing your best available resources to a defined thesis while keeping a concrete [[Plan B]]. A plan B can lower panic because it turns “the business fails” into specific scenarios: reduce expenses, take contract work, sell a technical service, move to a cheaper setup, or run a smaller paid experiment.

Set a review cadence rather than changing strategy every bad week. Define what would prove the thesis, what would disprove it, and how long you will give the experiment. Commitment is strongest when it is paired with [[Reality Testing]].

**On using your technical background correctly:**

Your React and Tailwind skills are leverage, but they can also make building feel safer than selling. You can produce an impressive interface without learning whether the workflow changes a customer’s economics. Use your technical advantage to shorten the cycle from conversation to useful experiment, not to hide in a longer build cycle.

A good weekly allocation at this stage might put the majority of your prime hours into customer conversations, selling, onboarding, and observing real usage, with engineering focused on the smallest changes that remove the biggest bottleneck. AI can help you ship those changes faster, but it should not choose the product thesis for you.

**On building proof before broadening:**

You do not need a giant case-study library to build trust. You need honest evidence. Record what a customer was trying to do, what you changed, what happened, and what remains unresolved. Publish or privately share the useful details with the next prospect.

If outcome proof is still thin, document [[Effort Proof]]: interviews completed, onboarding friction removed, failed experiments, time saved, manual work performed, and customer language learned. Reality is more defensible than generic AI-generated advice. Over time, this process turns into case studies, referrals, product insight, and a reputation for keeping promises.

**On product scope and the first repeatable workflow:**

Your first product does not need an ecosystem. It needs one workflow that a defined customer wants completed reliably. Identify the moment where the user receives value and make that path short, observable, and repeatable.

You can manually perform adjacent work while learning. That is not failure or lack of scalability; it is a [[Concierge MVP]] and a source of product discovery. The danger is not manual work. The danger is adding several unrelated workflows before you know which one customers will pay to repeat.

Each manual step should answer a question: Does the customer value this? Can it be standardized? Can it be removed? Can software perform it? What would make the result faster, safer, or more certain? Productize what reality proves valuable.

**On measuring focus:**

Do not measure focus by how intensely you feel about the business. Measure it by resource allocation. At the end of each week, count how much time, money, code, content, and outreach went toward the chosen customer and outcome. Also count what you declined.

Useful weekly metrics include [[Customer Conversations]], qualified demos, paid conversions, activation rate, time to first value, retained users, churn reasons, revenue per customer, and hours spent on non-core work. The numbers are not a substitute for judgment; they are a defense against self-deception.

**On patience and the danger of premature comparison:**

A new tool or market can look better because you are comparing its imagined future with your current frustrations. Compare like with like. Ask what your current SaaS could look like after another year of focused customer learning, stronger onboarding, better pricing, improved distribution, and accumulated proof.

This does not mean persisting indefinitely with a false thesis. It means refusing to make a pivot based only on novelty or the emotional high of starting over. Change the vehicle when evidence says the core assumption is wrong; do not change because the current vehicle requires another difficult repetition.

**On your life outside the business:**

Focus should be deliberate rather than totalizing. If you choose to keep a relationship, health practice, creative activity, or family commitment, acknowledge that it consumes resources and may slow business growth. That does not make it irrational. It means you are optimizing for a life, not just a spreadsheet.

Decide what “success” means on an ordinary Tuesday. Include sleep, people, location, responsibilities, work hours, and the kind of problems you want to spend your time solving. Then make sure your SaaS strategy is taking you toward that life. A business that grows while quietly producing a life you do not want is not a clean win.

## Action Items

*   [ ] **Choose one 90-day SaaS thesis**: Name the ICP, painful workflow, promised outcome, core product, primary distribution channel, and three measurable success criteria.
*   [ ] **Audit every active project**: List products, features, side businesses, content channels, and partnerships; pause anything that does not serve the chosen customer and outcome.
*   [ ] **Identify the current bottleneck**: Classify the constraint as demand, conversion, activation, retention, monetization, delivery, talent, or founder capacity, then dedicate the next 30 days to it.
*   [ ] **Compare the core with the next shiny idea**: Estimate the return from one more year of focused work on the current SaaS versus the first two years of the new idea; include switching costs and lost learning.
*   [ ] **Interview ten target customers**: Ask about the workflow, current workaround, economic cost, urgency, buying authority, and what would make the result indispensable.
*   [ ] **Define the one repeatable value moment**: Write the observable event that proves a user received first value and measure time from signup or payment to that event.
*   [ ] **Run a retention review**: Contact active, inactive, and churned users; document why they stay, stop, expand, or recommend, then choose one retention experiment.
*   [ ] **Build a concierge pilot**: Personally deliver the highest-value version of the outcome for three to five customers, charge for it, and document which manual steps should become product.
*   [ ] **Write the three hard conversations**: Identify the people and decisions currently blocking focus; prepare scripts and schedule the conversations this week.
*   [ ] **Create a distraction inventory**: Record every attractive opportunity and put non-core ideas in a dated [[Later List]] with explicit criteria for reconsideration.
*   [ ] **Write a Plan B**: Specify runway, expenses to cut, temporary income options, people to call, and the next experiment if the current thesis fails.
*   [ ] **Publish one proof-of-work update**: Share a real customer problem, experiment, failure, product change, and result without pretending to have more authority than you have earned.
*   [ ] **Track resource concentration weekly**: Measure hours, money, code, and outreach spent on the chosen thesis, plus the number of non-core commitments declined.
*   [ ] **Set a push-versus-pivot review date**: List the assumptions that must be true, the evidence that would disprove them, and the date you will review the data.
*   [ ] **Define your life target**: Describe a successful ordinary Tuesday at the business size you want, including health, relationships, work hours, responsibility, and freedom.
