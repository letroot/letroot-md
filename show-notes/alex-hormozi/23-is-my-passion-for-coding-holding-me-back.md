# Is My Passion for Coding Holding Me Back?

## Summary
[[Alex Hormozi]] responds to a founder who tried [[Freelancing]] and a [[Development Agency]], remained stuck in day-to-day survival, and concluded that technical skill and passion for [[Coding]] were holding him back. Hormozi rejects that explanation: the likely bottlenecks were [[Sales]], [[Pricing]], and operating a custom-build business without charging enough for the value and complexity involved. Passion for the product is not the enemy of business; it becomes a problem only when it is used to avoid learning the commercial skills required to make the product viable. The episode then reframes [[Business Uncertainty]] as a vague fear that can be reduced by specific scenario planning and consistent action: [[Action Alleviates Anxiety]].

## Key Insights

*   **Passion is not the business problem**: The claim that caring deeply about [[Coding]] or the product makes someone bad at [[Business]] is usually [[Coping]] rather than diagnosis. Passion can sustain the founder through the repetition required to improve a product, understand a customer, and keep showing up after rejection.
    *   The crucial distinction is between passion for the work and competence in the surrounding business system. Loving the craft does not automatically teach [[Sales]], [[Pricing]], [[Marketing]], [[Customer Discovery]], or [[Operations]].
    *   A founder should not discard a genuine strength because the first business model failed to monetize it. The better question is which complementary skills are missing.
    *   Passion becomes a liability only when it turns into identity protection: refusing feedback, overbuilding, resisting a price increase, or treating commercial constraints as beneath the craft.

*   **Separate the work from the price**: [[Custom Software]] may be valuable and enjoyable while still being commercially unviable at the price charged. Hormozi's diagnostic is simple: if someone would do the work for a billion dollars but not for a dollar, the work itself is not the issue; the [[Price]] is wrong.
    *   The amount of effort required does not determine the price by itself. [[Value-Based Pricing]] asks what the outcome is worth to the customer, how scarce the capability is, and how much risk or time the solution removes.
    *   The founder in the episode assumed price was fixed and therefore concluded that delivering less work was the only path to survival. In reality, the offer, customer, positioning, scope, and price could all change.
    *   A low price can create a destructive loop: undercharge, overdeliver, consume too much time, lack cash, become anxious, and then reduce selling activity just when more demand is needed.

*   **Mispricing often exposes a sales skill gap**: Hormozi connects the founder's low prices to not knowing how to [[Sell]]. Without confidence in explaining value, handling objections, and asking for payment, a technically strong founder often uses a low price as emotional protection.
    *   Underpricing is not generosity if the business cannot support delivery. It is a promise the business may be unable to keep.
    *   Selling is a learnable skill rather than a character trait. Hormozi recalls failing to sell a [[Free Trial]] when he started his gym, illustrating that early failure is normal even for someone who later becomes an accomplished operator.
    *   The relevant response to weak sales is deliberate practice: more conversations, clearer offers, better questions, recorded calls, objection review, and repeated attempts.
    *   The technical founder's job is not to become less technical. It is to add [[Commercial Skill]] to the existing technical advantage.

*   **A job can remove desperation from the experiment**: Because the founder now has a full-time role, Hormozi recommends selling the service for what it is actually worth rather than accepting any deal to pay immediate bills.
    *   A salary can provide [[Runway]] and reduce the pressure that causes premature discounts. It can turn a business attempt into a bounded experiment rather than an emergency.
    *   This does not mean the founder should work indefinitely without a plan. It means using the safety net to test the offer honestly, collect evidence, and learn to sell without panic.
    *   The founder can say no to poor-fit customers, low-margin scope, and vague projects while continuing [[Advertising Activities]] that create more opportunities.

*   **Making sales and making money require different decisions**: The desire to make every possible sale can conflict with the goal of making money. Profit requires the willingness to say [[No]] to opportunities that do not meet the needed price, scope, margin, or customer-fit criteria.
    *   A sale is not automatically a win if it creates unpaid support, custom work, schedule chaos, or an unhappy customer.
    *   The seller must define a minimum acceptable price and a clear scope before entering a negotiation. Otherwise the prospect's urgency sets the terms.
    *   Saying no is a bet that continued [[Lead Generation]] and [[Demand Generation]] will create another opportunity. That requires faith grounded in a repeatable process, not passive optimism.
    *   The business improves when the founder learns to prefer a small number of profitable, well-scoped customers over a large number of desperate transactions.

*   **Action alleviates anxiety**: Anxiety about a business often grows in the gap between imagined possibilities and observable action. Taking the next controllable step reduces that gap.
    *   Action does not guarantee a particular outcome, but it produces information: replies, objections, conversion data, usage patterns, costs, and customer language.
    *   Inaction preserves uncertainty because no new evidence arrives. It also gives the mind room to generate increasingly dramatic scenarios.
    *   For a SaaS founder, action could mean sending qualified outreach, asking for a payment, watching a user complete onboarding, or calling a churned customer. It should be an observable behavior, not “think harder about the strategy.”
    *   [[Momentum]] is often created after action rather than before it. Waiting to feel confident can therefore become an avoidance strategy.

*   **Fear lives in the vague, not the specific**: “The uncertainty of business frightens me” is emotionally real but operationally unhelpful. The founder must ask which particular event is feared: reaching out, being ignored, losing a customer, running out of money, or having to take a job again.
    *   Specific fears can be assigned probabilities, mitigations, owners, deadlines, and next actions. Vague fear cannot be managed because it has no defined object.
    *   The [[Amygdala]] is good at generating a strong protective response but not at producing a detailed business forecast. A feeling of danger is not proof that danger is imminent.
    *   Converting “business failure” into a chain of events makes the scenario less mythical: insufficient sales, insufficient cash, missed bills, reduced expenses, temporary employment, and another attempt.
    *   This is not an argument to ignore risk. It is an argument to make risk legible enough to manage.

*   **Play out the worst case instead of letting fear play games**: Hormozi asks what “failure” would concretely mean. For this founder, it may mean not making money quickly enough, taking a job again, or living with friends or family for a period of time.
    *   Many founders treat returning to employment as a permanent verdict on their identity. It can instead be a financing mechanism, a skills-building period, or a temporary reset.
    *   The absolute worst case described is survival with shelter, food, and the ability to work. That may be uncomfortable, but it is different from the catastrophic story the anxious mind implies.
    *   The point is not to romanticize hardship or ignore responsibilities. It is to distinguish inconvenience and embarrassment from irreversible catastrophe.
    *   A written [[Downside Plan]] can preserve judgment when revenue is volatile: minimum cash threshold, expense cuts, income alternatives, and the date for reassessment.

*   **Failure is a definition, not only an event**: If a founder keeps trying, learning, and improving, Hormozi asks whether the attempt has actually failed. The outcome may be disappointing, but the meaning assigned to it remains partly chosen.
    *   A business can fail as a model while the founder succeeds at learning a market, improving sales, building a network, or discovering a better offer.
    *   This framing should not become an excuse to repeat the same experiment forever. Trying counts when it includes adaptation and [[Learning Loops]], not when it is unchanged persistence.
    *   Define success at multiple levels: the commercial result, the skills gained, the quality of the process, and the integrity with which the attempt was made.
    *   The child analogy is useful: no one expects a seven-year-old who tried hard to be a billionaire. We naturally value effort and improvement in others, so we can apply a similarly humane standard to ourselves.

*   **Success can begin with the decision to pursue it**: Hormozi says someone is successful the moment they decide they are. This is an identity and process claim, not a claim that revenue, customers, or competence do not matter.
    *   External milestones remain important for paying bills and building a durable company. The internal definition protects those milestones from becoming the only evidence of worth.
    *   When the founder defines success as becoming better at the process, every sales call and customer conversation can count as progress even before it produces revenue.
    *   A useful founder identity is not “I am already a successful SaaS founder.” It is “I am the kind of founder who runs honest experiments, learns from evidence, and keeps improving.”

*   **Unlimited shots require a viable survival system**: The episode's “unlimited shots on goal” idea applies when the founder can preserve the ability to make another attempt. Runway, employability, low fixed costs, relationships, and transferable skills all increase the number of attempts available.
    *   A safety net is not necessarily a lack of commitment. It can be a way to prevent one experiment from carrying more emotional and financial weight than it should.
    *   Technical ability gives the founder an income option, but it should be paired with sales and product skills so that the safety net grows rather than shrinks.
    *   The goal is not to take reckless risks because survival is possible. It is to make measured bets while protecting the capacity to continue.

*   **The core business problem may be an incomplete skill stack**: A developer can be excellent at building and still lack the skills required to create a profitable business. This is a normal gap, not a permanent diagnosis.
    *   The stack may include [[Offer Design]], positioning, prospecting, sales calls, pricing, delivery management, retention, bookkeeping, and hiring.
    *   Skill gaps become dangerous only when they are mislabeled as evidence that the underlying craft or market is wrong.
    *   Build a deliberate curriculum around the current bottleneck instead of consuming broad business content. If pricing is weak, study offers and run price tests; if demand is weak, do outreach and interview buyers.
    *   Feedback must come from behavior and transactions, not only from other builders who admire the product.

*   **Advertising is letting more people know about the offer**: Hormozi uses a plain definition of advertising activities: exposing more people to the thing being sold. The practical implication is to keep creating opportunities while accepting that many people will say no.
    *   [[Marketing]] is not a one-time announcement. It is repeated distribution of a clear promise to a relevant audience.
    *   Rejection is not evidence that nobody wants the product unless the sample, message, audience, and offer were appropriately tested.
    *   A founder needs a volume and quality process: identify prospects, contact them, track responses, ask why they decline, improve the message, and repeat.
    *   Faith in the next yes should be earned through enough attempts to learn the conversion pattern.

*   **The $100M Scaling Road Map illustrates stage-specific diagnosis**: At the end of the episode, Hormozi offers the [[Acquisition.com]] Scaling Road Map, based on reviewing portfolio companies across ten stages of growth and identifying where they became stuck.
    *   The underlying lesson is that businesses have different constraints at different stages. A tactic that helps an established company may distract an early-stage founder.
    *   The functions named include product, marketing, sales, customer success, recruiting, IT, human resources, and finance. A company can be strong in one function while bottlenecked by another.
    *   Use a [[Growth Stage]] diagnosis rather than copying the visible tactics of larger companies. Identify the constraint that most limits the next step, then allocate effort there.
    *   The resource is a starting framework, not a substitute for customer evidence or an excuse to delay selling.

## Coach's Corner

**On diagnosing the real bottleneck in your SaaS:**

Daniel, the most important lesson for you is to stop treating “I love building” as a confession that needs correcting. Your technical interest is an asset. The question is whether you are using it to create a valuable, repeatable outcome for a specific [[ICP]], and whether you have built the commercial muscles around it.

Write down your current bottleneck in one sentence. Choose only one primary bottleneck from [[Demand]], sales conversion, activation, retention, monetization, delivery, or founder capacity. If you cannot choose, gather evidence for one week: number of qualified prospects contacted, replies, demos, paid conversions, time to first value, active usage, churn, and revenue per account. The numbers will not make the decision for you, but they will prevent you from calling every uncomfortable problem “the product.”

Then make your calendar reflect the bottleneck. If demand is the problem, do not spend the week polishing a component library. If activation is the problem, do not buy more traffic. If retention is the problem, do not add a second product. Your coding passion should be aimed at the smallest product change that removes the current constraint.

**On turning passion into leverage:**

Passion for coding gives you patience with complexity, curiosity about systems, and the ability to ship experiments cheaply. Those advantages matter in an early-stage [[SaaS]]. They become leverage when tied to a customer outcome rather than to code volume.

For every feature you want to build, state the customer behavior it should change. Will it help a user reach first value faster, complete the core workflow more often, invite a teammate, retain a subscription, or justify a higher price? If you cannot name the behavior and the metric, put the feature in a [[Later List]]. You are not rejecting your interest; you are protecting it from being consumed by work that has no commercial consequence.

Use your technical strength to run tighter [[Build Measure Learn]] cycles: speak with a prospect, identify one painful step, build the smallest intervention, observe usage, and ask for payment or a renewal. The point is not to become less of a builder. It is to become a builder whose work is continuously informed by the market.

**On pricing your work without apology:**

If your product or service takes substantial skill and time, a low price does not make it more virtuous. It may make the offer impossible to deliver well. Price is part of the product because it determines scope, support, customer expectations, and your ability to keep improving the system.

Create three packages or levels, even if you initially sell only one. Define the customer outcome, time to value, included support, limits, and exclusions. Ask prospects what the problem currently costs them in time, missed revenue, risk, or frustration. You do not need to charge the entire value created, but you do need to know whether the price leaves enough room for delivery and profit.

Because you have technical skills, you may be tempted to accept bespoke requests as proof that customers want the product. A request is not validation until someone commits time, access, money, or a clear path to payment. Distinguish [[Customer Feedback]] from a buying signal.

**On learning sales as a technical founder:**

You do not need a loud or artificial sales persona. You need a clear diagnosis and a willingness to ask whether the prospect wants the result. A simple sales conversation can follow this sequence:

1. Understand the prospect's current workflow and the cost of leaving it unchanged.
2. Ask what they have tried and why it has not worked.
3. Confirm the desired result, urgency, authority, and budget.
4. Explain only the part of your product that addresses the verified problem.
5. Propose a specific next step, price, scope, and start date.
6. Ask for the decision and record the objection if the answer is no.

Do not use more engineering as a substitute for more conversations. Set a weekly minimum for qualified conversations and demos. Review each call afterward: where did the prospect lose interest, what words did they use, what assumption was wrong, and what should change in the offer? Sales improves through this feedback loop just as coding improves through debugging.

**On using your full-time role as runway:**

Your job can be a strategic advantage if you use it deliberately. It lets you refuse bad-fit work, test higher prices, and let the business earn demand rather than forcing every prospect into a deal. Establish a fixed weekly schedule for the SaaS so the job does not become a permanent hiding place, but also protect your sleep and health so your experiments remain high quality.

Set a runway policy: how much personal cash can fund the experiment, how many hours per week you can sustain, what revenue or customer evidence would justify reducing employment, and what evidence would make you change the offer. A job is not proof that the startup failed; it is one way to finance learning while preserving optionality.

**On making uncertainty specific:**

When you notice yourself imagining doomsday, open a document titled [[Uncertainty Log]] and complete these prompts:

* What exactly am I afraid will happen?
* What evidence says it is likely?
* What part is under my control?
* What is the earliest warning signal?
* What action would reduce the probability or impact?
* If it happens, what is the next practical step?

For example, “the SaaS could fail” is too vague. A specific version might be “I will not reach five paying customers by October 1.” That can be tested by identifying the required number of qualified conversations, demos, close rate, price, and weekly outreach. If the result is missed, you have information and a decision point, not a supernatural verdict.

The fear may be rejection, public embarrassment, loss of income, disappointing a customer, or discovering that your current idea is not good enough. Name the fear so you can practice the corresponding skill. Seventeen unanswered emails require a better prospect list, message, volume, or channel; they do not require a new identity.

**On action as anxiety treatment:**

Your anti-anxiety protocol should be behavioral and small enough to execute on a bad day. When you feel stuck, choose one of these within ten minutes: send one highly relevant outreach message, ask one user for a call, present a price to one prospect, watch one onboarding session, fix one activation obstacle, or write one honest postmortem.

Do not wait for the action to feel meaningful. The purpose is to exchange imagined uncertainty for evidence. At the end of the day, record what happened. Over time you will build a personal database of evidence showing that most feared events are survivable and that consistent activity creates opportunities.

**On defining failure and success for this season:**

Choose two definitions: a commercial definition and a founder-process definition. The commercial definition might be a number of paying customers, monthly recurring revenue, retention rate, or a validated willingness to pay by a date. The process definition might be completing a fixed number of customer conversations, shipping only evidence-backed improvements, and reviewing the data weekly.

This prevents the common trap of moving the goalposts emotionally. If you do not hit the commercial target but complete an honest test, learn why prospects decline, and improve your offer, the experiment produced value. If you hit a revenue target by accepting unprofitable custom work, the number may be hiding a broken model.

Keep the compassionate frame from the episode: you would be proud of a child for trying and getting better, not only for reaching an extreme financial milestone. Apply that standard without lowering your standards for execution.

**On preserving your shots on goal:**

Your ability to keep trying depends on your [[Personal Runway]], health, relationships, and employability. Make those explicit. Know your monthly minimum expenses. Keep fixed costs low while the offer is uncertain. Maintain a credible technical portfolio and relationships so that contract work or employment remains available if needed. Do not interpret a safety net as permission to avoid commitment; interpret it as protection against making desperate decisions.

At the same time, avoid using endless runway to postpone a real test. Give each experiment a start date, a measurable thesis, a weekly activity target, and a review date. You want enough safety to think clearly and enough constraint to act.

**On saying no to low-quality revenue:**

For your SaaS, define your [[Minimum Viable Customer]] and [[Minimum Viable Price]]. Write the conditions under which you will decline a prospect: wrong persona, no urgent problem, excessive custom work, no access to the decision-maker, insufficient budget, or support burden that would damage retention for better-fit customers.

This will feel uncomfortable when your pipeline is thin. That discomfort is exactly why you need the rule before the negotiation. A price floor is not an ego exercise; it is a capacity and focus boundary. If no one accepts the price, investigate the offer, outcome, proof, and audience before automatically discounting.

**On the next 30 days:**

Run a focused commercial sprint. Pick one customer segment and one painful workflow. Conduct at least ten conversations, make a concrete offer to qualified prospects, and charge for a concierge or limited pilot if the product is not yet self-serve. Keep engineering constrained to changes that improve the promised outcome, time to value, or retention.

At the end of the sprint, review evidence rather than mood. Which prospects had the problem? Which words described its cost? Who paid or agreed to a paid next step? Where did delivery take too long? Which part of the offer was misunderstood? That review will tell you whether to improve the offer, change the segment, adjust price, or continue with more volume.

**On building an honest operating cadence:**

Create a weekly founder review with five questions:

1. What was the single most important constraint?
2. What customer-facing actions did I complete?
3. What did customers actually do, pay for, or refuse?
4. Where did I overbuild, undercharge, or avoid a conversation?
5. What is the one action and one experiment for next week?

Use [[Obsidian]] to keep the log, link each experiment to its customer evidence, and preserve the reasoning behind price and product decisions. This turns anxiety into an accumulating operating record. You will be able to see whether you are truly improving rather than relying on how motivated you felt.

## Action Items

*   [ ] **Name the bottleneck**: Classify the current SaaS constraint as demand, sales conversion, activation, retention, monetization, delivery, or founder capacity; support the choice with one week of evidence.
*   [ ] **Write a 30-day commercial thesis**: Define the target customer, painful workflow, promised outcome, price hypothesis, activity target, and review date.
*   [ ] **Create three offer levels**: Specify outcome, scope, support, limits, exclusions, and price for each package; define the minimum price you will accept.
*   [ ] **Interview ten target customers**: Ask about the current workflow, workaround, cost of the problem, urgency, decision process, and buying criteria.
*   [ ] **Run five real sales conversations**: Present the offer, ask for payment or a paid pilot, and record the exact objection when the answer is no.
*   [ ] **Build a pricing evidence sheet**: Compare customer value, delivery hours, support burden, costs, and desired margin; test a higher price with the next qualified prospect.
*   [ ] **Define the core value moment**: Write the observable event that proves a user received value and measure time from signup or payment to that event.
*   [ ] **Restrict the build queue**: Link every feature to activation, retention, monetization, delivery efficiency, or the core customer outcome; move the rest to a dated [[Later List]].
*   [ ] **Launch a concierge pilot**: Deliver the outcome manually for three to five customers, charge for it, and document which steps should become software.
*   [ ] **Start an uncertainty log**: Break each vague fear into a specific scenario, controllable variables, early warning signal, mitigation, and next action.
*   [ ] **Write a downside plan**: Set cash thresholds, expense cuts, temporary income options, support contacts, and the conditions for changing or pausing the experiment.
*   [ ] **Set a daily action floor**: Complete at least one customer-facing action every workday, even when anxiety is high; log the result in [[Obsidian]].
*   [ ] **Define success at two levels**: Set one commercial target and one process target so that learning and disciplined execution are visible before revenue compounds.
*   [ ] **Run a weekly founder review**: Audit outreach, conversations, offers, prices, product work, customer behavior, and the next bottleneck every week.
*   [ ] **Study one missing business skill**: Choose sales, pricing, offer design, or customer discovery based on evidence and practice it through live work rather than passive consumption.
*   [ ] **Download the [Scaling Road Map](https://www.acquisition.com/roadmap)**: Use it to generate stage-specific questions, then validate the diagnosis against your own customer and revenue data.
