# How To Make More Money Than You Deserve (My 2027 Framework)

## Summary

[[Alex Hormozi]] argues that the business vehicle matters as much as, and sometimes more than, the founder's work ethic and intelligence. His "idiot index" compares businesses by operational difficulty and potential financial upside, prompted by seeing exceptionally capable people struggle in difficult markets while less capable operators prosper in favorable ones. He groups opportunities into labor-heavy commodities, easy starter cash businesses, expert-dependent businesses, moat-built enterprises, and demand/supply arbitrage opportunities. The right choice depends on whether the founder wants income, immediate cash flow, a transferable asset, or an exceptionally large company. Barriers can be frustrating but also protect a business from competition; temporary demand imbalances can produce attractive economics, but they are not guaranteed to last.

## Key Insights

* **Choose the boat before rowing harder**: Hormozi recounts a story attributed to [[Warren Buffett]] about a more intelligent, harder-working business-school peer who entered steel while Buffett entered investing. Their different outcomes illustrate his argument that industry economics shape the return on effort. A founder can work 60-hour weeks in either a restaurant or a software company, yet their potential scale and enterprise value may differ substantially.
    * Ask what a business looks like when it is "maxed out": what have strong operators actually built, and does that outcome match your ambitions? An exception does not establish that the typical opportunity is attractive.
    * This is a decision framework, not a claim that everyone must maximize wealth. A business may be worthwhile because it supports a family, fits a lifestyle, or serves a calling.

* **1. Labor-heavy commodities: hard work, limited upside**: Low barriers to entry invite competition; incremental growth requires more people, management, and operational coordination. Hormozi places traditional mid-market sit-down restaurants, service-based gyms, small-business agencies, physical retail, and event planning in this category.
    * He recalls successful gym owners asking how to scale beyond ten locations. His answer was that the same operating skills might earn more in a different vehicle. A gym franchisor or owner of many franchise units is operating a different model from an individual service-based gym.
    * The important question is not whether success is possible, but how much work and organizational complexity it takes relative to the available upside.

* **2. Starter cash businesses: easier to start, usually capped**: Dropshipping, light home services such as power washing or deck staining, vending and ATMs, real-estate wholesaling, Airbnb arbitrage, and affiliate content can generate meaningful income with relatively modest complexity or capital.
    * One skilled operator can sometimes build a high personal income, learn sales and operations, and accumulate capital. That makes the category useful even if it seldom produces enormous enterprise value.
    * Do not confuse an income goal with a billion-dollar-company goal. These examples are starting points for investigating economics, not promises that a particular operation will be easy or profitable.

* **3. Expert-dependent businesses: high income without necessarily creating a sellable asset**: Online coaching, AI implementation, construction, rehab services, surgeons, enterprise agencies, business-opportunity businesses, and personality-driven influencers can generate significant earnings, often with a small team and high-margin services.
    * Media, staff, and systems act like an "Iron Man suit" around an expert, extending their reach. But if customers buy primarily because of the founder's personal involvement, the income stream may be difficult to transfer or sell.
    * Exceptional outcomes such as major podcast deals exist, but [[Survivorship Bias]] matters: compare them with the many creators who never reach that scale. Distinguish a valuable company from an enhanced, lucrative job.

* **4. Moat-built enterprises: hard to build, potentially high enterprise value**: Pharmaceuticals, specialized manufacturing, franchising, roll-ups, marketplaces, SaaS, consumer packaged goods, banks, energy, data centers, and telecom can produce substantial transferable value, but usually require combinations of skill, capital, time, regulation, brand, supply chain, or network effects.
    * Pharmaceuticals combine capital, technical skill, regulatory approval, and time; franchising needs sound unit economics and a model that does not depend on unusually talented individual operators. Roll-ups require acquiring and integrating businesses, which is much harder than describing the strategy.
    * Marketplaces are difficult to start but can gain network effects; CPG may depend on brand, capital, and supply-chain execution. Hormozi suggests AI makes software easier to build, but this also increases competition and can erode a purely technical barrier.
    * Difficulty can be an advantage once overcome: if every competitor must clear the same meaningful hurdle, fewer may compete. It is not proof of success—regulatory, capital, execution, and market risks remain.

* **5. Demand/supply arbitrage: strong demand and constrained supply**: Hormozi's "easy, high money" quadrant describes opportunities where demand outstrips supply or where demographic and regulatory changes open a window. These are relative judgments from his observations, not a guarantee that the businesses are easy.
    * He points to med spas, longevity and aesthetics, wealth advice, and elder care as potential beneficiaries of aging populations with needs, time, and purchasing power. He argues that wealth advisors may benefit from sticky client relationships and fees on assets that can compound, although customer retention and markets are not assured.
    * Regulatory changes can create a wave: he cites the earlier Botox and cannabis booms and current demand for GLP-1-related services. Such windows can change as supply, rules, and competition catch up.
    * Debt collection, commercial cleaning, and elevator maintenance illustrate his argument that unglamorous or specialized work can attract less competition. Commercial cleaning may have sticky recurring customers; elevator maintenance can benefit from specialization and a concentrated manufacturer base. These are hypotheses to validate locally, including labor, safety, compliance, and customer acquisition.
    * In real-estate syndication, the sponsor may gain substantial leverage through outside capital and borrowing. His illustration turns $1 million of sponsor capital into a $20 million fund and, with three-times property leverage, $60 million of buying power; the sponsor receives a share of the economics. Leverage magnifies risk as well as potential returns, and structures and terms vary.

* **Match the vehicle to the outcome**: For cash flow, prioritize a model that can produce cash and teach skills; for high income, expert leverage may suffice; for enterprise value, seek scalability, transferability, and defensibility; for a very large outcome, also consider market size. Hormozi's final advice is to select the boat deliberately and then work hard within it.

## Coach's Corner

**For your SaaS:** Software is a vehicle with potentially attractive distribution and recurring-revenue economics, not an automatic moat. Easier AI-assisted development can increase both your speed and competitors' speed. Look for a specific underserved customer and a painful, repeated workflow where you can earn retention through integration, embedded process, proprietary insight, trusted distribution, or network effects. Treat each proposed moat as a hypothesis until customer behavior supports it.

**Separate the destination from the route:** Write down whether this stage is about replacing income, accumulating runway, or building a transferable company. A service or concierge offer can fund learning and reveal a valuable workflow without being the eventual product. Conversely, a founder-dependent service should not be valued as if it were already a scalable SaaS company.

**Investigate the unglamorous:** Interview operators in niches whose work is recurring, expensive to get wrong, and underserved by software. Look for evidence of genuine urgency and willingness to pay—not just an appealing story about an aging market or low competition. A small niche may be an excellent entry point even if expansion is needed later to reach your desired market size.

**Compare economics before switching:** Hormozi's categories are directional and contain many exceptions. Assess actual acquisition cost, gross margin, retention, market size, regulatory burden, distribution, capital requirements, founder dependency, and transferability. Don't abandon a working product solely because another sector sounds easier; use customer evidence and the switching cost to make the decision.

## Action Items

* [ ] Write your desired outcome for the next 2–3 years: income, cash flow, transferable asset, or scale; specify a measurable target.
* [ ] Map your current SaaS against the five categories and identify which assumptions about demand, competition, retention, and defensibility are untested.
* [ ] Interview five prospective customers about a recurring, costly workflow, their current workaround, buying process, and willingness to pay.
* [ ] Compare two or three possible customer niches on urgency, reachable market, acquisition, competition, retention, and delivery cost.
* [ ] Identify one potential moat and define evidence that would demonstrate it, such as sustained retention after competitors appear or costly workflow integration.
* [ ] Calculate the economics of a small paid pilot and separate founder-dependent delivery from steps that could be made repeatable.
* [ ] Review the strongest real businesses in the chosen niche, including less-visible failures, before drawing conclusions about the ceiling.
