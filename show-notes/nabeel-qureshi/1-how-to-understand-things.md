# How To Understand Things

## Summary
[[Nabeel Qureshi]] argues that what we call "intelligence" is less about raw processing power and more about a set of learnable virtues: honesty with yourself, the willingness to sit with confusion, and the courage to look stupid. Using examples from [[Michael Faraday]], [[William Shockley]], and his own math education, he shows that real understanding requires attacking a topic from multiple angles, going slow, and getting close to direct, first-hand data rather than consuming other people's pre-digested narratives.

## Key Insights
*   **Intelligence as Virtue, Not Just Hardware**: Processing speed and working memory ("hardware") vary a lot even among very smart people, but the "software" traits -- honesty, persistence, willingness to not stop at a shallow answer -- are common to all of them and can be learned.
*   **The Will to Think**: Citing Shockley and Fermi, real thinking requires intrinsic motivation to do the "tedious and precise thinking" most people avoid because it's effortful and it's easy to fool yourself that you already understand something.
*   **Intellectual Honesty**: Feynman's principle that "the first rule of science is that you do not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." Writing is a forcing function for this -- it's much harder to hide confused thinking once you try to write it down clearly.
*   **Physical Intuition & Concrete Models**: [[Michael Faraday]] understood electromagnetism through hands-on experiments rather than abstract math, which let him make discoveries that eluded more mathematically sophisticated contemporaries like Ampere. Building a concrete, visualizable model of a concept beats a purely symbolic one.
*   **Unafraid to Look Stupid**: The willingness to ask "dumb" questions -- exemplified by Malcolm Gladwell's father -- is a courage trait, not an intelligence trait, and it routinely reveals that nobody else in the room understood the topic either.
*   **Go Slow, Go Narrow**: The Agassiz fish parable and the "Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance" Bozeman brick story both show that narrowing your focus (to one fish, one brick) unlocks depth that broad, shallow attention never reaches.
*   **Understanding Has Layers**: Understanding is not binary. You can "understand" the equals sign and still discover, as Kolmogorov showed, that there are much deeper layers to it. Depth comes from time spent, not a single pass.
*   **Get Close to Primary Data**: Reading secondhand narratives (news articles, popular science) is a lossy compression of reality and can make you stupider by filling your head with cached takes. Go for information-dense, first-hand sources and reason up from there.

## Coach's Corner
**On Marketing & Removing Friction:** When you're trying to understand your own customers, don't settle for secondhand narratives (what competitors say, what Twitter thinks your market wants). Go get primary data -- actual customer calls, actual usage logs -- and reason up from there. The founders who "get" their market deeply are the ones willing to sit with raw, messy data instead of a clean secondhand narrative.

**On Productivity & Winning the Morning:** Apply "the will to think" to the hardest, most avoided part of your business (pricing strategy, a technical architecture decision, a hard conversation with a cofounder). The discomfort of not immediately understanding something is exactly the signal to lean in, not to accept the first shallow answer and move on.

**On Building & Technical Depth:** When you hit a bug or an architectural question you don't fully understand, resist the urge to just copy a Stack Overflow fix and move on. Nabeel's "go slow" advice -- actually understanding *why* something works -- compounds over years into the kind of technical intuition that lets you build faster and debug better later.

**On Founder Courage:** Being "unafraid to look stupid" translates directly to founder life: asking a customer a basic question about their workflow, admitting to an investor you don't understand a term sheet clause, asking a technical cofounder to re-explain something. Most founders who seem confident are just better at hiding the same confusion you have -- ask anyway.

## Action Items
*   [ ] **Pick one confusing thing and go slow**: Choose one concept in your business (a metric, a technical system, a market dynamic) you've been glossing over, and spend a week actually working to understand it deeply instead of accepting the surface answer.
*   [ ] **Write to find your confusion**: Write a short explainer of your product's core value prop or your growth strategy. Wherever the writing gets confused or vague, that's where your understanding is shallow.
*   [ ] **Get primary data this week**: Talk directly to three customers or dig into raw usage logs instead of relying on secondhand summaries or assumptions about what users want.
*   [ ] **Ask one "dumb" question**: In your next meeting/call, ask the basic question you're tempted to suppress out of fear of looking behind.
