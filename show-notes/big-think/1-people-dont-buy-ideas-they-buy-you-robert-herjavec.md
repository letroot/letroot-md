# People don’t buy ideas. They buy you. | Robert Herjavec

## Summary
[[Robert Herjavec]] explains that most pitches fail before the details even matter because people are evaluating the person carrying the idea, not just the idea itself. Drawing from years on [[Shark Tank]], he argues that strong communicators build trust, emotional connection, and context before diving into numbers or features. The central lesson is simple: if you want an idea to land inside a company or with investors, you need to embody it, match your delivery to the moment, and translate your message into the language of the person making the decision.

## Key Insights
* **People buy the founder before they buy the idea**: Herjavec says the difference between two similar pitches is often not the numbers but the person. One entrepreneur can miss details and still get grace because they have already sold confidence, credibility, and conviction.
* **It is your responsibility to be heard**: His blunt line from [[Shark Tank]] is that listeners do not automatically owe you perfect attention. If your message matters, you have to package and deliver it in a way that earns focus and cuts through distraction.
* **Bad pitches start with features instead of connection**: Weak presenters rush into product mechanics, metrics, and technical detail. Strong presenters first establish who they are, why they care, how the idea emerged, and why it matters.
* **Meaning creates persuasion**: Herjavec wants to know what the idea means to the presenter, not just what the idea does. Personal investment signals seriousness and makes the pitch feel more believable.
* **The first two minutes carry disproportionate weight**: He says that on [[Shark Tank]], they often know whether they are interested within the first two minutes, even though the full pitch can last over an hour before editing. Early impression formation matters more than most people think.
* **The subconscious message often beats the conscious one**: Posture, pacing, emotional steadiness, and physical presence affect trust before the audience evaluates the actual content. If a presenter looks frantic, the audience starts feeling danger even if the words are fine.
* **Your delivery must match the idea**: If you are selling stability, your demeanor should feel stable. If you are selling energy and customer excitement, your delivery should feel energetic. The person and the proposition need to feel coherent.
* **Mismatch weakens trust**: Audiences notice when the emotional tone is off. A smiling pitch for something solemn or a nervous pitch for something that requires calm authority creates subconscious friction.
* **You are the representative of the product**: The pitch is not separate from the presenter. In the room, the audience experiences the business through your face, voice, timing, and emotional control.
* **Context changes the language of persuasion**: Herjavec says you should speak differently depending on who is in front of you. A manager may care about execution details, while a CEO will care more about business outcomes, cost, value, and return on investment.
* **Higher-level decision-makers think in outcomes**: As you move upward in an organization, pitch less around tasks and more around impact. Link your proposal to [[ROI]], strategic value, efficiency, cost, growth, or risk reduction.
* **Communication is audience design**: A pitch is not merely self-expression. It is translation. The same idea should be reframed for a teammate, a manager, an executive, or an investor.
* **Confidence is emotional regulation, not volume**: Herjavec's examples suggest that trust comes less from sounding loud or aggressive and more from seeming centered, composed, and equal to the ask.
* **A pitch is partly a trust test**: The audience is silently asking whether this person can carry the idea through uncertainty. Delivery becomes evidence of execution ability.
* **Self-presentation is part of execution**: Founders and operators sometimes think presentation is secondary to substance. Herjavec's point is that presentation is part of substance because it determines whether the underlying substance gets a fair hearing.

## Coach's Corner
**On Selling as a Founder:**
This is one of those lessons that sounds obvious until you watch yourself ignore it. As a founder, you will be tempted to believe that if the product is good enough, people will see it. Usually they will not. They will see you first. They will infer product quality from your clarity, confidence, structure, and presence. That is not always fair, but it is real. Your job is not just to build a good thing. Your job is to become a credible carrier of the thing.

**On Why Technical Founders Get Punished Here:**
Technical builders often open with mechanics because mechanics feel safe. Features are concrete. Architecture is concrete. Numbers feel objective. But buyers, hires, investors, and even teammates are first trying to answer more basic questions: Why this? Why now? Why you? Why should I trust your judgment? If you skip those questions and sprint into detail, you force the listener to work too hard before they care.

**On Embodiment:**
The strongest line in this piece is that "you has to equal the idea." That is a founder discipline. If your company sells calm reliability, your communication cannot feel chaotic. If your product promises simplicity, your pitch cannot feel confusing. If you sell speed, your follow-up cannot drag for days. Over time, your business becomes believable when your behavior and your claims line up.

**On Executive Communication:**
A lot of smart people lose influence because they do not change altitude. They pitch the CEO as if they are pitching a peer. Senior leaders usually care less about your internal fascination with the mechanism and more about the effect on the business. When you are speaking upward, translate your idea into leverage: revenue, cost savings, risk reduction, speed, retention, or strategic positioning.

**On First Impressions:**
The first two minutes matter because people are not only evaluating the proposal. They are deciding what category to put you in. Calm operator or scattered amateur. Trusted guide or needy persuader. Serious builder or clever talker. You do not win that judgment with hype. You win it with clarity, grounded energy, and a sense that you know exactly what problem you are solving.

**On Your Own SaaS Work:**
For you, the practical move is to treat every explanation of your product as pitch practice. Sales calls, demos, investor updates, landing pages, onboarding flows, even support replies all train the same muscle: can you make the value legible quickly and credibly? You do not need to become slick. You need to become clear, congruent, and audience-aware.

## Action Items
* [ ] **Rewrite your core pitch in two layers**: one version that leads with human stakes and one version that leads with business outcomes.
* [ ] **Record a two-minute explanation**: watch for posture, pacing, filler words, and whether your energy matches the promise of the product.
* [ ] **Lead with meaning before mechanics**: on your next pitch or demo, explain why the product matters before showing features.
* [ ] **Translate one idea for three audiences**: write versions for a user, a manager, and a CEO.
* [ ] **Audit congruence**: list what your product promises and check whether your communication style reinforces or contradicts that promise.
* [ ] **Tighten first impressions**: script the first ninety seconds of your pitch so it creates trust before detail.
