# Vulnerable

## Summary
[[Tyler Denk]] argues that one of the strongest founder advantages is the ability to separate ego from the current state of the product. In this [[Big Desk Energy]] essay, he explains how publicly inviting users to criticize [[beehiiv]] turned into a fast, concrete product-planning exercise instead of a reputational crisis. The core lesson is that harsh feedback is only dangerous when the founder treats it as a verdict on himself rather than as a roadmap for what to fix. The piece connects public vulnerability, fast shipping, product velocity, and emotional detachment into one operating muscle.

## Key Insights
* **Untangle ego from the product**: Denk frames founder vulnerability as a product skill, not a personality trick. If the current product state feels like a verdict on you, criticism becomes threatening. If the product is an artifact under construction, criticism becomes useful data.
* **Ask what you are getting wrong**: Listening to user requests is the easy half of [[Customer Feedback]]. The harder half is directly asking where the product is disappointing people, especially when the answer might be public and uncomfortable.
* **Caring beats silence**: Over 200 people responded when Denk asked users to criticize [[beehiiv]]. His read is that strong criticism is better than apathy. If nobody complains when something is broken, the deeper problem may be that nobody cares enough.
* **Public feedback can compress roadmap discovery**: A single public prompt produced enough raw material to shape a Q3 roadmap. Denk read every reply, answered where useful, shared the raw feedback with relevant teams, synthesized themes with [[Claude]], and booked follow-up sessions for the loudest complaint.
* **Raw feedback creates internal urgency**: The website builder and Ad Network complaints were already known priorities, but the public specificity made them harder to ignore. The team killed some lower-priority work, added bandwidth, brought on new advertisers, and increased average Ad Network payouts.
* **There are real downsides to public candor**: Publicly exposing weaknesses can hurt team morale, make the external narrative messy, and hand competitors talking points. Denk does not pretend the tactic is costless; he argues the learning upside can still dominate.
* **Ship at 90 percent when learning matters more than polish**: Denk connects vulnerability to [[Product Velocity]]. The last 10 percent of work can consume most of the time, and some of that polish rests on assumptions that only real users can validate.
* **A mediocre first impression can be a strategic tradeoff**: The tradeoff is not between perfect and bad. The tradeoff is between waiting for imagined polish and letting real usage tell you which rough edges matter. For Denk, a mediocre first impression today can produce a better experience for the next hundred thousand users.
* **Real users focus engineering teams**: Nothing sharpens product judgment like people using the feature in production. Internal taste matters, but live usage reveals the actual hierarchy of pain.
* **Vulnerability is a repeatable muscle**: Denk compares public product criticism, emotional podcast conversations, and shipping unfinished work as versions of the same move: put the real thing in front of people before it feels fully guarded.
* **Progress requires visible imperfection**: Too many people stall because they fear the first unpolished step. Starting a company, launching a project, shipping a feature, or asking someone out all require a willingness to be seen before the outcome is guaranteed.

## Coach's Corner
**On Founder Ego and Product Truth:**
This is a useful note for you because SaaS builders can easily confuse product criticism with identity criticism. If a user says the onboarding is confusing, the website builder is weak, or the offer is unclear, that is not a statement about your worth. It is a map. Your job is to build the internal strength to hear the map without defending the old terrain.

**On Asking for the Hard Feedback:**
Most founders say they want feedback, but they mostly want validation with a few safe suggestions. Denk is pointing to a higher standard: ask users what is wrong, where they almost churned, what they would warn a friend about, and what makes the product feel amateur. That kind of question creates pain, but it also reveals what vague analytics often hide.

**On Shipping Faster Without Becoming Careless:**
The 90 percent idea needs discipline. It is not permission to ship broken work because you are impatient. It is permission to ship a coherent, usable version before you have guessed every edge case. The standard should be: useful enough to respect the user, rough enough to learn fast, and monitored closely enough to correct quickly.

**On Team Morale:**
If you share harsh feedback with your team, do it as a builder, not an accuser. The message should be: "Here is what reality is telling us. Here is what matters most. Here is how we are going to respond." Public criticism can demoralize people when leadership uses it as shame. It can energize people when leadership uses it as clarity.

**On Your Own Development as a Young Man:**
The personal lesson is not only about products. There is a broader pattern here: growth requires letting yourself be seen before the work is fully polished. You cannot build a company, develop competence, or mature socially while hiding from all possible judgment. The move is to detach ego, expose the work, listen carefully, and improve fast.

## Action Items
* [ ] **Ask users what you are getting wrong**: Send one direct prompt asking for the most frustrating, disappointing, or confusing part of your product.
* [ ] **Separate criticism from identity**: Write down three current product weaknesses and phrase each one as a to-do list, not a personal failure.
* [ ] **Triage feedback into a roadmap**: Group user complaints by theme, severity, frequency, and revenue impact.
* [ ] **Run five follow-up sessions**: For the loudest complaint, invite affected users into short calls and listen for repeated pain.
* [ ] **Choose one 90 percent ship**: Identify a feature or improvement that is coherent enough to release but still needs real usage to guide the final 10 percent.
* [ ] **Share raw feedback with care**: Give the relevant team the actual user words, plus a clear owner, priority, and next step.
