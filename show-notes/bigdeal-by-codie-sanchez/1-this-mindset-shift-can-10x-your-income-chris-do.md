# This Mindset Shift Can 10x Your Income | Chris Do

## Summary

[[Chris Do]] joins [[Codie Sanchez]] to explain why most founders, freelancers, and creatives do not have a pure pricing problem so much as a [[Positioning]] and self-concept problem. The conversation moves from practical negotiation tactics into deeper ideas about [[Brand]], value perception, scarcity, and the internal stories people tell themselves about what they are worth. Chris's core argument is that money is more malleable than most people think, and once you stop entering deals from a needy posture, your pricing, confidence, and decisions change dramatically.

## Key Insights

- **[[With or Without You Energy]]**: One of Chris's clearest ideas is that desperation leaks into every sales conversation. He argues that when you walk into a negotiation acting like you need this exact client, this exact approval, or this exact paycheck, you immediately give away leverage. The practical example underneath the point is simple: the person solving a real business problem is often more valuable than they realize, but they sabotage themselves by speaking from scarcity. Chris keeps returning to the idea that your posture should be, in effect, "I would love to work together, but I do not need to force this." That shift changes tone, boundaries, pricing confidence, and what kinds of compromises you are willing to make.
- **[[Underpricing Is an Identity Problem]]**: Chris pushes beyond tactics and says many people charge too little because they still see themselves as junior, unproven, or easy to replace. In other words, the spreadsheet is not the whole problem; the self-concept is. That is why some people learn negotiation frameworks and still fold the second a client pushes back. They have not yet become the kind of person who believes their thinking, taste, or execution justifies a premium. The episode treats under-earning less like a math error and more like a worldview issue.
- **[[Self-Worth Sets the Ceiling]]**: A major thread in the episode is that income often expands only to the level your internal identity can tolerate. Chris frames self-worth as the hidden ceiling over pricing, ambition, and opportunity. If part of you believes you are not that valuable, not that special, or not yet allowed to occupy a bigger room, then you will unconsciously keep restoring yourself to a lower level even when chances to earn more appear. That is why the conversation feels bigger than business advice: it suggests that money is not just negotiated externally but also emotionally permitted internally.
- **[[Childhood Scripts Echo in Business]]**: Chris connects present-day business behavior to much older emotional programming. The way someone avoids conflict, seeks approval, apologizes for their prices, or hesitates to make a bold ask may have less to do with market conditions and more to do with family patterns formed early in life. This gives the episode a psychological depth that a normal pricing conversation would not have. Instead of treating low confidence as a temporary performance glitch, Chris treats it as something that may be rooted in long-standing scripts about worth, safety, love, and acceptance.
- **[[Praise, Love, and Emotional Reserve]]**: One of the most memorable arcs in the conversation is not about pricing at all but about family and emotional expression. Chris reflects on how hard it can be to say certain affirming things directly, including telling his children that he is proud of them. That example matters because it reveals the same underlying tension present in the business material: love may be real, care may be real, value may be real, but articulation can still be blocked. The episode uses that kind of example to show how emotionally reserved patterns do not stay in one compartment; they often shape parenting, leadership, relationships, and business communication all at once.
- **[[Achievement Cannot Fully Heal Insecurity]]**: Another important implication in the episode is that accomplishment does not automatically resolve inner wounds. A person can become highly skilled, financially successful, respected, and influential, and still carry old emotional constraints that show up in subtle ways. Chris's reflections suggest that business success can coexist with guardedness, difficulty receiving affection, or difficulty giving direct affirmation. That makes the conversation more honest than typical success content because it refuses the fantasy that money alone completes the emotional story.
- **[[Ask for Measurable Promotion Criteria]]**: In the more practical career advice sections, Chris offers a concrete alternative to passive frustration. Instead of quietly resenting a boss or hoping good work gets noticed, ask what exact outcomes would justify a raise or promotion. The strength of this idea is its specificity: define the benchmark, agree on the measurement, set a time horizon, and then revisit the conversation with evidence. It turns vague desire into a testable path and removes some of the emotion from the ask.
- **[[Charge More to Serve Better]]**: Chris makes an ethical argument for premium pricing rather than a purely selfish one. He suggests that charging too little can actually reduce the quality of what you are able to deliver because it leaves no room for time, care, margin, experimentation, or talent density. In that sense, lower pricing is not always a virtue. Sometimes it is the thing that prevents great service from existing. The deeper message is that price is not just extraction; it can also be what funds excellence.
- **[[Price Anchoring and Bracketing]]**: Rather than tossing out one defensive number and waiting to be judged, Chris describes a more intentional way to structure the conversation. By anchoring high and showing options or ranges, you influence how the buyer interprets value before they fixate on a single price. That reframes the exchange from "Why does this cost so much?" to "Which level of solution fits what I am trying to achieve?" The idea is not manipulation so much as intelligent framing.
- **[[Budget Excavation]]**: Chris also emphasizes that serious sales conversations require better questions. Instead of asking for budget once and accepting the first answer, the stronger move is to uncover what the problem is costing, what the upside of solving it would be, and what resources are actually available when the issue becomes urgent enough. This point matters because many founders assume customers are price-constrained when in reality they are value-unconvinced. Better discovery changes that.
- **[[Brand Is Premium Justification]]**: Chris talks about [[Brand]] as far more than a logo, aesthetic, or decorative layer. Brand is the meaning system around the offer: what people assume about your taste, standards, distinctiveness, and reliability before they fully inspect the mechanics. That is why two similar services can command radically different prices. The episode makes the case that premium pricing works best when the surrounding signals make the premium believable.
- **[[Different Beats Better]]**: A strong recurring idea is that trying to be generically "better" often leaves you forgettable. Chris argues that businesses gain pricing power when they become more specific, more legible, and more sharply contrasted from the pack. In practice, that means clearer point of view, clearer category framing, and a willingness to not resemble everybody else in the market. Memorability is treated as an economic advantage, not just a branding nicety.
- **[[Passion Follows Mastery]]**: Chris pushes back on the popular advice to begin with passion and let everything else follow. His view is that passion often emerges after repeated practice, growing competence, and earned momentum. That is an important correction for people who are waiting to feel perfectly certain before going deep on something. The episode suggests that devotion is often built, not discovered in a flash.
- **[[Rich Owner vs Poor Owner Mindset]]**: Chris contrasts a more expansive owner mindset with a fear-driven one. The poor-owner mindset clings to comfort, negotiates from panic, and optimizes for short-term relief. The richer mindset thinks in assets, leverage, positioning, and long-term upside, even when that requires discomfort in the present. This is not only about having money; it is about making decisions from a different internal frame.
- **[[Money Conversations Reveal the Person]]**: One reason the episode lands so well is that money becomes a diagnostic tool for character. Pricing conversations expose shame, ego, self-respect, fear of rejection, need for approval, generosity, and boundary strength. Chris and Codie are not really just talking about fees; they are talking about the human being underneath the fee. That is why the note should be read as both business advice and personal development material.

## Coach's Corner

**On Pricing as a Founder Psychology Problem:**

This is the part that matters most for you. Early-stage founders often tell themselves a noble-sounding story about being "customer friendly" when they are really just afraid of rejection. Cheap pricing can feel humble, but in practice it often signals uncertainty. If you do not believe your product materially changes a customer's situation, you will keep pricing it like a nice-to-have. The fix is not only a pricing spreadsheet. It is building a stronger conviction about the cost of the problem you solve and the value of solving it well.

**On Sales Energy and Founder Neediness:**

The "with or without you" idea is worth holding onto because it protects your judgment. Neediness makes founders over-customize, over-promise, discount too quickly, and accept bad-fit customers who create support drag later. Calm detachment improves almost everything: qualification, positioning, negotiation, and follow-through. You do not need indifference, but you do need enough emotional slack that one prospect cannot hijack your sense of worth.

**On Positioning Before Promotion:**

A lot of SaaS founders think they have a marketing problem because traffic is weak or conversions are mediocre. Chris's framing is sharper: often the real issue is that the offer is not positioned distinctly enough to deserve attention. If your product sounds like a softer, smaller clone of ten alternatives, then more promotion just scales ambiguity. Before pushing harder on distribution, get painfully clear about who the product is for, what expensive pain it removes, and why your framing is more precise than the generic category description.

**On Building a Premium Brand Without Pretending:**

There is a useful nuance here. Premium does not mean fake luxury aesthetics or inflated language. It means coherence: sharper taste, better promises, stronger proof, clearer exclusions, and the confidence to not serve everyone. A founder brand gets stronger when the product, copy, pricing, onboarding, and customer experience all tell the same story. If you want to charge more, the whole system has to feel more intentional.

**On Mastery and Long-Term Identity:**

Chris's point about mastery is a good antidote to restless founder energy. You do not need to wait for some mystical certainty before committing harder to a craft. Depth creates desire. The more competent you become at solving a real problem, the more your ambition has something solid to attach to. That matters in SaaS, because durable confidence usually comes from repeated wins in a domain, not from motivational content.

## Action Items

- [ ] **Rewrite your positioning in one sentence**: Define exactly who your SaaS is for, what painful outcome it prevents, and why that matters financially
- [ ] **Audit your discount reflexes**: Review the last few sales or pricing conversations and note where you lowered price because of fear rather than evidence
- [ ] **Create a premium version of the offer**: Design a higher-tier package or service layer that reflects deeper access, faster implementation, or stronger outcomes
- [ ] **Tighten your brand signals**: Check whether your landing page, onboarding, pricing, and product copy all support the same high-conviction story
- [ ] **Study better negotiation framing**: Practice asking discovery questions that uncover cost of inaction, budget flexibility, and desired business outcomes
