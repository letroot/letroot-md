# What They Don't Teach in Business School about Entrepreneurship

## Summary
A [[Stanford Graduate School of Business]] panel from the 2010 Conference on Entrepreneurship about what founders learn in the trenches that business school cannot fully simulate. [[Mike Dever]], [[Nazila Alasti]], and [[Will Hummer]] compare different routes into startups: founding from scratch, leaving big-company structure, moving from [[Venture Capital]] into operating, and joining a company after technical founders have already started it. The panel keeps returning to a practical claim: entrepreneurship is not a clean application of classroom frameworks, but a live test of [[Sales]], speed, team quality, investor communication, self-knowledge, and judgment under uncertainty. The strongest lessons are less about having the perfect idea and more about building the kind of operating system that can learn, sell, adapt, and keep moving.

## Key Insights
* **Entrepreneurship is a full-person test**: [[Will Hummer]] describes founding as a "full wafer test" because it tests passion, endurance, perseverance, leadership, sales ability, and emotional range at once. [[Venture Capital]] lets you form opinions from the outside, but operating a company forces you to live with incomplete information and still move.
* **Process beats heroic certainty**: Hummer's biggest surprise was learning that it is okay not to have all the answers. A founder must shift from investment-thesis thinking to [[Process Thinking]]: run the plays, watch the market, absorb data, and trust a disciplined process to move toward the answer.
* **Control is a real founder motive**: [[Nazila Alasti]] left big-company life because she wanted creative freedom and wanted her decisions to matter. [[Mike Dever]] tells the [[Hughes Aircraft]] solar race car story: after helping build a car that won across Australia, he was rewarded with a routine raise. That moment clarified his desire to control his own fate.
* **The Sunday night test is a career diagnostic**: Dever's test is simple: notice how you feel at 9 p.m. on Sunday. If you feel alive about Monday's work, stay. If dread returns every week, it may be time to change your operating context.
* **Small teams with high morale can survive wrong ideas**: Dever says three of his four companies had to abandon the original idea. The surprise was that the original idea mattered less than the quality, morale, and adaptability of the team. Chuck Holloway calls this the art of small-boat sailing: startups rarely move in a straight line.
* **Partners are not optional decoration**: Alasti learned the hard way that founder partners matter. Business school may tell you what you are good at, but a startup punishes what you lack. The right partner should compensate for your negative space, not merely validate your existing strengths.
* **Course changes require investor management**: Hummer argues that investors are thesis-driven. If you raise money on one story and learn that the market demands another, you must bring investors into the learning process: show the data, explain the signals, and make them part of the solution rather than surprised critics.
* **Listen to investors without becoming reactive**: CEOs who ignore investors can lose trust, but CEOs who obey every opinion lose the company. The skill is to respect strong opinions, make investors feel heard, and still make the call from the operating truth of the business.
* **Speed is an operating weapon**: Dever's strongest lesson is speed. He compresses business development deals, fundraising, hiring, and product launches because delay creates doubt, second thoughts, and dead momentum. He claims the probability of a business development deal closing drops sharply every day it remains open.
* **Fundraising speed is prepared before the pitch**: Dever's same-day term sheet story was not magic. He walked into the meeting with customer evidence, an "if-then" commitment, a hiring plan, a build plan, and competitive pressure among investors. Speed comes from preparation, not improvisation.
* **Persistence is not passive waiting**: Alasti's version of persistence is not simply refusing to quit. If you still have passion and are still producing good ideas, keep working the process, even without a term sheet. Do not let other people derail you before the evidence says you are done.
* **Know whether you need a complementary operator**: Alasti's point about needing a different kind of partner is a serious founder lesson. If you are a builder who undersells, find someone who can confidently package, sell, and defend the work. If you are the seller, find someone who can build and ground you.
* **Risk is relative**: Hummer argues that "safe" corporate careers are not actually safe. Many classmates who chose consulting, banking, or large-company paths were still laid off. If risk is unavoidable, entrepreneurship can at least put more of the risk under your own agency.
* **Organizations are not as rational as school suggests**: Business school can overteach rational decision-making. In actual companies, decisions are often shaped by politics, affiliation, status, and communication failures. Founders need to understand [[Organizational Behavior]] as lived reality, not just as a class.
* **Sales is not beneath strategy**: The panel is blunt that business schools underrate [[Sales]] and [[Sales Management]]. CEOs sell constantly: to customers, investors, recruits, partners, and employees. Dever's early phone-sales story shows that sales skill improves through reps, experiments, and asking directly for commitment.
* **Do the first sales yourself before hiring specialists**: Hummer cites [[Mark Leslie]]'s idea of cracking the sales code personally. Use flexible generalists early, learn what messaging and process actually closes, then hire execution specialists once the process is repeatable.
* **Know what you are maximizing**: Alasti pushes against the reflex that bigger is always better. At [[Jooners]], charging for subscriptions meant she was not maximizing raw registrations. She was maximizing proof that the product solved a painful enough problem for people to pay.
* **Big companies can teach scale**: Alasti says she once dismissed [[Apple]] as slow and bureaucratic, but later realized big companies teach lessons about shipping, organizational design, decision processes, and scale. Hummer says [[Morgan Stanley]] taught him excellence in work product, execution under pressure, and the gap between good and great.
* **Bias needs both conviction and reflection**: In the Q&A, Alasti says founders need belief because nobody else will believe if they do not, but they also need space to sit, reflect, and let contrary data settle. Hummer adds that authentic conversations with trusted people help founders process fear without spreading panic.
* **Engineers can learn sales through support**: Alasti gives a practical path: sit on help desk, answer customer problems, learn to shift customer perception, then shadow sales calls as the technical expert. Support is often the first sales classroom for technical founders.
* **Macro conditions are not the main excuse**: Late in the panel, Hummer notes the rise of capital-efficient startups, [[Amazon EC2]], [[Amazon S3]], [[Y Combinator]], and investors who can make smaller exits work. Dever closes by saying founders should mostly ignore macro conditions and build, because bad markets also bring easier hiring, cheaper space, and weaker competition.

## Coach's Corner
**On Becoming a Real Operator:**
This episode is valuable because it strips entrepreneurship of the romance. The panelists are not saying "have vision and work hard" in the abstract. They are saying that a founder must become a practical operator: someone who can sell, recruit, interpret market signals, manage investors, ship quickly, handle uncertainty, and learn from uncomfortable feedback. For your own SaaS work, the challenge is not only to become better at writing or building. The challenge is to make the whole machine tighter: idea, customer pain, sales motion, support loop, hiring standard, and investor narrative.

**On Process Over Mood:**
The Hummer point about process is especially important for you as a young founder. You cannot let confidence be the only fuel. Confidence rises and falls. A process compounds: customer calls, weekly shipping, written learnings, small experiments, follow-ups, support conversations, and sober review. When you do not know the answer, the move is not to wait until you feel certain. The move is to run a process that makes reality speak faster.

**On Speed Without Sloppiness:**
Mike Dever's speed lesson is not hustle theater. It is about reducing the time between interest and commitment. If a customer is warm, get the next call booked while the energy is alive. If a hire is strong, check references quickly and make the offer before the market cools. If a feature can test a pricing assumption, ship the smallest honest version and learn. But notice the deeper requirement: speed depends on preparation. Dever could get same-day investor action because he already had customer evidence, hiring plans, build plans, and a competitive fundraising process.

**On Sales as Founder Training:**
Do not outsource sales too early. For a SaaS founder, the first sales calls are not only revenue attempts; they are product research, copywriting research, positioning research, and confidence training. If you cannot personally get someone from vague interest to a concrete next step, hiring a salesperson will mostly add cost and confusion. Your early job is to find the words, objections, proof, and triggers that make the product legible. Once that pattern repeats, then you can hand it to someone else.

**On Technical Founder Sales Practice:**
The help-desk advice is underrated. If you are not yet comfortable "selling," start by supporting users. Answer their confused emails. Watch where they get stuck. Explain the product in plain language. Notice which explanation changes their posture. That is sales without the psychological pressure of asking for money. Over time, move closer to the money: join customer calls, ask discovery questions, handle objections, and request the next commitment.

**On Choosing Partners:**
Alasti's partner lesson should make you audit your own negative space. Do not only ask, "Who likes my idea?" Ask, "Who sees what I do not see? Who can do the thing I avoid? Who can stay steady when I get intense? Who will challenge me without poisoning momentum?" The wrong partner duplicates your strengths and leaves the same holes open. The right partner expands the company's range of action.

**On Investor Updates:**
The investor management section maps directly onto founder maturity. Investors do not need theater. They need a coherent learning loop: what we believed, what the market showed us, what changed, what we are doing next, and what help we need. If you wait until the board meeting to reveal that the original thesis is failing, you create distrust. If you narrate the learning process as it happens, you give serious people a way to stay aligned.

**On What You Are Maximizing:**
Alasti's subscription point is a clean SaaS question: are you optimizing for users, revenue, retention, learning, credibility, or optionality? A free product can produce vanity adoption and hide weak demand. A paid product creates friction, but it also exposes whether the pain is real. You should know which metric is currently allowed to win and which metric you are deliberately sacrificing.

**On Being a Young Man Developing:**
The career lesson here is agency plus humility. Agency says: if the path makes you dead every Sunday night, take responsibility for changing it. Humility says: you still need partners, reps, sales practice, reflection, and exposure to people who operate better than you. Do not confuse ambition with readiness. Build readiness through contact with reality.

## Action Items
* [ ] **Write the Sunday night diagnosis**: Describe how you feel at 9 p.m. on Sunday about your current work, then write what that feeling is telling you.
* [ ] **Create a founder process scorecard**: Track weekly customer conversations, shipped experiments, follow-ups, support issues, sales calls, and written learnings.
* [ ] **Do five direct sales reps**: Ask five real prospects for a concrete commitment: a paid pilot, a follow-up call, a referral, or a clear no.
* [ ] **Map your negative space**: List your three weakest founder functions and the type of partner, hire, advisor, or habit that would compensate for each.
* [ ] **Rewrite your investor update**: Use the structure: what we believed, what we learned, what changed, what we are doing now, where we need help.
* [ ] **Cut one cycle time in half**: Pick one slow loop in the business, such as follow-up, product release, customer research, or hiring, and redesign it for speed.
* [ ] **Sit on your own help desk**: Spend one focused block answering user/customer issues and note the phrases that actually shift perception.
* [ ] **Define what you are maximizing this month**: Choose one primary metric and write the tradeoff you are willing to accept to maximize it.
