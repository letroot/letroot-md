# Lecture 1 - How to Start a Startup (Sam Altman, Dustin Moskovitz)

## Summary

[[Sam Altman]] opens [[Y Combinator]]'s [[How to Start a Startup]] course by laying out the four controllable pillars of startup success: [[Startup Ideas]], [[Product]], [[Team]], and [[Execution]]. He argues that startups are not normal companies, and that advice for big companies often fails in the high-growth, high-uncertainty environment of an early startup. The first half of the lecture focuses on ideas and products: founders need a market that can grow, a mission that can sustain them for a decade, and a product that a small number of users truly love.

[[Dustin Moskovitz]] then explains why to start a startup, mostly by dismantling the bad reasons people give: glamour, being the boss, flexibility, and automatically making more money or impact. His argument is sober: startups are stressful, psychologically demanding, and long commitments. The best reason to start one is that you cannot not do it: you are deeply compelled by the problem, the world needs the solution, and you are unusually well-suited to build it.

The lecture is especially useful because it balances ambition with warning. It does not say “startups are easy if you follow the formula.” It says the opposite: they are painful and risky, but if you pick a problem you love, build something users love, stay close to customers, and keep your psychology intact, you can increase the odds on the parts you control.

## Key Insights

* **[[Startups]] are not normal companies**: Altman warns that the course is aimed at companies pursuing [[Hypergrowth]] and eventually very large outcomes. Much of the advice will not work in big companies or ordinary small businesses because startups operate under different constraints: speed, uncertainty, extreme focus, and the need to find compounding growth.

* **The startup outcome is multiplicative**: Altman frames startup success as roughly idea x product x execution x team x luck. Luck can dominate, but the founder's job is to maximize the controllable inputs. Because the formula is multiplicative, a near-zero score in one category can wreck the whole company.

* **Being poor and unknown can be an advantage**: In normal careers, lack of resources and status can be a disadvantage. In startups, they can create freedom. Unknown founders can move in markets incumbents ignore, take unpopular bets, and avoid the social pressure that makes bigger players conservative.

* **You should not start a startup just to start one**: Altman is blunt that there are easier ways to become rich. Startups are too painful to survive on status-seeking alone. The specific passion should come first, and the company should be the vehicle for solving that problem.

* **Ideas matter more than the startup culture often admits**: Altman pushes back against the “ideas don't matter, execution is everything” cliché. Execution is much harder and more important than people think, but great execution toward a terrible idea still usually fails. Most great companies begin with a strong idea, not with random pivoting.

* **A startup idea includes more than the product**: The idea includes [[Market Size]], market growth, the company's growth strategy, defensibility, and why the company can eventually become difficult to replicate. A founder who only thinks about features is not really evaluating the idea.

* **Planning is useful even when plans change**: Altman repeats the classic line that plans may be worthless but planning is valuable. Long-term thinking is rare in startups, so founders who think seriously about how a market, product, and company could evolve get an edge.

* **Choose the idea you cannot stop thinking about**: If a founder has several ideas, Altman's test is to work on the one that keeps returning when he is not trying to think about work. Durable obsession matters because the company may take ten years.

* **Mission-oriented companies are stronger**: A mission helps the founder endure pain, helps the team stay focused, and makes outsiders more willing to help. Derivative companies do not excite employees, investors, customers, or the founder deeply enough to sustain the required effort.

* **It can be easier to start a hard company than an easy one**: Hard, important projects attract better people and more support. Easy, derivative ideas often sound safer, but they may be harder to recruit for and easier to ignore.

* **Great ideas often look bad at first**: Google as the thirteenth search engine, Facebook as a college-only social network, or Airbnb as strangers sleeping on couches all sounded bad early. If an idea looks obviously good to everyone, the market may already be crowded.

* **The target is “unpopular but right”**: Founders need conviction in their own beliefs and willingness to ignore naysayers. But Altman also warns that the line between right and crazy is thin. The ideal is an idea that sounds bad, but has a specific reason it is actually good.

* **Start with a small market you can own**: The first version does not need to sound huge. A strong startup can start by serving a small subset of users extremely well, winning all of them, then expanding. This connects directly to [[Peter Thiel]]'s later argument about monopoly.

* **Current market size matters less than future market growth**: Altman prefers a small but rapidly growing market to a large but slow-growing market. Founders should ask whether the market will be big in ten years, not just whether it is big today.

* **The market is the hardest thing to change**: You can change almost everything about a startup except whether the market wants to exist. A founder should be honest about whether he is surfing a real wave, stepping into an up elevator, or trying to manufacture demand from nothing.

* **“Why now?” is a critical question**: Altman cites [[Sequoia Capital]]'s question: why is this the perfect time for this company? Why could it not be done two years ago, and why will two years from now be too late? Strong startups usually have a crisp answer.

* **Build something you personally need if possible**: If the founder is the user, he starts with better intuition. If he is building for someone else, he must compensate by getting extremely close to customers, even working in their office or speaking with them multiple times a day.

* **Good ideas are usually easy to explain**: If the idea takes more than a sentence to explain, it may be too complicated. The best ideas are often clearly different from existing companies in one important way, or entirely new.

* **Think market-first, not product-first**: Altman closes the ideas section by emphasizing customer demand. Many founders, especially students, fail because they do not think about the market first and what people actually want.

* **Until you build a great product, nothing else matters**: Fundraising, press, hiring, conferences, advisors, and partnerships all become easier when the product is great. Before then, they are usually distractions.

* **Build something users love, not merely like**: Altman's central product advice is to build something a small number of users love rather than something many users mildly like. It is easier to expand from deep love in a narrow group than from broad indifference.

* **Word of mouth is evidence of love**: When people really love a product, they tell friends and coworkers about it. Organic growth in both consumer and enterprise products is one of the strongest signs that the product is working.

* **Do not build a growth machine before love exists**: Sales and marketing matter, but trying to force growth before people love the product wastes time. Breakout companies usually have products so good that users naturally spread them.

* **Most startups die from failing to make something users love**: Altman says few startups die because of competition. Most die because they spend time on other things instead of building something users deeply want.

* **Start with something simple**: Simplicity increases the chance of greatness. A startup should begin with the smallest surface area possible so it can do one thing extremely well. Google, Facebook, and the iPhone all began with unusually simple user experiences.

* **Founders need product fanaticism**: Altman uses the word “fanatical” for how great founders care about details, customer support, copy, and product quality. Great founders feel physical pain when the product is bad and fix problems quickly.

* **Recruit early users manually**: Founders should not buy ads to get initial users. They should recruit the few people who are likely to care, talk to them constantly, and use their feedback to build love.

* **Do things that do not scale**: Altman uses [[Ben Silbermann]] and [[Pinterest]] as an example of manually recruiting early users. Early-stage founders should not hide behind scalable systems before they understand who loves the product and why.

* **Build a feedback engine**: The company should transform user feedback into product decisions, ship improvements, and repeat. Ask users what they like, dislike, would pay for, would recommend, and whether they would be disappointed if the company disappeared.

* **Tight feedback loops compound**: If the product improves 10% every week, the gains compound quickly. Software startups can make feedback loops extremely short, sometimes measured in hours.

* **Founders should stay directly connected to users**: Great founders do sales and customer support themselves early. Hiring people to stand between founders and users too early weakens the learning loop.

* **Measure what matters**: The company builds what the CEO measures. Vanity metrics like total registrations should give way to [[Active Users]], activity levels, [[Cohort Retention]], revenue, and [[Net Promoter Score]]. Growth is the indicator of a great product.

* **Entrepreneurship is not glamorous**: Moskovitz says the media often romanticizes startups as parties, brilliant insights, and status. The real experience is mostly hard work: customer support, sales, engineering problems, and long stretches of focused execution.

* **Startups create serious psychological stress**: The founder carries responsibility for employees, investors, customers, and the opportunity cost of everyone who follows him. The founder is always on call and must manage pressure deliberately.

* **The CEO's first job is managing his own psychology**: Moskovitz quotes [[Ben Horowitz]] on the CEO's core responsibility. Founder psychology is not a soft topic; it is operational infrastructure.

* **Being the boss is mostly an illusion**: Moskovitz cites [[Phil Libin]]: everyone becomes your boss—employees, customers, partners, users, media, and investors. Entrepreneurship is not the right path if the main desire is authority over people.

* **Founder flexibility means working any 24 hours a day**: The founder may technically control the calendar, but the company controls the founder. Important problems override weekends, vacations, and personal preference.

* **The founder is a role model for company intensity**: If the founder takes his foot off the gas, the team often does too. This does not mean founders should destroy their health, but it does mean energy and seriousness cascade from the top.

* **Joining a great later-stage company can create more impact than founding**: Moskovitz argues that impact and financial reward are not automatic reasons to start a company. Employees at companies like [[Facebook]], [[Dropbox]], or [[Google]] can have massive impact because they inherit distribution, infrastructure, and teams.

* **The best reason is “you cannot not do it”**: Moskovitz's preferred reason has two parts: the founder is so passionate that he has to work on the problem, and the world needs this specific solution. If either is missing, joining another company may be the better path.

* **Founder-market fit matters**: The world needing the idea is not enough. The founder should be unusually suited to solve the problem. If another team is much better suited, forcing yourself into the opportunity may be suboptimal for both you and the market.

* **[[Asana]] came from unavoidable internal pain**: Moskovitz and [[Justin Rosenstein]] worked on Asana-like internal task tools at [[Facebook]] because the problem kept pulling them back. The idea felt like it was forcing itself into the world. That is the level of compulsion he recommends looking for.

## Coach's Corner

**On Choosing a Problem You Can Survive:**
This lecture should slow you down in a good way. As an early-stage SaaS founder, there is always pressure to move fast, ship something, and “just validate.” That matters, but Altman's warning is important: if the underlying problem is not one you can love for years, speed will not save you. The boring, painful, repetitive parts of building will expose whether the mission is real.

Before committing to a product direction, ask yourself whether the problem still interests you when nobody is praising you for working on it. Do you keep noticing it in the world? Do you find yourself reading about it unprompted? Do you feel annoyed that the current solutions are bad? That kind of persistent irritation is often a better signal than excitement.

**On Building From a Narrow Wedge:**
Do not make the first version sound huge just to impress people. A vague big idea is usually weaker than a narrow wedge with obvious pain. For your SaaS work, the question is: who is the smallest group of users you can make genuinely delighted? Not “SMBs.” Not “founders.” Not “operations teams.” Which specific operators, in what context, with what urgent workflow?

If you can win a tiny market completely, the note becomes strategic. You can then ask what adjacent group has the same pain, what expansion path exists, and what data, workflow, or distribution advantage compounds from the first niche. That is how small becomes big.

**On Product Love vs Product Politeness:**
Altman's “small number of users love you” line is a useful mirror. Many founders mistake polite interest for product love. A user saying “this is cool” is not enough. Love looks like repeated use, impatience when the product breaks, referrals, detailed feedback, willingness to pay, and sadness if the product disappears.

For your product, design early tests around intensity, not vanity. Instead of asking “would you use this?” ask “what would you stop using if this worked?” “Would you be upset if I shut this down?” “Who else has this problem?” “Would you introduce me to them?” The strength of the reaction matters more than the number of lukewarm reactions.

**On Staying Close to Customers:**
Do not outsource the learning loop too early. If you are the founder, customer support, demos, onboarding, and sales calls are not beneath you. They are where the company tells you what it wants to become. Every confused user is a product lesson. Every repeated objection is copywriting guidance. Every workaround is roadmap intelligence.

This is especially important if you are building for a customer unlike yourself. If you are not the user, proximity has to replace intuition. Spend enough time with them that their workflow becomes emotionally familiar.

**On Avoiding Premature Growth Theatre:**
It is easy to want press, content, partnerships, advisors, and launch announcements because they feel like progress. Altman is direct: none of that matters until users love the product. Growth tactics before product love can create motion without truth.

A better early operating rhythm is brutally simple: talk to users, improve product, measure usage, repeat. If the product gets materially better every week, compounding begins. If not, marketing will only reveal the weakness faster.

**On Founder Psychology:**
Moskovitz's section is especially important. The founder role is not just strategy and product taste. It is emotional load-bearing. People will bring you uncertainty, conflict, fear, and disappointment. You will be responsible for decisions where every option has a cost. If you do not manage your own mind, the company will inherit your chaos.

This means sleep, exercise, focus, journaling, prayer, therapy, solitude, or whatever keeps you sane are not luxuries. They are part of the job. A founder who burns himself out becomes a bottleneck, even if he is talented.

**On Authority vs Responsibility:**
If part of you wants to be a founder because you want freedom, this lecture corrects that. You do not get fewer bosses; you get more. Customers, employees, investors, partners, and the market all become your bosses. The reward is not freedom from responsibility. The reward is the chance to take responsibility for a problem you care about.

That is a healthier frame. Do not ask, “How do I become my own boss?” Ask, “What problem am I willing to become responsible for?”

**On Whether You Should Start This Company:**
The strongest test from Moskovitz is “you cannot not do it.” For your current ideas, write two columns. First: why I cannot stop thinking about this. Second: why the world needs this from me specifically. If either column is thin, that does not mean you should quit. It means you should keep searching, narrow the market, talk to customers, or find a sharper wedge.

The goal is not to romanticize obsession. The goal is to find a problem with enough pull that discipline becomes easier. When the idea is right, you still need effort, but the work has gravity.

## Action Items

* [ ] **Write your “cannot not do it” test**: Explain why this problem keeps pulling you back and why you are unusually suited to solve it.
* [ ] **Define your smallest lovable market**: Name the narrow user group you can make love the product before expanding.
* [ ] **Answer “why now?”**: Write why this company could not have worked two years ago and why two years from now may be too late.
* [ ] **Map your market tailwind**: Identify the external change making this market grow quickly.
* [ ] **Replace vanity metrics**: Choose the few metrics that reveal product love: active use, retention, referrals, revenue, or NPS.
* [ ] **Recruit five users manually**: Do not buy ads. Find five specific users, onboard them yourself, and talk to them repeatedly.
* [ ] **Ask the product-love questions**: Ask whether users would be disappointed if the product disappeared and what would make them recommend it.
* [ ] **Simplify the first version**: Cut the product to the smallest surface area that can still create deep user love.
* [ ] **Create a weekly feedback loop**: Set a rhythm for user conversations, product decisions, shipping, and measuring impact.
* [ ] **Build a founder psychology system**: Decide how you will protect sleep, health, reflection, and emotional steadiness while building.
* [ ] **Audit your founder motive**: Separate status, autonomy, money, and impact from the deeper reason you want to build this company.
