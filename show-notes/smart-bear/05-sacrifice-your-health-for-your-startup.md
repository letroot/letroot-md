# Sacrifice your health for your startup

## Summary

Jason Cohen makes an intentionally uncomfortable argument: maximizing the odds of startup success often requires real sacrifice, including health, time, and family bandwidth. He distinguishes this from generic productivity advice by saying the issue is not merely poor time management but the sheer total volume and obsession required to get a small company off the ground. The piece is best read not as a universal prescription or moral ideal, but as a blunt description of the cost structure of ambitious startup building, especially in the bootstrap phase.

## Key Insights

- **Early-stage startups tend to consume all available time.** Cohen's "Rule of Time in Startups" is that a bootstrapped company takes all of it. There are simply too many essential functions for a tiny team to do without spillover into the rest of life.
- **[[Obsession]] matters more than passion.** Passion is too soft a word for the level of mental occupation startup building often demands. The deeper reality is obsessive commitment: financial, emotional, and cognitive bandwidth all tied up in the company.
- **Productivity advice does not remove total workload.** Better systems, prioritization, and sleep can improve output, but they do not change the fact that the startup may still ask more of you than a balanced life can comfortably give.
- **Sacrifice is contextual, not universally wise.** Cohen frames this as something he did in a stage of life where the tradeoff was more acceptable: younger, fewer responsibilities, some savings, and a spouse who understood the load. That context matters.
- **There is a difference between acknowledging cost and glamorizing dysfunction.** The article's value is in honesty about tradeoffs, not in pretending burnout is noble. If you choose the path, choose it consciously.
- **Financial freedom changes the equation.** Cohen's claim is specifically about the pre-freedom grind. The point is not that life should always orbit work, but that some founders temporarily put work before self until the company is stable.

## Coach's Corner

**On Reading This Without Getting Trapped by It:**

This article is useful if you read it as diagnosis, not dogma. Startups are demanding enough that "balance" can become a comforting lie when what you actually need is honesty about season, cost, and tradeoff. But the danger is taking that honesty and turning it into an identity performance where suffering itself feels like progress. It isn't.

**On Sustainable Ambition:**

The real question is not "Should I destroy myself for the company?" The real question is "What level of sacrifice is justified for this stage, and for how long?" Temporary overreach can be rational. Permanent degradation is usually just strategic stupidity wearing a heroic costume.

**On Your Life Design as a Founder:**

Because you're building as a real person with a future beyond one company, you need deliberate boundaries even during intense seasons. Maybe the answer is not balance every day, but non-negotiables: sleep floor, protected relationships, exercise minimums, recovery windows, or a cap on how long a sprint can last before reassessment.

**On Obsession vs Clarity:**

Obsession can help you endure the grind, but it can also narrow your judgment. The founder move is not just to work hard, but to make sure the hard work is pointed at reality: customers, retention, distribution, cash, and product truth. Exhaustion without strategic clarity is just expensive self-harm.

## Action Items

- [ ] **Define your acceptable sacrifice window**: Decide what kind of intense season is acceptable right now and what your stop conditions are.
- [ ] **Set non-negotiable floors**: Choose minimum standards for sleep, health, or relationships that you will not casually trade away.
- [ ] **Audit where exhaustion is helping versus hurting**: Identify one area where pushing harder is useful and one where fatigue is degrading judgment.
- [ ] **Review your founder workload**: List the functions consuming your bandwidth and decide which are essential, delegated, automated, or deferred.
