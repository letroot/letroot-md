# Double your productivity without more work or stress

## Summary
Jason Cohen argues that the popular idea of getting 1% better every day until you become 37x more productive is mathematically cute but practically false. His central point is that productivity is usually dragged down not by a lack of peak effort, but by too much low-velocity work spread across the day. Using a driving-speed analogy, he shows that periods of slow movement are hard to offset later, even with bursts of speed. The practical solution is not to work harder, but to identify and eliminate the activities that quietly average your day down.

## Key Insights
* **The 1 Percent Compounding Myth**: [[Compounding]]. The article pushes back on the motivational cliché that small daily gains automatically compound into absurd productivity multiples. While the math works in isolation, real human work does not improve in a smooth compounding curve forever because constraints, fatigue, and low-value work interfere.
* **Productivity Behaves Like Average Velocity**: [[Productivity]], [[Average Velocity]]. Cohen uses a road-trip example to show that if the first half of your trip is too slow, the second half cannot realistically save the average. This maps cleanly to work: long stretches of slow, distracted, low-leverage activity wipe out the gains from short periods of sharp focus.
* **Low Velocity Work Is the Real Productivity Killer**: [[Attention Management]], [[Time Management]]. The biggest enemy is not a lack of heroic effort but the accumulation of email, unnecessary meetings, app management, context switching, and casual drift. These activities feel normal because they are socially accepted, but they silently absorb the hours that matter most.
* **Elimination Beats Optimization**: [[Opportunity Cost]], [[Leverage]]. Learning to type faster or work slightly quicker can help, but removing useless inputs has a much larger effect. Cutting waste often produces more real output than becoming marginally better at processing waste.
* **Measurement Creates Awareness**: [[Feedback Loops]], [[Self Awareness]]. Tools like [[RescueTime]] help surface where attention is actually going. The article shows that once time usage becomes visible, bad patterns become harder to rationalize and easier to change.
* **The Eight Hour Workday Is Often an Illusion**: [[Deep Work]], [[Meetings]]. Being at work for eight hours does not mean eight hours of productive work happened. A large chunk of the day can disappear into coordination overhead, chatter, and administrative maintenance.
* **Big Gains Come From Protecting the Right Work**: [[Big Rocks]], [[Prioritization]]. The article ends by tying productivity not just to removing drag, but also to making sure the remaining effort is aimed at the most meaningful tasks. It is possible to become more efficient at the wrong things, so focus must pair with prioritization.

## Coach's Corner
**On Building a Founder Day That Actually Compounds:**
The most useful thing here for you is the distinction between being busy and maintaining high average velocity. A founder can have intense days, packed calendars, and endless tabs open, and still produce very little that moves the company forward. That is dangerous because startup work already contains ambiguity, and ambiguity makes low-velocity activity feel legitimate. You can always tell yourself you were “working” because you answered messages, checked analytics, reviewed tools, or reorganized ideas. But if the day did not produce clearer positioning, better product decisions, more customer understanding, stronger distribution, or shipped improvements, the day probably got averaged down.

**On Removing Friction Instead of Becoming a Better Mule:**
A lot of productivity advice accidentally trains you to become a more efficient pack animal. You get better at carrying noise rather than reducing it. This essay is a useful correction. The real win is not becoming faster at inbox triage or more skillful at surviving chaos. The real win is designing a system where less chaos reaches you in the first place. That might mean fewer notifications, tighter communication windows, a smaller set of recurring commitments, clearer criteria for what deserves your attention, and more aggressive refusal of tasks that feel urgent but are strategically empty.

**On Founder Leverage and Attention Quality:**
Your most valuable work is usually thinking, deciding, writing, designing, and shipping. Those require uninterrupted attention. The problem is that low-velocity work often arrives in fragments and disguises itself as responsibility. Every tiny interruption feels harmless, but the accumulation destroys the kind of cognitive depth required for meaningful output. If you want a 2x jump in real productivity, it probably will not come from squeezing more hours out of yourself. It will come from creating a day shape where your best mental energy lands on your highest-leverage problems before the noise gets a turn.

**On Measuring What Is Actually Happening:**
Most people underestimate how much time leaks into operational sludge. That is why tracking matters. You do not need to become obsessive, but you do need to make invisible drag visible. If a week of measurement shows that a shocking amount of time goes to email, calendar wrangling, passive reading, or context switching, that is not a reason for guilt. It is a diagnostic gift. Founders improve faster when they stop arguing with reality and start instrumenting it.

**On Strategic Brutality With Time:**
There is also a mindset angle here. To get more done without more stress, you must become more ruthless about what does not deserve a place in your day. That includes not just tasks, but ways of working. A startup founder should treat scattered attention as a structural threat, not a personality quirk. You do not need to become robotic. You just need to protect the parts of the day where actual value gets created.

**On the Right Standard for a Good Day:**
A good day is not one where you endured a lot. It is one where the important thing moved. That could mean shipping a feature, clarifying an offer, speaking to a customer, tightening your landing page, or resolving a strategic uncertainty. If you use that standard consistently, low-velocity work becomes easier to spot because it stops feeling impressive.

## Action Items
* [ ] **Track one full workweek**: Use [[RescueTime]] or another tracker to measure where your actual attention is going.
* [ ] **List your top three low-velocity drains**: Identify the recurring activities that consume time without creating meaningful progress.
* [ ] **Create a founder-first block**: Reserve the first high-energy block of the day for shipping, strategy, writing, or customer work before reactive tasks.
* [ ] **Reduce inbox exposure**: Set tighter windows for email and chat instead of letting them fragment the whole day.
* [ ] **Define your big rocks weekly**: Decide the few outputs that would make the week genuinely meaningful, then protect time for them.
