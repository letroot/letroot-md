# Your customers hate MVPs. Make a SLC instead.

## Summary
[[Jason Cohen]] argues that the classic [[MVP]] framing optimizes too hard for founder learning and too little for [[Customer Experience]]. His alternative is [[SLC]]: [[Simple]], [[Lovable]], and [[Complete]]. The idea is to ship something small enough to learn quickly, but scoped and crafted well enough that a real customer can genuinely want it in its current form. He backs the claim with examples like [[Google Docs]], [[WhatsApp]], [[Snapchat]], [[Twitter]], [[Dropbox]], [[Stripe]], [[Twilio]], and [[Slack]], then argues that delight is not a luxury layer added later but a strategic wedge that helps early products win.

## Key Insights
* **MVP is often founder-centric**: [[Minimum Viable Product]] sounds disciplined, but in practice it often becomes permission to ship something embarrassing, incomplete, and frustrating as long as the team learns from it. That may maximize [[Validated Learning]], but it externalizes the cost onto customers.
* **Customers do not want your experiment**: A user does not care that your product is an early test. They care whether it solves a problem well enough to be worth adopting now. Cohen’s point is blunt: customers hate products that feel unfinished, even if the product team calls that strategic.
* **Simple is compatible with complete**: [[SLC]] rejects the false tradeoff that a small v1 must be broken or partial. A product can have narrow scope and still fully accomplish one coherent job. Early [[Google Docs]] is his core example: tiny relative to [[Microsoft Word]], but complete for straightforward collaboration.
* **Lovable is a strategic requirement, not decoration**: Products that do less but are loved can outperform products with more features that people dislike. Love can come from beautiful design, deep empathy for a customer identity, strong brand posture, or a product philosophy that feels unusually respectful and human.
* **Complete means the product stands on its own**: An [[MVP]] that never gets a v2 remains a bad product. An [[SLC]] that never gets another release can still remain a good, modest product that creates lasting value.
* **Many iconic products started as SLCs**: Cohen points to early versions of [[WhatsApp]], [[Snapchat]], [[Twitter]], [[Slack]], [[Stripe]], and [[Twilio]] as products that began with constrained scope but real wholeness. Their first versions did not try to be everything; they tried to be fully satisfying within a narrow lane.
* **Scope narrowly enough to be whole**: The trick is not reducing ambition at the company level. It is reducing scope at the feature level until the first version can honestly feel finished for a specific use case.
* **SLC is not anti-learning**: Cohen keeps the valid parts of [[MVP]] thinking: ship small, get real users, and learn from usage. The correction is that the learning vehicle should still respect the customer.
* **Delight can win before feature depth does**: [[SLC]] breaks the typical product hierarchy assumption that usefulness must be built first and delight added later. Cohen argues that delight can and should be part of day one because it helps customers forgive limitations and creates differentiation that features alone cannot.
* **Identity and meaning matter in product adoption**: Some products create attachment because they reflect who the customer is or wants to be. He cites examples like [[Linear]], [[Basecamp]], [[Moleskine]], [[Patagonia]], and even [[Vim]] vs [[Emacs]] debates to show that products can signal values, culture, or belonging.
* **Utility is easier to optimize than delight**: Teams default to utility because it is easier to define, budget for, and measure. Delight requires taste, insight, and hard choices, which is exactly why it becomes a competitive advantage when done well.
* **SLC improves your future options**: If version one already delivers real value, you gain time and flexibility. You can build v2 carefully, ask customers what matters next, or even leave the product alone if it is already good enough for its niche.
* **Not every product should become complex**: Cohen makes an underappreciated point that maturity does not always mean accreting features. Some products should remain simple forever because simplicity itself is the value proposition.

## Coach's Corner
**On the trap technical founders are vulnerable to:**
The danger in [[MVP]] thinking is that it flatters builders. It lets you say, “This is rough on purpose,” when the harsher truth might be, “I have not yet done the thinking required to make this feel coherent.” As a technical founder, you can always justify shipping one more half-finished flow because you understand the roadmap in your head. But users only experience what is in front of them. They do not buy your intent. They buy the felt experience.

**On what this means for your SaaS product:**
You do not need to launch big. But you do need to launch whole. Pick a problem narrow enough that the first user can complete the job and feel, “Yes, this works. I would use this again.” If they instead feel like they volunteered to beta test your unfinished architecture, you are taxing trust at the very moment you should be earning it.

**On delight as leverage, not fluff:**
A lot of founders hear “lovable” and immediately think that means polished animation or visual garnish. That is too shallow. Lovable can mean clarity. It can mean surprising usefulness. It can mean speaking in the customer’s language. It can mean respecting their time. It can mean a sharp product opinion that makes the right people feel seen. Especially early on, delight is often just evidence that someone cared enough to shape the experience rather than dump features on the page.

**On product scope discipline:**
This piece should push you to ask a harder question than “What is the minimum we can ship?” Ask instead: “What is the smallest version that feels self-respecting?” That framing is better. It forces you to cut surface area until the remaining scope can actually shine. Most weak launches are not too small. They are too broad and too undercooked at the same time.

**On long-term strategy:**
There is a second-order advantage here. If v1 is genuinely complete for a narrow use case, you buy yourself optionality. You can deepen, expand, stay narrow, reposition, or even leave it alone. That is much healthier than launching something brittle that demands immediate follow-up just to stop disappointing people.

**On your founder standard:**
You are trying to build trust as much as software. Every early user interaction teaches the market what kind of company you are. If the first impression is “these guys ship broken things and call it iteration,” that sticks. If the first impression is “this does one thing, but it does it cleanly and thoughtfully,” that compounds in your favor.

## Action Items
* [ ] **Rewrite your current MVP as an SLC**: Define one narrow customer job your product can fully complete today, without leaning on future roadmap promises.
* [ ] **Cut one feature cluster entirely**: Remove anything that expands scope without increasing completeness for the core use case.
* [ ] **List 3 ways your product could become lovable**: Identify delight through UX clarity, product opinion, speed, emotional resonance, onboarding, or brand voice.
* [ ] **Test for completeness, not just usefulness**: Ask whether a new user can accomplish the promised job end-to-end and still feel satisfied if the product never changed again.
* [ ] **Audit where you are hiding behind MVP language**: Find any place you are excusing poor experience as “early stage” instead of fixing the experience.
* [ ] **Pick one meaning signal**: Decide what kind of identity, belonging, pride, or relief your first users should feel when using the product.
