# Using the Needs Stack for competitive strategy

## Summary
[[Jason Cohen]] introduces the [[Needs Stack]], a way to understand that customers rarely want the product they appear to be buying. They want a higher-level outcome, and the product is only one step in the chain of means that leads to that outcome. A customer may seem to want cloud infrastructure, a WordPress site, a personal website, an audience, a book deal, or speaking opportunities, but those are successive layers in a hierarchy of needs. The strategic insight is that a company operating one level higher can make the lower-level product irrelevant—not by competing on the same features, but by helping customers reach their real goal faster.

## Key Insights

* **Customers buy means to reach higher-level ends**: [[Customer Needs]] form a hierarchy. A customer buying AWS infrastructure may actually want a WordPress site, a personal website, a following, a book deal, or a successful speaking career. The visible purchase is only one step in the [[Jobs To Be Done]] chain.

* **The Needs Stack reveals hidden competitors**: [[Competitive Strategy]] should include companies operating at higher levels of the customer’s stack. WP Engine competes with AWS for some customers even though WP Engine buys infrastructure from AWS, because WP Engine removes the need for those customers to buy and manage infrastructure directly.

* **Higher-level products can make lower-level products irrelevant**: When a product fulfills a more important need directly, the customer may never enter the lower-level market. The customer who wants a working WordPress site does not care that WP Engine uses AWS behind the scenes; infrastructure has disappeared from the customer’s problem.

* **Up the stack means speed and convenience**: Products higher in the stack usually help customers reach their end goal faster and with less effort. [[Managed Services]] and vertical products trade away some flexibility and customization in exchange for reduced complexity and faster progress.

* **Down the stack means control and flexibility**: Lower-level products expose more power and customization, but require customers to do more work and understand more complexity. AWS offers more infrastructure control than WP Engine; WordPress offers more customization than Substack. The right choice depends on whether the customer values control or progress.

* **Do not stop at the level your company serves**: Companies naturally define the market around their current product. Cohen argues that this is dangerous because there is always another level above. “Website” may be a means to “build an audience”; “build an audience” may be a means to “become a famous speaker.” Strategy requires asking what the customer is ultimately trying to accomplish.

* **Features and benefits depend on the customer’s current level**: “Sell benefits, not features” is incomplete. Customers do not always want to hear about their highest-level aspiration. Someone actively searching for “how to get a WordPress site” wants concrete features and implementation details, but the product can still connect those features to the next level up: “Become famous online with a customizable WordPress website.”

* **Position your product one level above its mechanism**: A company does not need to compete on its rivals’ strongest terrain. Substack should not try to beat WordPress on customization; it can instead position around building an audience. WP Engine can make infrastructure irrelevant by positioning around a fast, supported WordPress site.

* **Moving up the stack can create more value and more risk**: A higher-level offer may require a different product, brand, business model, expertise, and target market. Selling infrastructure by the gigabyte is fundamentally different from selling websites by the month or taking a percentage of speaking fees. The company may also discover that the next-level outcome is too difficult for software to guarantee.

* **A full pivot is not the only way to climb**: Companies can move partially upward through [[Sub-Brands]], vertical products, add-ons, education, partnerships, customer stories, and better marketing. The existing business can continue while a focused product tests whether a higher-level need is commercially viable.

* **Measure the next level up**: Operational metrics show whether the product is delivering its direct promise. Strategic metrics show whether customers are succeeding at the next level. Shopify can measure store creation, but [[GMV]] better reflects whether customers are succeeding as merchants. The next-level metric may be harder to control, but it can be a stronger predictor of retention and long-term value.

* **The next-level outcome may be outside your control—but still worth measuring**: Shopify cannot guarantee that every store will generate thousands of dollars in sales. It can, however, measure GMV, teach customers about marketing, and build features that improve checkout conversion. A company should help customers progress toward the next level without pretending to control every external factor.

* **The higher stack contains the company’s purpose**: Immediate goals such as activation, conversion, support reduction, and churn reduction are necessary but rarely inspiring. The higher-level customer outcome—building a career, gaining independence, earning respect, or becoming a hero inside an organization—can provide a more meaningful source of [[Purpose]] for the company and its employees.

## Coach's Corner

**On positioning your SaaS above its mechanism:** The technical description of a product is usually much lower in the Needs Stack than the reason anyone pays for it. A dashboard, workflow, integration, automation, or AI feature is a mechanism. The customer is probably trying to make more money, reduce operational anxiety, win credibility, save a team, or become more effective in a role.

That does not mean you should hide the mechanism. It means you should connect it to the outcome one level above. Keep the feature language for people actively evaluating implementation, but let the headline and positioning explain why the implementation matters.

**On discovering your real competitors:** Do not limit competitive research to products with similar interfaces or feature lists. Ask what else could make your customer’s current problem disappear. A service business, spreadsheet, internal hire, consultant, agency, marketplace, or completely different software category may be a stronger competitor than a direct SaaS alternative.

For example, if your product helps a founder manage a process, the real alternative may not be another tool. It may be hiring an operations person, outsourcing the process, tolerating the inefficiency, or changing the workflow entirely. The Needs Stack gives you a way to see those alternatives clearly.

**On moving upward carefully:** The attractive part of moving up the stack is that the customer values the outcome more than the tool. The dangerous part is that higher-level outcomes are often harder to control. A product can help someone build an audience, but it cannot guarantee fame. It can help a store sell more effectively, but it cannot guarantee demand.

For your own product, distinguish between the outcome you directly own and the outcome you influence. Promise the former clearly. Measure and support the latter intelligently. That gives you ambitious positioning without making dishonest guarantees.

**On choosing the right level for an early-stage company:** You do not necessarily need to climb to the highest imaginable aspiration. A small company can win by serving a specific layer extremely well. The useful question is whether the chosen level is valuable, reachable, and differentiated enough for your current capabilities.

A narrow higher-level product may be powerful if you understand one persona deeply. But it may also shrink the market or require capabilities you do not yet have. Start with the layer where you can produce a real result, then add services, education, partnerships, or product features that help customers advance.

**On next-level metrics:** This is probably the most actionable part for a SaaS founder. If your product’s activation metric is merely “the user set up the tool,” ask what success looks like after setup. What business behavior should change? What result would make the customer keep paying even if the interface stopped improving?

That next-level metric might be revenue generated, hours saved, qualified leads handled, projects completed, errors prevented, or customers retained. You may not fully control it, but you should understand it. A product that optimizes only for usage can become very good at generating activity without creating value.

**On building with purpose:** The higher level also gives you better language for recruiting, marketing, and product decisions. “We help businesses manage tasks” is operationally accurate but emotionally thin. “We help small teams operate with the confidence and leverage of a much larger company” gives the work a reason to matter.

Find the version that is true for your product. The point is not to manufacture grandiosity. It is to understand what customer transformation makes the product worth building in the first place.

## Action Items

* [ ] **Map your product’s Needs Stack**: Write the mechanism, immediate job, business outcome, personal outcome, and ultimate aspiration your customer may be pursuing.
* [ ] **Find non-obvious competitors**: Identify alternatives one level above and below your current product, including services, internal processes, and doing nothing.
* [ ] **Rewrite your positioning one level up**: Connect the product’s direct function to the next meaningful customer benefit without making an outcome you cannot control.
* [ ] **Choose a next-level success metric**: Identify the customer result that matters after activation and track it alongside your operational product metrics.
* [ ] **Design one upward-moving feature or service**: Add education, automation, support, partnerships, or a workflow that helps customers progress toward the higher-level outcome.
* [ ] **Write your company’s higher purpose**: Describe the meaningful change your product enables for customers beyond conversion, retention, or feature usage.
