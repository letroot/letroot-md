# Product/Market Fit (PMF): Experience & Data

## Summary
Jason Cohen argues that real [[Product-Market Fit]] is both visceral and measurable. Subjectively, it feels like the business flips from pushing hard for every customer to being pulled forward by demand you can barely keep up with. Numerically, he argues that PMF shows up as a durable jump in growth, strong retention, and enough scale to prove the result is a market phenomenon rather than a tiny-sample illusion. The essay also stresses that churn is the killer metric: if customers keep leaving, you may have acquisition momentum, but you do not have true fit.

## Key Insights
* **PMF Feels Like Pull, Not Push**: [[Product-Market Fit]], [[Demand Pull]]. Cohen’s clearest definition is experiential: if getting customers still feels like a daily grind, fit has not happened yet. When PMF arrives, the company’s problem changes from begging for demand to managing the surge of it.
* **The Operational Pain of PMF Is a Good Sign**: [[Scaling]], [[Customer Support]], [[Hiring]]. PMF does not arrive as a peaceful victory lap. It often creates chaos: support queues expand, onboarding breaks, hiring becomes urgent, and the whole company feels behind at once.
* **PMF Has Three Core Numerical Signals**: [[Growth]], [[Retention]], [[Critical Mass]]. The article defines PMF as a combination of easy growth, strong retention, and meaningful scale. In his framework, growth should step up to a new sustainable pace, cancellation should stay under roughly 3% monthly for B2B or 5% for B2C, and the business should be large enough to rule out tiny-base distortions.
* **Growth Curves Usually Bend, Then Stay Linear**: [[Growth Curves]], [[Linear Growth]]. Across bootstrapped, VC-backed, viral, and media businesses, Cohen argues that PMF often looks like slow linear growth, then a sudden bend upward, then faster linear growth rather than true exponential growth.
* **Funding Does Not Create PMF**: [[Venture Capital]], [[Bootstrapping]]. The essay is useful because it separates growth shape from financing narrative. Whether a company is self-funded or VC-funded, the underlying pattern of fit comes from the product and market relationship, not the capital structure.
* **A Single Big Customer Is Not a Market**: [[Consulting]], [[Market Validation]]. One large customer can validate usefulness for that buyer, but not broad PMF. That is customer fit, not market fit.
* **Churn Is the Hard Ceiling on Growth**: [[Churn]], [[Retention]], [[SaaS Metrics]]. Cohen’s strongest analytical point is that high cancellation eventually defeats acquisition. Even if marketing keeps bringing in new revenue, churn rises with scale in absolute dollars, eventually flattening growth.
* **Acquisition Success Can Hide Product Failure**: [[Customer Behavior]], [[Retention]]. Strong inbound demand can coexist with weak retention. That means the promise, pricing, or positioning might work while the actual product experience does not.
* **Fit Must Be Sustainable, Not Just Exciting**: [[Sustainable Growth]], [[Unit Economics]]. The essay rejects the idea that explosive top-line growth alone proves PMF. If customers leave quickly, the business may have hype or short-lived relevance, but not a durable fit.
* **Slow Growth Does Not Automatically Mean Failure**: [[Founder Psychology]], [[Iteration]]. Not having PMF yet is not the same as being doomed. It means you are still searching, still learning, or possibly building a smaller but still profitable company.

## Coach's Corner
**On Recognizing the Difference Between Friction and Search:**
This essay is especially useful because it helps you distinguish ordinary early-stage difficulty from genuine traction. A lot of founders either panic too early or congratulate themselves too early. If every customer still requires hand-to-hand combat, you are probably still in search mode. That is not shameful. It just means you should think like an investigator, not like someone scaling a proven machine.

**On Why Retention Matters More Than Excitement:**
The sharpest idea here is that PMF is not proven by applause, signups, compliments, or even bursts of growth. It is proven when customers keep choosing the product after the initial novelty wears off. That matters because founders can get emotionally fooled by activation spikes, positive conversations, and a handful of eager early adopters. Retention is harsher, but it tells the truth. If people do not stick, the product is not yet solving something deeply enough.

**On The Founder Trap of Scaling Too Soon:**
One hidden danger is acting like you have PMF before you do. Founders start hiring, broadening the roadmap, adding process, and trying to scale acquisition while the product is still leaky. That usually amplifies confusion, not traction. You do not want to pour fuel into a system that has not yet proven it deserves more fuel. This is where discipline matters: solve for real pull first, then build the machine to serve it.

**On What to Watch in Your Own Product:**
For your own work, I would treat this essay like a scoreboard design guide. Watch where growth is coming from, how hard each customer is to win, what happens after they start using the product, and whether usage naturally deepens or fades. If acquisition is hard and retention is weak, you are not at PMF. If acquisition is getting easier and retention is strengthening, you may be approaching it. If both are strong and operations are starting to strain, that is a high-quality problem.

**On Emotional Readiness for PMF:**
Another underrated point is that PMF often feels worse operationally before it feels better psychologically. Many founders imagine PMF as relief. In reality, it can first feel like drowning in a business that suddenly became real. That means you need to prepare not only to find fit, but to absorb it. Systems, hiring judgment, support quality, and product discipline all start mattering more the moment the market says yes.

**On The Real Job Right Now:**
If you do not yet have PMF, your job is not to look impressive. It is to learn fast enough to find a truth worth scaling. That means tighter feedback loops, better conversations with users, sharper positioning, and more ruthless focus on the smallest thing that produces repeated value. If you do have PMF, your job changes: protect retention, fix bottlenecks, and scale without letting quality collapse.

## Action Items
* [ ] **Define your PMF scoreboard**: Track acquisition ease, retention, churn, and meaningful usage in one simple weekly view.
* [ ] **Audit your churn honestly**: Identify why users leave and separate weak-fit exits from onboarding or support failures.
* [ ] **Test whether growth is push or pull**: Write down how much manual effort each new customer currently requires.
* [ ] **Avoid premature scale moves**: Delay major hiring or growth spending if retention still says the product is leaking.
* [ ] **Interview retained users**: Study the customers who keep coming back to understand the real source of value.
