# 162. Faith for Natural Life — Abridged

## Church Leaders Are Given for Discipleship

In Ephesians 4:11, Paul says that the Lord Jesus gave apostles, prophets, evangelists, and pastor-teachers. These are descriptions of the leadership Jesus gave to his church. The point is not a hierarchy of titles. A pastor, bishop, and elder are terms used of the same work of leadership, and deacons are assistants in that work.

The church is not arranged as a permanent division between clergy and laity. The leaders are to equip the saints; the saints are to grow, assist in the work, and themselves become able to lead. Every believer is a priest before God, offering spiritual sacrifices through Jesus Christ. A leader is not a priest in place of the believer; the leader teaches the believer to function as a priest.

Christ himself is the rock upon which the church is built. Peter was given a real place of leadership among the apostles, but the church is founded on Christ and his redemptive work, not on Peter as its foundation. Christ builds his church, and the gates of Hades do not prevail against it.

Why, then, did Christ give leaders to the church? Ephesians 4:12 gives the answer: for the perfecting, or equipping, of the saints.

## Perfecting Is the Work of Discipleship

The word translated perfecting is *katartismos*. Its verbal form, *katartizo*, is used of being perfectly joined together and of being perfectly trained. In Luke 6:40, Jesus said that a disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher. The work done toward a disciple is therefore the work of perfecting.

This is why the perfecting of the saints in Ephesians 4 is the same work as discipleship. In Matthew 28, Jesus did not merely tell the apostles to pass information on to the nations. He commanded them to make disciples, teaching them to observe all that he commanded. Teaching is the tool of discipleship, but discipleship has a definite end: that the person being taught becomes formed by the truth taught.

Someone may teach without intending to bring anybody to a particular conviction, skill, or way of life. But a disciple-maker teaches toward an outcome. The goal is not only that the person hears the information, but that the person becomes what the teaching is meant to form. That is why the central question to ask of church leadership is whether the saints are being discipled.

The church is not primarily for a brief encouragement service, a religious activity, or even the partaking of communion. Such things may have their place, but the stated work for which Christ gave leaders is the perfecting of the saints. The saints are perfected unto the work of ministry and unto the building up of the body of Christ.

## The Perfect Man Concerns the Mind

Ephesians 4:13 says that we are to come to the unity of the faith, the knowledge of the Son of God, a perfect man, and the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. The first emphasis is knowledge, so the direction of this work is the mind. The perfect man is not the physical body, and it is not the believer's spirit. It is the man of the mind.

At salvation, the believer's spirit is made new. By faith, the Spirit baptizes us into the body of Christ. We are united with Christ in his death, burial, and resurrection. The old man is crucified with him, and the believer becomes a new creation. This is not merely a symbol effected by water or another sacrament; the reality is effected by the Spirit through faith in Christ.

The spirit of the believer is therefore already a complete divine state. First Corinthians 6:17 says that he who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with him. Romans 8 says that the Spirit of God dwells in us and that Christ is in us. Second Peter says that we are partakers of the divine nature, and Colossians says that we are complete in Christ. Nothing needs to be added to the substance, content, or quality of that new nature.

The new creation does not mean that every expression, habit, attitude, and action changes automatically at the moment of salvation. The nature changes completely, but the mind must come into the knowledge and consciousness of that reality. This is why Christ gave the church: not to improve the believer's spirit, but to perfect the believer in the mind.

## Knowledge Must Become Consciousness

The work of the church addresses both knowledge and consciousness. A person may know that he is a new creation and still act contrary to that knowledge. That does not mean the new creation is false; it means knowledge has not become the governing consciousness in that situation.

You do not necessarily do what you merely know. You do what you are conscious of. A believer may know that he is righteous, loved, and born of God, while remaining more conscious of sin, failure, unworthiness, or the experiences of his past. That contrary consciousness will affect conduct even when the correct knowledge is present.

The mind must therefore be renewed. Paul moves from the perfecting work of Ephesians 4 into the instruction that believers should no longer walk as the Gentiles walk in the futility of their minds. The intended result of the church's work is a different walk. The mind directs the order of life; when it is governed by the truth of the new creation, it directs the believer in the path of purpose rather than in the patterns of the flesh.

Every person has a personal history and has been affected by sin and death in distinct ways. But the answer is not different in kind for one person and another. The individual expression may be unique, but the fundamental problem is the same, and the answer is the same: the new creation in Christ. We must take responsibility for what we agree with and what we allow to become consciousness on our minds.

## Reckoning Is the Faith Stand

James says to receive the implanted word, which is able to save the soul, and then says to be doers of the word, not hearers only, deceiving yourselves. The language of deceiving is connected with reckoning. The person who hears the word but does not do it is misreckoning himself: he is taking account of something other than the truth of who he is.

Reckoning is not inventing a reality. It is taking the reality God has already established into account. The believer does not become a new creation by reckoning; he reckons because he is already a new creation. He does not create the divine nature by affirming it; he brings his mind into conscious agreement with the divine nature he has received.

This is the faith stand. It is not the mere repetition of phrases. A person can say, "I am a new creation," without attention and simply be reciting words. The faith stand is attentive affirmation: the will gives attention to the truth, agrees with it, and holds it before the mind until it becomes consciousness. The purpose is that the truth already known should govern the walk.

There are therefore two aspects of faith. The first is agreement with what God has revealed to be true. The second is the consciousness of that truth, by which it becomes active in the person's conduct. The first establishes what is believed; the second is the reckoning by which the believer walks in the light of it.

## Faith Has Natural Effects

Faith is principally faith in God, his redemption, his purpose, and the believer's identity in Christ. It is not fundamentally a pursuit of natural possessions or circumstances. Yet the faith that concerns spiritual realities has effects in natural life. Faith for natural life is not a different faith from faith in God; it is the natural effect of the same faith.

This was true from the beginning. In Genesis, man's faith or unbelief concerning God's word affected the natural world. When man fell into unbelief, death and corruption entered, and the ground was cursed. The spiritual action had natural consequences. That shows that faith was never meant to be disconnected from the natural world.

Abraham is the principal example. Genesis 15:6 says that Abraham believed Yahweh, and it was accounted to him for righteousness. He was not believing merely for a car, house, money, or another natural object. He believed the self-existent God who would redeem and make right what had gone wrong. His faith was about righteousness, redemption, and God's purpose.

Yet that faith had natural effects. Abraham prayed for Abimelech, and God healed Abimelech, his wife, and his female servants. The faith was not a faith in natural things, but the faith in Yahweh affected natural things. In the same way, the believer's faith in God, redemption, and the divine life is meant to have effect in the natural life.

To think only naturally about every natural matter is to fall short of the full implication of faith. Natural processes exist and may be attended to, but the believer must not leave the power and life of God out of the account. Faith for natural life does not make natural things the object of faith; it brings the realities of God into the way the believer approaches natural life.

## Jesus Demonstrated Faith for Natural Life

The Lord Jesus demonstrated this repeatedly. He dealt with sickness, storms, provision, death, and other realities of natural life from the standpoint of faith in God. His works were not an invitation to make faith a pursuit of material things; they revealed that God's life and power are not irrelevant to the natural world.

When Jesus spoke of little faith, he was addressing people who did not allow what they knew of God to govern their consciousness in the situation before them. Faith involves knowing who God is and what he has done, then standing in that truth when natural circumstances are demanding attention.

The believer must learn to take the same stand. The Christian does not deny what is happening in the natural, but neither does he permit the natural circumstance to become the final account of reality. He reckons the life, power, preservation, provision, and help of God as realities that belong in the way he faces natural life.

## Remain in Constant Faith for Natural Life

Faith for natural life is not a momentary response used only in emergencies. It is a constant state of reckoning. The believer remains conscious that God's power is working in his life—spiritually and naturally—and allows that consciousness to produce the fitting emotions and conduct.

The emotion that agrees with faith for natural life is not panic. It is peace. This does not mean a person has no natural feelings, but that the governing consciousness is confidence in God's power and care. The believer can say that God's power is constantly working for his natural life: for preservation, deliverance, provision, safety, and the fulfillment of what God has assigned.

Our faith remains faith in God: in his redemption, his purpose, his nature, and his power. But that faith is meant to be lived. As we remain in faith, it affects the way we walk through the realities of natural life.
