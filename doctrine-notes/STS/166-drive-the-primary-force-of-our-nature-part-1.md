# 166. Drive - The Primary Force of our Nature (Part 1)

## The Singular Function of Church Leadership

Turn your Bibles to Ephesians 4:11. Paul says that the Lord Jesus himself gave the apostles, prophets, evangelists, and pastor-teachers. He is telling us that Jesus gave the leaders of the church.

It does not matter what title they go by. Someone may say, "I am Apostle so-and-so." Another may say, "I am Evangelist so-and-so," "Prophet so-and-so," or "Pastor so-and-so." Someone else may use bishop or elder. Whatever title they use, they are pointing to the fact that they function in leadership.

No matter what title they go by, we are about to find out why Jesus gave them. They could call themselves this or that, and we could call ourselves this or that. What matters is whether the function is being performed. Someone can say, "I am a doctor," and then you ask for help in that regard. If the person cannot perform the function, or fails to perform it, the title does not matter. It is like saying, "I am a barrister and solicitor of the Supreme Court of Nigeria," while never doing anything in that capacity.

There is a reason Jesus gave the leaders of the church. Verse 12 opens that reason to us: he gave them for the perfecting of the saints. Other translations, such as the NKJV, use the expression "the equipping of the saints." Jesus gave the leaders of the church for the equipping of the saints.

Any pastor, apostle, bishop, elder, evangelist, or prophet you can think of was given by Jesus for the perfecting of the saints. From that text we can also infer the reason for the church, because the leaders given by Jesus were meant to found or continue the church.

The inference is important. If Jesus gives the people who establish and continue the church for one stated function, that function tells us what the church itself is meant to accomplish. The title does not create a second purpose. Apostle, prophet, evangelist, pastor, bishop, or elder must be measured by whether the saints are being brought toward the point Jesus intended.

Matthew 28:18-20 says Jesus told his apostles, "All authority has been given to me in heaven and on earth. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I commanded you; and lo, I am with you always, even to the end of the age."

## Discipleship Is the Work of Perfecting

He told them to go and make disciples. The expression "perfecting" and the command to "make disciples" are not the same Greek words, but they are essentially communicating the same work. If we ask for the singular reason Jesus gave anyone to be a leader in the church, we can answer, "the perfecting of the saints," or we can equally say, "to make disciples."

Luke 6:40 helps us see why. The Lord Jesus said, "A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher." Jesus was speaking about a discipleship relationship, and then he introduced the language of being perfectly trained. This is related to the word for perfecting, *katartismos*, and in this case its verbal form, *katartizo*. A disciple cannot be more than his master, but when the disciple is perfectly trained, he will be as his master. The activity or work carried out in discipleship is perfecting, and the goal is to be like the master. Therefore, saying that Jesus gave leaders to make disciples is the same as saying Jesus gave leaders to perfect the saints.

Whoever leads the church, and whatever title the person bears, has this singular job: the perfecting of the saints. Today there are places where people say, "I go to church to partake of the Eucharist." That was not the primary understanding of the church we find with the apostles, even though they had such a practice. What Jesus clearly sent the leaders to do was make disciples, not merely give people something to eat, though such a practice may be carried out with a scriptural intention. We have to stay in line with the Lord's expectation concerning his church: the church should be for the perfecting, or discipleship, of the saints.

This raises a question: are you signed up for discipleship in church? It is one thing for the leader of the church to be committed to discipleship. It is another thing for you to see why Jesus gave the church and say, "I am committing myself to discipleship."

I often speak of three levels of ambition. There is God's ambition, there is the leader's or preacher's ambition, and there is the recipient's ambition. That third level also affects whether God's will is fulfilled. What determines the speed and progress people make in God's purpose is often how much ambition they themselves have in the ambition God has.

God says, "I want these people to be disciples. I want them to be perfected. I want them to be like me." Then he tells the leaders, "Go and perfect them to be like me." But if the recipients do not respond, "I want to be like him; I want to turn out the way he wants me to turn out," there will be a mismatch and something will be lost. The leaders must sign up to God's ambition, and the recipients must also sign up to it. Are you actually signed up to discipleship? Are you committed to what the Lord wants to do in you?

That commitment affects the way you receive church. You are not merely attending a meeting or receiving information from a preacher. You are consenting to be trained toward likeness to the Master. The leader's ambition cannot substitute for that consent. When the recipient's ambition agrees with God's ambition and the leader's assignment, the work of perfecting can proceed with speed and progress.

## Katartismos and the Completion Point

Ephesians 4:12 says he gave the leaders for the perfecting of the saints. The word *katartismos* has a simple idea. It can be explained with the prefix *kata*, meaning downward, and the idea of *artios*, a completion point. It communicates accomplishing something, bringing it to an intended end or marked point of completion.

The moment you see *katartizo* or *katartismos*, the real question should be: "What point are they saying we should get down to? What point are we meant to reach? What point are we meant to arrive at?" Jesus is saying, "I am giving the church to ensure that the saints arrive at a particular point." We call that discipleship. Luke 6:40 says the point is that the disciple will be like the master.

The next question is obvious: what is the point Jesus wants us to reach? Ephesians 4:13 answers it. We have established that Jesus gave the leaders of the church for the perfecting of the saints, and that perfecting means bringing the saints to a particular completion point. Verse 13 describes that point.

It says, "Till we all come." That language gives us the picture of a journey with a destination. It is like saying, "I am already on my way to your house. Am I supposed to turn right or left?" You may not know every turn from the person's starting point, but once the person reaches a landmark you know, you say, "Take the first turning there, then keep going straight until you come to the yellow ice-cream place." The instruction "keep going until you come to" identifies both movement and arrival. In the same way, perfecting is not movement without an end. The church is leading the saints through a journey toward a defined destination.

The destination is described in four ways:

"Till we all come to the unity of the faith, and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to a perfect man, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ."

The first description is the unity of the faith. The second is the knowledge of the Son of God. The third is the perfect man. The fourth is the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.

These are four descriptions of one completion point, not four unrelated points. Yet we can notice two dimensions in the descriptions. The unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God both concern knowledge. Faith here is the message believed, and the unity of the faith concerns the wholesomeness and full detail of that message.

The next two descriptions concern a person and the stature of that person: the perfect man and the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. They are no longer merely describing knowledge in the mind; they describe a man who has arrived at a perfect point in growth. So, for the moment, we can describe one destination in two dimensions: an end point in knowledge and an end point in growth.

## The Destination in Knowledge

Knowledge is a feature of our mind. It is not a feature of the physical body. The church's work, therefore, is addressed to the mind. Coming to the unity of the faith concerns the mind because faith is believed in the heart, or mind, and the unity of the faith concerns the whole detail of the message believed.

Ephesians 4:5 has already spoken of one faith. The unity of the faith speaks of the wholesomeness of that message. You believed the foundational message, but you are now being brought into its full detail.

We can speak of truth at three levels. There is foundational or primary truth, which is what you hear to be saved. It is entrance truth. Then there is intermediate truth, the entire body, wholesomeness, and full detail of that truth. There is also tertiary truth, which is educatory and practical: "What does that truth now mean for me? What am I supposed to do?" Not every practical judgment is written as a direct sentence in God's word, so in some matters you must make a judgment in your context and find out what is acceptable to the Lord.

Those practical judgments are not all stated as direct rules in scripture. Ephesians 5:10 says we should find out what is acceptable to the Lord. In one context, a person may conclude that something is acceptable to the Lord, while someone in another context may judge differently.

A common example is clothing. What we presently consider modest, and rightly maintain as modest in our context, might not have been considered modest a hundred or two hundred years ago. Imagine somebody dressing today in the way people are portrayed in films set in older Yoruba or Igbo times, tying something here and something there, and then walking into church that way. People would not be comfortable. Yet in that person's historical context it could have been ordinary and modest. If somebody does it today, it is not modest because the cultural context has changed. This is why there are practical truths in which we must find out what is acceptable to the Lord.

The fabric itself is not the only issue in that practical judgment. Clothing communicates within a culture. What communicated ordinary covering in one historical setting may communicate exposure in another. Tertiary truth therefore applies the character and intention of the foundational message to a living context. It does not overturn the faith; it asks how the faith is acceptably expressed here and now.

So there are practical truths in which we find out what is acceptable to the Lord. But the unity of the faith concerns the wholesomeness of the first message believed, and that concerns the mind. The knowledge of the Son of God also concerns the mind.

## The Perfect Man Is Not Bodily or Spiritual Growth

The next descriptions are the perfect man and the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. What part of our person needs to arrive at this stature?

When Paul says "the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ," is he talking about the stature of our bodies physically? Definitely not. We cannot all have the same bodily stature, and scripture does not instruct us to do so. Then is he talking about the stature of our spirits? That answer sounds spiritual, but it is still wrong. The believer's spirit is not on a journey from partial divinity to full divinity. It is already joined to Christ and complete in him.

We can easily make the error of saying that Ephesians 4:13 speaks of the growth of our spirit, but that would be wrong. In scripture, the stature of our spirit is already like Christ. The reality of a believer's spirit is such that it cannot grow into a more complete spiritual state. Unlike your body, which can still grow and change in different ways, there is no change that can happen to your spirit to make it more divine. It is already in a complete, perfect, and divine state in Christ.

First Corinthians 6:17 says, "He who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with him." The way his Spirit is, in nature, is the way our spirit is. There is no difference in kind. That is why Paul says the body is the temple of the Holy Spirit, whom we have from God, and then says, "Glorify God in your body and in your spirit, which are God's." Our spirit is still mentioned because our spirit and God's Spirit have become united.

This is why the language can sound interchangeable without confusing the Creator with the believer. You may say, "God's Spirit is working in me," and you may say, "My spirit is energizing me." What you have is God's Spirit, but your spirit has entirely embodied his Spirit in union. We are not describing two spirits acting independently, one acting upon the other before the other can act. God's Spirit has fused with your spirit. We are describing the believer as one spirit with the Lord, and the believer's spirit is therefore in a complete, perfect, and divine state.

Romans 8:15 says, "You did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, 'Abba, Father.'" Verse 16 says, "The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God."

Notice the order in Romans 8. Verse 15 says we received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, "Abba, Father." Verse 16 then says the Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God. The Spirit and our spirit are so united that their testimony concerning our sonship is the same. My spirit is now of God, while the Spirit I received is the Spirit of God. If you speak about your spirit now, you speak of it as embodying God's Spirit; if you speak of God's Spirit in you, you speak of him working in that union. This is why the believer's spirit is already in the complete state being described.

## Partakers of the Divine Nature

Second Peter 1:3-4 says that God's divine power has given to us all things that pertain to life and godliness through the knowledge of him who called us to glory and virtue. By these were given to us exceedingly great and precious promises, that through these we might become partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust.

Peter places two realities together. We have escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust, which speaks of going out of the former nature, and we have been made partakers of the divine nature. Salvation did not merely improve the old spirit. The old condition was left behind, and the nature now held in common is the divine nature.

The word translated "partakers" is *koinonos*, related to *koinos*, meaning common. It does not say divinity was shared out so that one person received ten percent, another person ten percent, and another ten percent. It means everybody has that nature in common. You do not have a tiny percentage of humanity because there are billions of human beings; you have humanity fully in common with every other human being. In the same way, what God has done in Christ, and what we have received, has caused us to have the divine nature in common.

Colossians 2:9-10 says, "In him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily, and you are complete in him," who is the head of all principality and power. The word *Godhead* refers to divinity. If we ask what is in Christ, the answer is the fullness of divinity. If we ask where the believer is complete, the answer is in him. We are complete in the one in whom the fullness of divinity dwells. Therefore, when Ephesians speaks of the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ, it cannot be describing a process of adding divinity to the believer's spirit.

Therefore, when Ephesians 4:13 says we are to come to the perfect man and the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ, it cannot be speaking about the body and it cannot be speaking about the spirit. It is again speaking about the mind.

## The Man of the Mind

The perfect man in Ephesians 4:13 concerns a man of the mind. Scripture uses the word "man" in different ways.

Second Corinthians 4:16 says, "Even though our outward man is perishing, yet the inward man is being renewed day by day." The outward man is the physical body, which is decaying or growing older because it is mortal. The inward man in that context is the spirit, which is not decaying but remains fresh.

"Renewed day by day" does not mean the spirit becomes stale over twenty-four hours and receives one refresh at the beginning of the next day. It is a way of saying that, while the mortal body is decaying, the inward man is not participating in that decay. It remains fresh. In that text, then, the outward man is the body and the inward man is the spirit.

Yet scripture also speaks of a man pertaining to the mind. First Peter 3:3-4 speaks to wives and says their adornment should not merely be outward. They should also adorn "the hidden man of the heart." It speaks of a man of the heart or mind.

Peter's illustration is ordinary and practical. A person dresses the outward person and gives attention to outward adornment. Peter says not to make that the only adornment; dress inwardly as well. The place of that inward adornment is "the hidden man of the heart." He is not naming the physical body or merely repeating the spirit-man language. He is identifying a man whose condition is expressed in the state of the heart or mind.

We can therefore speak of the outward man, the body; the inward man, the spirit; and the hidden man of the heart, the mind-man. The outward man is perishing because it is mortal. The inward man is not decaying; it remains fresh. But First Peter identifies a man that pertains to the heart or mind and tells wives not merely to adorn themselves outwardly, but also to adorn the hidden man of the heart.

This mind-man is not substantial in the same sense as the body or spirit. The body has physical or material substance, and the spirit has spiritual, immaterial substance. Yet scripture still describes something of the mind as a man: the hidden man of the heart, or the inner man in the language Paul uses in Ephesians 3. Its substance is the state of the mind.

We should think of the mind as attached to the spirit the way the brain is attached to the body. The classic example is the Lord's account of Lazarus and the rich man. Death separated both men from their physical bodies, but it did not leave their spirits without thought, memory, recognition, or response. Their minds continued to operate with their spirits. Scripture therefore expects you to think about yourself primarily as one who is a spirit with a mind and who also has a body with a brain. You have a mind to your spirit just as you have a brain to your body.

Ephesians 4:13 first speaks of knowledge on the mind, but then it speaks of the perfect man and the measure of the stature of Christ. That is no longer simply knowledge; it concerns growth on the mind.

What determines the substance of this growth of the mind? It is not merely knowledge. We can best describe it with the words consciousness or reckoning. The growth of the mind is growth in consciousness and growth in reckoning.

Your mind can grow in knowledge without having grown in consciousness. What scripture really calls growth in this practical sense is consciousness, while knowledge is prerequisite to it. The fact that you know you have the Spirit of God, including the nature of love, does not mean you will actually walk in love. Knowledge by itself does not produce the conduct; the substance of this growth is consciousness or reckoning.

## Knowledge, Consciousness, and Conduct

You know that you are born of God, that your nature is love, and that you can only walk in love. But knowing that does not automatically mean you will walk in love. There is the nature of love in your spirit, there is the knowledge of that nature on your mind, and there must also be the consciousness of that nature on your mind. It is that consciousness which causes the walk of love in your conduct. You do not simply do what you know. You do not even necessarily do what you are convinced of. You do what you are conscious of.

The perfect man and the measure of the stature of Christ concern the hidden man of the heart, or what Paul calls the inner man in Ephesians 3. The substance of that mind-man is consciousness.

It is not a physical substance like the body or a spiritual substance like the spirit. Nevertheless, it is described as a man: the hidden man and the inner man. God wants the consciousness you have of yourself to come to a particular state that he calls perfect. This is how your mind-man ought to be. The church is therefore given so that we can be perfected not only toward a point in knowledge, but toward a point in consciousness.

## Perfecting Serves God's Purpose

Why does Jesus want us to be perfected as saints? To what end? Growth is good in itself, but that is not the full reason Jesus wants us to grow.

Growth is good in itself, but that is not the full answer to why Jesus wants us to grow. He wants us perfected because he has a purpose and wants us to fulfill it. We have answered the question of God's purpose before: God's purpose is sonship. Jesus died and rose from the dead, and then he gave people to perfect us in knowledge and consciousness so that we can fulfill that purpose.

We can think of the whole framework in five stages.

This framework helps us place growth inside the whole purpose of the Christian life. We are not studying nature, knowledge, consciousness, discipline, or conduct as disconnected subjects. There is a reality God has created in us, an intention for which he created it, an order by which that intention is carried out, an actual doing of that order, and a final accomplishment. If one stage is known while the next is neglected, the purpose has not yet reached fulfillment.

First, there is the reality of your nature. Once you are born again, you receive God's nature.

Second, there is the reality of your purpose, God's purpose for your life. When God saved us and gave us his nature, it was because of the purpose he wants us to fulfill.

Nature and purpose belong together. God did not give us his nature without an intention for its expression. When he saved us and made us partakers of the divine nature, he brought us into the purpose for which that nature is to function. That is why knowing who you are in Christ must lead into knowing what God intends his child and heir to fulfill.

Third, there is how to fulfill that purpose. "How" means process. There is a process, order, or method that details what must be done for the purpose to be fulfilled.

The how is not yet the accomplishment. A person can possess a complete plan and still not have built anything. Order identifies the sequence: what is done first, what follows, what resources are used, and how daily time is arranged. It makes the purpose actionable, but the actions must still be performed.

Fourth, there is actualization. Everything you have seen must be done. You must carry it out.

Fifth, there is fulfillment. You have accomplished it.

## Nature, Purpose, Order, Actualization, and Fulfillment

Do you know God's purpose for your life? Ephesians 1:5 and 1:9 show that God's purpose is sonship. Sonship means that we are his children and his heirs.

An heir is somebody to whom something has been assigned. We are heirs in two dimensions: natural and spiritual. You have a natural assignment and a spiritual assignment. There is something God has called you to fulfill in the natural, and there is something he has called you to fulfill spiritually. His purpose is that you function as his child, his natural heir, and his spiritual heir, walking as his child and fulfilling your assignment in both dimensions.

After the reality of nature and purpose comes the process. The how is like a detailing or map that spells out everything you are supposed to do to fulfill the purpose. If someone says, "You are supposed to build this house," the next question is, "How am I going to build it?" You will need sand, you will need this and that, and you spell out the whole process. If you follow that process to the letter, you have the house. We can describe the how with the words *order*, *process*, or *method*.

The best way to define the how is that you are supposed to use your time to fulfill God's purpose. You have twenty-four hours a day and one hundred and sixty-eight hours a week, so you define your time. "I am supposed to wake up and have my devotion. When I have my devotion, I am energized to fulfill his assignment for the day. Then I freshen up. After that I do this next, and then I do that next." You can outline what you are supposed to do in the form of a daily order.

Within that general order, each assignment has its own smaller process. The day identifies when you address the assignment; the detailed how identifies the steps by which you carry it forward.

The daily order is the general how into which the more specific processes fit. Devotion supplies spiritual energy for the day, ordinary responsibilities occupy their appointed time, and the natural and spiritual assignments each receive concrete action. The order is not an abstract schedule admired on paper. It translates purpose into what is done with the next hour and the next responsibility.

Within your natural assignment there is also a process. You may know that God wants you to begin a business. You may even know the product, the people it should serve, the scale it should reach, and the stages through which it should grow. You can spell out the process.

It is possible to know the natural assignment with considerable specificity. You may know the kind of product, the people it should serve, the price at which it should be sold, the scale it should eventually reach, and the stages between a small beginning and that scale. All of that belongs to the how. The clarity of the map does not remove the need to walk through it.

Suppose your natural assignment is to start a business producing a snack. You know the product you are supposed to produce, the price range, and that the business should grow until it is selling perhaps a million pieces a day. You can spell out the how: "I will start small, I will do this, then I will go here, and then we will grow to that point." Your natural assignment has an order inside it.

Within your spiritual assignment, you know God wants you to partake of what he is doing in this redemptive age and share the gospel with people. You outline your time: "I will reach so many people every day. I will do this and keep progressing over time." It grows until we are influencing the earth, salting the earth, and lighting the world.

As a child of God, you understand that you are to walk in God's nature and character: in love, righteousness, boldness, and everything else that belongs to him.

This dimension is not separate from the natural and spiritual assignments. You fulfill both as God's child. The same nature must govern the way you build, preach, lead, work, and relate to people. God's purpose includes walking in his character while carrying out what has been assigned: love rather than selfishness, righteousness rather than sin, and boldness rather than fear.

You can see your nature and spell your purpose out to the last detail. You can know exactly what you are meant to do naturally, spiritually, and as a child walking in God's nature and character. You can even know the whole order. But then what? The next thing is action. We can use the word *actualization*: everything we have seen, we must fully do. After that comes fulfillment: we have done it.

Your life is very simple when viewed in these five realities. God made you for his purpose, so he gave you his nature. You come to know your purpose and how to do it. You then actualize that purpose in its process, and in doing so you fulfill it. Number one: nature. Number two: purpose. Number three: order or process. Number four: actualization. Number five: fulfillment.

Paul spoke of fulfillment in Second Timothy 4:7-8: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. Finally, there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous Judge, will give to me on that day, and not to me only but also to all who have loved his appearing."

Paul could say definitively, "I have fulfilled it." At the beginning he knew his nature and purpose. The Lord told him, "You are a chosen vessel for this." He knew the how and went about doing it; he said, "I made it my ambition to preach the gospel in this way." But then he did the major fourth part: he actualized it. At the end he could say, "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith." He had fulfillment.

## Actualization Requires the Will

We have examined the reality of who we are in Christ, our nature, how to develop the inner man, and how to grow. We have seen the general principle of developing and adorning the inner man by the faith stand.

We are now placing those teachings in order. Knowing the reality of the new creation answers the question of nature. Seeing sonship, natural assignment, and spiritual assignment answers the question of purpose. Learning the faith stand, daily order, and the process of the assignment begins to answer the question of how. But none of those answers, by itself, means the purpose has been actualized.

Now you have seen your nature, your purpose, and how to carry out that purpose by your nature. What must happen next? You must do it. How are you going to do God's purpose? Some people simply try to do it with stubborn force. But before you do anything, you take a decision. Anything you are going to do requires the will to decide. That decision is not yet the completed deed, but it is the point at which the mind engages the force that will move into action.

Man has a body and is a spirit, and the spirit has a mind. We have studied seven things in the mind of man.

The first is the consideration space, where you receive information and process it. Everything being said to you first enters there. You consider it, inspect it, and think about it before the other faculties work with it.

The second is the memory bank. Everything that goes into your consideration space also goes into your memory, but some information is flushed later. That is why you remember some details and cannot remember others, depending on how well the information has been kept.

The third is the will or free will, the executive, decision-making faculty. It relates both to intention and attention. You intend to do something, and so you set your will in motion. Or you put your attention on something, and your will is operating. This is the faculty that must act when what you know is to be actualized.

The fourth is the conviction bank, containing the things you agree with. If someone says an object is made of plastic, the information first enters your consideration space. You inspect and confirm it, then agree with your will: "It is actually made of plastic." If you merely remember that somebody said it, that is memory. But after you consider, confirm, and agree, you can say, "I know that thing is made of plastic." That is conviction.

The fifth is the consciousness bank, which is particularly important for conduct. Something can be your conviction without being your consciousness. You may know that you are a new creation and still fail to act as a new creation because you are not always conscious that you are a new creation. Someone can know First John 3:9, that he is born of God and cannot sin, but if his consciousness of that reality is weak, his conduct may contradict what he knows.

Such a believer will be repentant, because the mark of a believer is repentance on the ground of faith in Christ. Repentance does not mean the person has already reached perfection. The person may be at a lower or higher point on the spectrum of growth, but a believer cannot be without repentance grounded in faith in Christ. The point here is that even conviction does not by itself determine conduct. You do what you are conscious of.

Miracles, tongues, or other outward manifestations do not replace this mark. The believer may not yet express perfection, but faith in Christ has brought a repentant relationship to sin. The person may be low or high on the spectrum of growth, yet cannot settle into the old nature as identity. That conflict illustrates the distinction: conviction may be present while consciousness is still too weak to govern conduct consistently.

The sixth is emotions, and the seventh can be described as conscience or expression, depending on how the faculties are arranged for explanation. You can list number six as emotions and number seven as conscience. Or you can group emotions and conscience or judgment together and call number seven expression. The arrangement may differ, but the framework shows the operations that explain why a person may know one thing and still express another.

These faculties do not operate as unrelated boxes. Information enters consideration and memory; the will can agree with it so that it becomes conviction; reckoning can form consciousness after that conviction; emotions and conscience respond; expression appears in conduct. When conduct contradicts truth, the framework helps us ask where the process stopped instead of merely saying, "But I know this already."

If you do not understand these dimensions of mind, you may not understand why you act as you do. You may say, "I know who I am in Christ, so why am I still doing this?" You must locate the issue. Do you already agree? Are you convinced? But are you conscious of it? Do you reckon it?

Your spirit is already what it is, and your body is already in its present state, but your mind is working out the details. Your mind may know in conviction the reality of your nature, your purpose, and the order that will lead to fulfillment. Yet number four in the five-stage sequence is doing, action, actualization. You must carry it out. Your spirit and body act through your mind, and the faculty of mind that acts for anything to happen is the will.

This is the real issue when somebody says, "I know who I am in Christ, and I know what I am meant to do, but why am I not doing it?" The question must be located in the operations of the mind. Has the information only been considered and remembered? Has the will agreed so that it is conviction? Has that conviction become the consciousness from which the person acts? Actualization does not occur merely because correct information is present somewhere in the mind.

## Reckoning Forms Consciousness

Paul first asks, "Do you not know that as many as were baptized into Christ were baptized into his death?" Then, after stating the reality of their union with Christ in his death and resurrection, he says, "Likewise, reckon yourselves." Knowledge is already present, but now they must reckon that reality so it becomes the consciousness from which they act.

Paul does not tell them to reckon themselves into Christ's death as though their reckoning creates the union. He first teaches what happened in Christ and asks whether they know it. "Likewise" then directs the mind to take that known reality into account. Knowledge supplies the truth; reckoning forms the corresponding consciousness; conduct proceeds from that consciousness.

Reckoning is what forms consciousness. James says, "Do not be a hearer only, but a doer," deceiving or misreckoning yourself. Reckoning in this sense is the faith stand, the operation by which you become conscious of the reality. The reality does not begin when you reckon it; reckoning takes the reality already given and forms your consciousness after it.

Faith stand is attentive affirmation, not merely reciting words. Someone may say, "I was doing my faith stand," when all the person did was recite a faith-stand text. You can read, "There is one body and one Spirit," and simply be reading. You can say, "I am a new creation," without putting your attention on what you are affirming. That is recitation, not yet the faith stand in its proper operation.

Attentive affirmation is different. Your will is engaged as you say, "I am a new creation. I am born of God. God's power works in my body. The healing power of God works in my body; it affects my soul and my body." You do not rush through those words merely to finish them. Faith stand is attentive affirmation.

The attention is not an optional decoration added to the words. It is the will directing itself toward the reality being affirmed. The same sentence may be mere reading in one moment and a faith stand in another. In the faith stand, the person is not trying to complete many lines quickly; the person remains with the truth until the mind consciously takes account of it.

The word *logizomai* means to take something into account. You do not *logizomai* something that is not already real. Suppose there were eighteen kilograms of chicken, but somebody entered twenty kilograms in the account. That person did not reckon reality; that is fraud. *Logizomai* takes things into account as they are. When you say, "I am a partaker of the divine nature," you are not trying to create divinity by speaking. You are taking the existing reality into account.

Your spirit is already what it is. Your body is what it is. Your mind is working out the details. Your mind knows in conviction the reality of your nature, your purpose, and the order for fulfilling the purpose. But actualization requires doing.

What faculty of the mind acts so that something can happen? It is the will. Yet everything we do involves an interaction between the faculty called will and the force called drive. The will is a faculty of mind. Drive is not simply another item in the mind setup; it is the primary force of nature. The first thing the will needs to affect is drive.

## Drive Is the Primary Force of Nature

The faculty of the mind called the will must first engage this primary force of nature called drive.

Second Peter 1:4-5 helps us see this. Peter says that we have become partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust. Then he says, "But also for this very reason, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue."

The opening of verse 5 is translated poorly in the KJV as "beside this." You may think Peter is saying, "Put this former point aside and let us go on to something else." But the expression means "upon this selfsame thing" or "for this very reason." He has just said that we are partakers of the divine nature and have escaped the corruption in the world through lust. It is on that same reality that the action of verse 5 proceeds.

The expression translated "giving" is related to the Greek *pareisphero*. It can be explained through *para*, beside or alongside; *eis*, into; and *phero*, to bear or carry. The picture is of carrying something in alongside something already present.

Take the parts carefully. *Para* means beside or alongside, as in the idea behind "paramilitary." *Eis* means into or unto, as when scripture speaks of baptism into the name. *Phero* means to bear or carry, as one bears a burden. Put together, the picture is "carry into alongside." Peter is saying, "Bring in diligence alongside something that is already there so that you can add every other thing."

The construction therefore gives us movement and position. Something is carried in, and when it is carried in, it stands alongside what was already present. Peter is not describing diligence as a replacement for faith. Neither is he describing faith as the final expression by itself. Faith is present, drive is brought alongside it, and from there the listed expressions are supplied.

Peter is saying to bring diligence in alongside faith and then add virtue and the other expressions. Which thing was already there? Faith. He does not say, "Add faith"; he says, "Add to your faith." It is the faith you already used to receive the gospel, now continuing as the faith stand. Bring diligence in alongside that faith, and then add virtue and the remaining expressions. The very first thing brought in is diligence.

The word translated diligence is *spoude*. It communicates diligence, earnestness, haste, zeal, or drive. So Peter is saying that the first thing you are supposed to bring in is drive. Now that you have become a partaker of the divine nature, bring the drive of that nature in alongside faith. From there, the other expressions are added.

Drive is therefore the primary force of our nature.

You can have an order, which is part of discipline. You can write down what you intend to do, decide when it should be done, and make the order definite. Drive also belongs to discipline, but it is the primary force that makes the expressions of that order move from intention into action. The written order identifies the deed; drive supplies the wanting that carries the will toward it.

## The Flesh Had Passions and Desires

Galatians 5:24 says, "Those who are Christ's have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires." The flesh is the old man, the old nature we used to have.

Romans 6:6 says, "Knowing this, that our old man was crucified with him, that the body of sin might be done away with, that we should no longer be slaves of sin." The body of sin is the entity or substance that did sin, the former state of our spirit.

Galatians 5:24 does not say those passions and desires existed only on the mind. Paul attaches them to the flesh, the old man, the nature formerly in the spirit. They were with that nature and moved from there toward the mind. That means the energy causing a person to want to do something wrong was not merely generated by the mind. It came from the nature toward the mind.

The word translated passions is *pathema*, and the word for desires is *epithymia*, often translated lust. The desire to do one thing or another, the energy of wanting, is not of the mind on its own. It is a force pertaining first to nature and then going to the mind. You may say, "I feel like doing this," and you are speaking of what is happening on your mind, but the drive or energy of that desire came toward the mind from the nature.

Romans 6:12 says, "Therefore do not let sin reign in your mortal body, that you should obey it in its lusts." Sin is pictured as an authority or slave master. Because you are dead to it, that former nature should not reign. Yet the text says sin has lusts or desires of its own.

The wording treats sin as more than an isolated wrong action. It is pictured as an authority seeking to reign, and its lusts seek obedience. The believer's death to sin means that authority no longer belongs within the spirit, but the will must still refuse the desire presented to the mind. "Do not let" places responsibility at the point where that offered drive would be accepted or rejected.

When people had the body of sin, the old man, that entity had a drive it communicated to the mind. For anything you do, the desire to do it is first a force that reaches the mind. When a person stole, what first happened was not that the mind independently produced the desire to steal. The flesh sent the wanting toward the mind.

The desire, affection, or force that makes you want to do something pertains first to the spirit and then enters the mind. For every expression, including fulfilling God's purpose, there is first an energy or force of desire that reaches the mind. It is the energy of drive.

Once that desire was accepted by the will, the old nature also supplied ability, capacity, or energy for the act of stealing. So for every expression there is energy that drives the person toward it, and there is energy by which the action is performed. When we speak of fulfilling God's purpose, the will must first receive and engage from the new nature the energy of desire, the energy of drive, and then the capacity for the action.

## The Drive of Sin in the World

Ephesians 2:1-3 speaks of the negative side. It says, "You he made alive, who were dead in trespasses and sins, in which you once walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit who now works in the sons of disobedience, among whom we all once conducted ourselves in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, just as the others."

The passage moves from nature to course and then to conduct. They were dead in trespasses and sins; that was the condition of the spirit. They walked according to the course of this world and the prince of the power of the air; that nature participated in a larger order of sin. Then the same reality appeared in conduct as they fulfilled the desires of the flesh and of the mind. The action was the outward end of a drive rooted in nature and received on the mind.

The death in trespasses and sins describes the former nature of our spirit. We walked according to the course of this world and according to the prince of the power of the air. The nature that was in the unregenerate spirit was the nature operating in the enemy and in the fallen spirits that accepted it: the nature of sin. Sin has both the desire to sin and the actual act of sinning. In the positive, God has the will of love and the act of loving. Sin still reigns in the world, so its energy and drive toward sin are still present.

The difference for the believer is the location of that drive. Sin no longer emits it from within our spirit as it once did. But while we remain in the world, its drive still comes toward us from outside. Words, images, sounds, and other influences can carry and emit that energy toward the mind.

This explains why becoming a new creation does not mean the believer can no longer encounter sinful desire. The source has changed. Before salvation, the flesh emitted its passions and desires from within. Now the believer is dead to that nature, but still lives in a world ordered by sin. The course of the world continues to communicate its drive through what is heard, seen, watched, and repeatedly entertained.

Someone says something, and the energy of wanting and doing sin is emitted toward you. The instruction, "Do not let sin reign," means your will must not accept that energy and obey sin in its lusts. Ephesians 2 says the spirit now works in the sons of disobedience. Among them we also once conducted ourselves in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind. The desires of the flesh were sent toward the mind and fulfilled through it.

## God Works in Us to Will and to Do

Philippians 2:12-13 gives us the positive side: "Therefore, my beloved, as you have always obeyed, not as in my presence only, but now much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for his good pleasure."

The fear and trembling can include awe before the goodness of God: he chose to give us his Spirit, and we hold that gift and reality in awe as we work out what he has worked within us. Second Corinthians 5 also speaks of fear in light of judgment, because judgment affects how we live.

God works in us both to will and to do. There is energy worked in us even to will. The language includes determination, choosing, preference, inclination, and binding oneself toward something. God works in us so that we are inclined to his drive, disposed to it, and bound toward his good pleasure. Anything we are meant to do first has the energy that makes us want it, and then the energy that enables us to perform it.

The word behind "will" carries the idea of determination, preference, inclination, and being disposed toward something. The first divine working is therefore not limited to physical capacity for the deed. God works in us at the level of wanting: to prefer, incline toward, and bind ourselves to his good pleasure. Then he also works the doing. The energy of being driven toward the act precedes the energy expressed in the act.

That is why Peter says to take drive first and then add the other expressions. God is at work, which means he is not doing this only occasionally. If we may use the expression, the radiations or emissions of drive toward the things of God are constantly coming from his Spirit within us. At the same time, the energy that makes us able to perform the deed is also supplied. The primary thing to receive is the drive; with that drive, we readily receive the energy to do the corresponding act.

## Two Competing Drives

Drive is the primary force of any nature in existence. This is true whether we speak of sin and death or of God and life. The primary force emitted by a nature is the desire and drive to express according to itself.

Sin, the nature that entered the world through man's rejection of God's purpose, has a drive toward its own expression. God's nature also has a drive toward its own expression. The adversary and fallen spirits accepted sin, and the spirit or course of this world causes them to want and act contrary to God.

The principle can therefore be seen beyond one human decision. Sin existed as a nature accepted by the adversary and fallen spirits, and its drive causes those who participate in it to want and act contrary to God. Life also exists in God with its own drive toward love and his good pleasure. In each case, nature first presses toward the expression that agrees with it.

For the person who is not born again, that drive causes the person to want things for self and sin and to carry them out. Sometimes you see people so bent on fulfilling selfish desires that everything is about self. That bent is not merely a collection of detached decisions. There is a drive emitted from sin, making the person all about self rather than God and his purpose. In the same way, a person can become so consumed by God's drive that the person's life is wholly about God and his purpose.

The believer therefore lives in the presence of two competing drives. We are in the world, but we are not of the world. God's drive is emitted from within us, while sin's drive bombards us from outside. Our reckoning is, "I am dead to sin. I do not want to do this. I am alive to God, and I am only driven toward his purpose." As the drive of God is ignited and strengthened, the drive of the world is deadened and weakened in consciousness, and we are on track to walk in God's purpose.

This contention is not resolved by pretending that no influence is being presented from outside. It is resolved by taking the truth of the new nature into account. The will refuses to define the external pressure as the believer's own nature and agrees with the drive coming from the spirit. "I do not want this" is grounded in death to sin; "I am driven toward God's purpose" is grounded in life to God.

The external drive may arrive repeatedly and with emotional or bodily force, but repetition does not turn it into the believer's identity. The question remains whether the will will obey it in its lusts. The faith stand answers from the new nature, weakens the foreign drive on consciousness, and gives attention to the desire God is working from within.

What chooses to do this? Our will, through the faith stand. The will says, "I am a new creation. I want to do God's will. I want to walk in love. I want to walk in righteousness. I am always driven to walk in righteousness."

## Drive Reorders Mind and Body

As the will takes that faith stand, it begins to influence even the faculties of drive reflected in the physical body. Dopamine, moods, and other bodily processes become reordered toward God's will. There is a supernatural effect on the natural because of the faith stand taken by the will, until new habits are formed.

The body has natural reflections of drive. Hormones, neurotransmitters, moods, and reward patterns can become arranged around what a person has repeatedly desired and done. When the will takes its stand in the reality of the new nature, that supernatural reality begins to affect the natural system. The change is not only a verbal exercise on the mind. Bodily responses are progressively reordered toward God's will until a different pattern of action becomes habitual.

Remember the order: there is the reality of your nature, the reality of your purpose, the order or process, actualization, and fulfillment. In actualization, two things must be noted: the faculty of the mind called the will and the primary force of nature called drive.

Drive has reflections in bodily hormones and neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine. A person may have been bound to a particular thing before salvation. It can also, unfortunately, happen after salvation because of patterns formed in the mind and body.

A person may have been bound to a particular thing before salvation and carried into salvation a consciousness and bodily pattern built by repeated conduct. Unfortunately, a believer can also form such a pattern after salvation by repeatedly yielding to an external drive. In either case, the existence of neurological or bodily wiring does not mean the bondage is the person's new nature. It means the effect of former choices must now be addressed from the reality of that nature.

At salvation, the person realizes, "I am a new creation. I am not bound to this. This is not my nature." Yet something may still be wrong in the person's consciousness and in the bodily wiring formed through former habits.

What must the person do? The sequence matters. The person receives knowledge: "I am a new creation. I am not bound to this. This is not my nature." In that light, the will takes the faith stand and first affects drive, because conduct cannot be sustainably changed while the old desire is continually strengthened. As drive changes consciousness, it also affects the bodily response. New actions then establish new habits.

There is the energy of drive in our spirit that first changes us, and there is the energy for conduct and action. The energy of drive is emitted by God's Spirit, and there is also energy for the deed. The first thing is to receive the energy of drive, and then receive the energy of action.

These two workings should not be collapsed into one. The energy of drive forms the wanting, affection, and inclination on the mind. The energy of action supplies the doing that agrees with that inclination. If a person tries to reach immediately for the deed while continually feeding the opposite desire, the action becomes strained and unstable. Receive the energy that changes what you want, and then receive the energy by which the deed is performed.

## Develop the Drive of the Inner Man

In developing the inner man, the first thing to develop in our consciousness from our spirit is drive: desires, lusts, affections, and passions. In this case, it is desire for the right things, affection for the things of God, and passion for God's purpose.

We are not surrendering the vocabulary of desire, passion, affection, or even lust to sin. Galatians showed those forces attached to the old nature, but the principle of drive belongs to nature itself. The new nature has its own desires. The inner man must therefore be developed after desire for righteousness, affection for the things of God, and passion for the purpose of God.

Once drive is being formed rightly, the corresponding deeds begin to follow. We can then speak of developing character and other expressions, but we must first develop drive.

This does not make character or deeds unimportant. Peter says to add virtue and the other expressions. The order tells us how they are developed. The will first receives and strengthens the drive of the new nature; then it more readily receives the energy for the action. We develop the affection that agrees with God's character and then act in love, righteousness, boldness, and service.

We need drive for every part of God's purpose: to carry out his good pleasure, to fulfill natural assignment, and to fulfill spiritual assignment.

Say:

I am born again. I am born of God. I have a new nature, which means I have new desires and new abilities, new drives, new passions, new affections, and new capacities. The nature I received is not empty of desire and is not powerless to act. God's drive is present within me, and God's ability is present for the deeds that agree with that drive.

I affirm the new desires I have and form my drive on my mind and consciousness after them. I am driven toward righteousness. I am driven to walk in love. I am driven to walk in his good pleasure. I am driven to walk in his purpose. I am always willing to do all of his will. That is the way I am. I do not take the old habit, the former bodily response, or the pressure of the world as the definition of who I am. I form my consciousness after the desires of the nature God has given me.

Sometimes people say, "That is the way the person is biologically. That is the person's natural psychology." There is a supernatural solution when that natural state contradicts truth, God's word, and righteousness. The person can change from the mind to the body.

Biology or learned psychology may describe the present pattern, but it does not have the final word over the new creation. If the natural pattern contradicts truth and righteousness, the person is not required to accept it as permanent. The will can take the faith stand from knowledge, form a different drive in consciousness, and through repeated new action carry that change from the mind into the body.

I am driven to walk in love. I am driven to do the supernatural. I am driven to carry out my material assignment. I am driven to carry out my spiritual assignment. I am driven to reach the lost. I am driven to raise disciples. I am driven to glorify Jesus.

When we engage attentive affirmation, we reckon and stand in the reality. Our drives, affections, and desires change. We can take our faith stand in understanding and in tongues. We can affirm, "I am a new creation, and I am only driven toward the things of God."

The faith stand in tongues does not replace understanding; it carries the same conscious agreement in prayer. You can pray in tongues while holding the reality before the mind, then state it plainly: "I am a new creation. I am only driven toward the things of God." The point is not the sound or length of the prayer. The will is attending to and affirming the new nature until its drive is strengthened on consciousness.

Sometimes you must remain with the matter of drive. Do not hurry away merely because you can state the action you ought to perform. Change the drive on your mind, ignite the new drive, and strengthen it until the desire of the new nature is alive on the mind. Strengthen the affection for the right thing and weaken the competing affection. Once drive has been dealt with, the energy for the corresponding deed is received more readily.

Trying to perform an action without first dealing with drive will not work properly. The will must affect drive, and drive in turn affects actions.

As we wrap up, affirm who we are concerning our drive: the drives we have, the affections we have, and the passions we have. We affirm our affection, passion, and desire for the things of God, only the things of God; the will for the things of God and the drive for the things of God.

We affirm that our desires agree with God's good pleasure. We do not merely confess ability while leaving desire untouched. We affirm both the wanting and the doing: affection for his will, passion for his purpose, willingness for his order, and capacity for every deed he has assigned.

We are driven to have our devotion, to get up, and to carry out our assignments. We are driven to carry out the work of ministry and to fulfill our order every single day. We are driven to be orderly, to fulfill the natural assignment, and to fulfill the spiritual assignment. We are driven toward the lost, driven to raise disciples, driven to walk in love, and driven to glorify Jesus. Our will agrees with the drive of God's nature, and we act after all his good pleasure.
