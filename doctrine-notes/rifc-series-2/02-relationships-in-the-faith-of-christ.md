# Relationships in the Faith of Christ (2)

Father, I'm so glad that I'm your child and a part of your family. In this meeting, gathered with my siblings, I receive of your word light, truth, and revelation of Christ and his work in all its fullness, and of his fullness which is in me, his Spirit in me, causing me to have the nature and the steps to walk in my relationships to fulfill your purpose, in the name of our Lord Jesus.

## The Church and the Perfecting of the Saints

Turn your Bibles to Ephesians chapter 4. Ephesians 4 and verse 11. Paul, speaking about the Lord Jesus, says he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, and the pastor-teachers for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of ministry, and for the edifying of the body of Christ.

Till we all come to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, to a perfect man, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ; that we, being no longer children, tossed to and fro and carried about by every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men in cunning craftiness and deceitful plotting, but speaking the truth in love, will grow up into him in all things, who is the head, Christ. From him the whole body, joined and knit together by what every joint supplies, according to the effective working by which every part does its share, causes growth of the body for the edifying of itself in love.

Ephesians 4:1 all the way to 16 is a passage we often examine and study. There is a reason for the church, and that reason is to perfect the saints. It is the singular reason the church exists. Sometimes people get distracted by other things when it comes to church. Why do you go to church? Some say, "To worship God." Some say, "That is where my friends are." Some go because there is someone in particular they are eyeing.

They say, "The person is okay; I will go to church because of that sister or that brother." Some say, "That is my family church. All generations of my family have been attending it." Some say, "That is the first church. That is why I go there." Those were never the reasons people went to church in the Bible. What is "the first church"? Where do you get that definition? People create something that does not exist and confuse themselves. If anything were to be called the first church, the likely name would be Jerusalem, not Rome. That is not what makes a church a church. Wake up and get out of delusion.

What makes a church a church is already well spelled out in God's word. It is the place where the perfecting of the saints takes place by the unity of the faith. The church is the ground and pillar of the truth; you find the unity of the faith there. That means the message the Lord Jesus gave us, the doctrine the apostles outlined for us, and the Pauline revelation in the way, manner, and pattern it was presented. That is church, not what you think it should be.

If that is what the church is and what it is to do, perfecting the saints by that message, there is a question to ask: why exactly is that the reason for the church? The Lord Jesus had died and risen, and then he gave the church. That is what Paul explains here. He says that to each one of us grace was given according to the measure of Christ's gift. What grace is he talking about? He is talking about the grace to be apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers, because he completes that statement in verse 11.

Then he explains when he gave that grace: when he ascended on high, he led captivity captive and gave gifts to men. He gave these people to humanity. These are believers functioning in leadership responsibility over others because of what they do toward humanity. He first descended into the lower parts of the earth. He who descended is also the one who ascended far above all the heavens, that he might fill all things. It happens after his descent and then his ascent, which we said is a spiritual fact.

Of course, when he was buried physically, you could call that a descent to some degree. But the descent he is talking about is spiritual, into the lower parts of the earth. That is not merely the experience of physical burial; it is the spiritual experience of death. In scripture, someone goes down to the pit or to Sheol, a spiritual place you could call the spiritual underworld. What is the reason he said we should perfect the saints?

It is because there is a purpose accomplished in redemption, and the perfecting of the saints is given to help fulfill that purpose. What did Jesus accomplish in redemption? The Lord Jesus accomplished God's eternal purpose, the purpose God has always had. What is that purpose? We have examined it several times: sonship. Sonship means he wants a family that will inherit what he owns. What does inheriting the whole earth entail? It means they will own and possess it. They will possess the whole earth for him.

Every detail of the training we receive from his word makes us effective in the responsibility of inheriting and possessing the earth that he is going to give us ownership of. God has this purpose of sonship, and that purpose is why we now have the church and the training of the church. It makes us effective in functioning in the family of God, but it is also critical to the fulfillment of purpose. So when I am in church, my mindset is, "I am here to fulfill purpose." That is why I am in church.

As definite as salvation is, the choice of a local church is also absolute and definite. We can identify whether someone is making a right or wrong choice of a local church, just as we can identify someone who is rightly saved or wrongly assumes he is saved. Some people wrongly assume they are in the right local church, while others are rightly in a local church. That scriptural fact rests on the premise of the perfecting of the saints by the faith. Are you being perfected by the unity of the faith when you say you are in church?

If the answer is no, you are not rightly in a local church. You might want to argue about "the right local church," which I do not like to say, but that is what it is in this case. The decision must be crystal clear. Your choice of a local church is based on the singular reason for which churches exist: discipleship by all the things the Lord has commanded, the entire doctrine, or the perfecting of the saints by the unity of the faith. Once we identify what the church is for, we can say, "I have been saved and taken the first step into purpose."

Now I am supposed to grow spiritually by committing to a local church where I will be discipled by the unity of the faith. I must value discipleship for all areas of my life. Ephesians 4:15. Let me go backwards a bit. Verse 13 spoke about coming to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God. Scripture is big on knowledge; God is particular about knowledge. I said that knowledge, though it is toward the end of a work, is first an end in itself and then a means to another end. You must first know, because the end of knowledge itself is the growth within that knowledge.

You must know. You have that knowledge as your growth. Then, with the knowledge you have as your growth, there are expressions you should have. But first you must grow, which means having that knowledge. God wants you to grow and mature by knowing his doctrine entirely, in the manner and outline in which he has laid it out. A believer should be able to say, "I am in the process of fully knowing God's word. I am engaging God's word to know it fully.

I am involved in that." Of course, that means the church must carry out the responsibility of presenting God's word in a way and outline in which the believer can fully receive it. That is what we have endeavored to do to a fair extent here, and we are still endeavoring to do. The teachings are laid out for you in an outline. There is the Single Teaching Series, the Basic Discipleship Outline, and the Complete Discipleship Outline. They are laid out so that you can say, "I have gone through this.

I have been able to put this together. I am learning it, no doubt, but I can say I have an awesome understanding of the gospel." That is Christianity. That is the faith. There must be an ambition to say first, "I have thoroughly learned the word," before you talk about relearning it. I am concerned that some people do not have that ambition to say, "I have learned God's word, and I am relearning it." It is a goal. It is an intention. It is what ought to be and what the apostles spoke about.

They expected to present people perfect in Christ and fully teach the word of God. They understood their duty to perfect the saints to the unity of the faith, and they wanted that to happen. On the recipient's part, there must be the same desire: "I want to be presented perfect. I want to be fully taught the word." There was that boast made about Timothy: "You have fully known my doctrine." Your pastor or leader should be able to make that boast about you.

You are hungry for it. You are trudging after it. You are going for it. First comes the knowledge that produces growth by itself, before we start talking about the work or expression that the knowledge will also bring forth. The knowledge must be an end in itself. "I just need to know first." That must be a goal in itself. "I need to know. I have gone long enough not knowing, staying at this length and not going the whole distance. It is time I fully know the word. So I am going to sit down and commit."

Follow through with the whole teaching of God's word through the STS, the BDO, the CDO, the workers' course, correspondence course, and leadership, as the case may be. "I am going to go through it." That is the discipline and training of a believer in the word: to know.

The ambition must be to know the doctrine entirely in the way God presented it, not merely to collect isolated ideas. You should be able to say, "I have gone through this teaching. I have gone through that one. I have put the message together. I am still learning, but I have an awesome understanding of the gospel." That is not pride; it is the goal the apostles stated. Paul wanted to present every man perfect in Christ. He labored to teach the word of God fully. The leaders Christ gave to the church are to perfect the saints until all come to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God.

The recipient must want the same thing. "I want to be presented perfect in Christ. I want to be fully taught the word. I do not want to remain at the same length of understanding for years." Paul could say of Timothy, "You have carefully followed my doctrine, manner of life, purpose, faith, longsuffering, love, perseverance." Timothy had fully known Paul's doctrine. There should be something like that which a pastor or leader can say about you, because you have been hungry for the word, trudging after it, reviewing it, and learning it again.

Knowledge is first an end in itself. Before we discuss visible work and expression, there is a maturity that consists in knowing. God wants his children to know him, know the gospel, and know the outline in which one truth fits another. Then that same knowledge becomes a means to another end: it supplies the steps of the walk. But do not hurry past the first end. You must know. You cannot continually say, "I do not understand that doctrine," while refusing the disciplined process by which it is taught.

That is why the church must present the word in a form believers can follow through. There is the Single Teaching Series, the Basic Discipleship Outline, the Complete Discipleship Outline, workers' training, correspondence, and leadership training. The arrangement is not an administrative ornament. It enables a believer to follow the whole teaching, join the pieces, and come to stability. You do not merely hear whichever message happens to interest you today. You submit to an outline and let the word bring you to the marked point.

Ephesians 4:14 describes the result: we are no longer children tossed to and fro and carried about with every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men, in cunning craftiness and deceitful plotting. A child can be pushed in a new direction every week by whatever idea is trending. One week it is a temperament theory. Another week it is a scheme for power. Another week it is a psychological slogan. But a believer perfected by the unity of the faith can judge those claims by truth. The knowledge itself gives stability before it produces any other work.

So say, "I have gone long enough without knowing. I have gone long enough staying at this level and stopping halfway. It is time to know the word fully. I will sit down, commit, follow the teaching, review what I have heard, and ask questions where I do not understand." That decision is a spiritual discipline. It is part of the believer's training in the word. Knowledge is not an optional interest for a few people who like study; it defines the maturity to which Christ is bringing the whole church.

## Knowledge Must Produce a Walk

We saw that knowledge in Ephesians 4:14, where he says you will no longer be a child tossed to and fro. There is the possibility of being tossed to and fro, particularly relative to this teaching on relationships. As I have said, this teaching is not only about marital relationships. Marital relationships will show up in their own place, no doubt. The Bible talks about marital relationships in their proper place, and we will get to marital matters in the faith of Christ.

We said we must be careful about psychological opinions that are simply thrown around and given to us. You see one book full of psychological jargon and theories, and the person writes a name like "Professor Robert Scoffield, PhD." Then you see a commentary: "The best book I ever read on how the mind works." Robert Scoffield is confusing you. Or it is another supposed expert whose opinion will mislead you. You must be wary. Sometimes people have said there is a dominant trait.

There is a dominant one and a secondary one. I also saw the book *The 48 Laws of Power*. Nothing in it is plain, straightforward, sweet, or smooth. Everything is orchestrated and unreal. "Never outshine the master." If you live by that, you watch and scrutinize people. Someone does something bad and you will not talk; you just smile. People manipulate themselves that way, even inside marriages. "This person is not taking me seriously again. I know what to do." You become very busy. "Why have you not been available?" "I have been busy."

"Now you will value and appreciate me. You will do what I say." That is a believer being tossed to and fro. Do not try it. Do not be children tossed by winds of doctrine or by that kind of rubbish. What makes it worse is finding believers who appreciate it and say, "You do not really understand." Look at it. What is there not to understand? I have found that most sane people reject it. I saw a thread where some people who had read it said they had to detox from it.

But I also saw people defending it. The thread said you could never trust anybody who swears by *The 48 Laws of Power*. Some replied, "You do not understand; it is good for corporate life." It is not good for anything. There are places where politics play out, but there is a genuineness to life by which you can still do what you need to do effectively while remaining genuine, and even navigate politics to your advantage. But that sinister approach to living can never be normal. It is not even normal for someone who is not born again.

You are going to the devil's level. You do not merely want to be bad at a normal human level; you want to reach the devil's level of being bad. That is too much. You have become too sinister. I even doubt the man who wrote the book lives by it. Do not be tossed to and fro by every doctrine, by the trickery of men in cunning craftiness and deceitful plotting. Do not buy into the lie. I accept that there are different personalities, but in scripture we have the same character. Our personalities might differ. Things that excite us, things that put us off, and cultural leanings can form our personalities.

But character is the same. There is, for example, the personality of being very ambitious. That is not a Christian or non-Christian thing; it is likely a tendency. Another person prefers to keep things even. As long as you are personally responsible, that is personality. But character is the same. It is important that we know this and make the proper distinctions.

I have had people try to fit me into those temperament categories. One person would say I was phlegmatic; another would say melancholic; another would mix in choleric because I was quiet but strong-willed. At A-levels, a man kept looking at me in class because I was very quiet. I could sit without saying anything, and he seemed to wonder what I was planning. I was not planning anything; I was simply looking. If the labels can be rearranged until every person becomes two or three categories at once, they are not a safe definition of Christian character.

The terminology itself used to sound strange to me. Someone would say, "You are melancholic," and I would answer that I was not "melan" anything; I was a man. Another would say, "You are phlegmatic," and then add choleric because one label supposedly could not explain everything. The word choleric even sounded toxic to me, as though something chloric had entered the person. The point of the humor was that I did not want to receive a strange label as my identity simply because a book had divided humanity into four boxes.

People then explained that one temperament was dominant and another subordinate. That flexibility made the scheme seem able to absorb every contradiction. If you were quiet, that proved one category. If you were quiet but determined, another category was added. If you laughed in a different situation, a third explanation appeared. At that point the believer can spend more time interpreting himself through the scheme than affirming and learning the character supplied in Christ.

My father had one of those books, and I remember wondering whether the people who recommended it had actually read everything in it. The descriptions often felt orchestrated rather than natural, as though a person had to perform a type. Nothing was simple or straightforward. Yet believers would repeat the labels confidently because a professor or psychologist had written letters after his name. That is precisely how people become tossed by the opinions of men.

I am not denying observable differences. The error is making the category lord over the word. If the book says your type avoids people, but scripture says to be hospitable and friendly, the step of Christ governs you. If the book says your type is naturally controlling, but scripture says love does not seek its own, the character of love governs you. If the book says your type stores resentment, but scripture teaches forgiveness, you do not protect resentment as personality. You learn the exact steps of your nature in Christ.

There may be a dominant tendency and a secondary tendency. There may be differences in energy, sociability, ambition, caution, humor, preferred activities, and the way people process events. I admit those differences. One person likes to talk; another is quiet. One likes sports; another does not. One is easily excited by a new project; another wants to examine it slowly. Culture, family background, education, and repeated experiences can all contribute to personality. But none of those things changes the character supplied by the Spirit.

You cannot say, "I am suspicious because that is my temperament," or, "I manipulate people because that is how my personality works." Suspicion, deceit, bitterness, uncontrolled anger, and manipulation are character issues. The believer has the same spiritual character: love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Personality gives a slant to expression; it does not excuse the flesh. If a psychological category tells you that bad character is fixed as your identity, reject the category at that point.

That is the danger in books that teach calculated power. "Never outshine the master" sounds clever until you begin to imagine every relationship as a competition. Then you watch the other person, hide what you think, smile while keeping a secret reply, and wait for a strategic moment. Someone offends you, but instead of having a truthful conversation, you pretend nothing happened and begin arranging a counter-move. That is not wisdom. It is a sinister disposition toward life.

The same scheme enters marriage. A person thinks, "My spouse no longer takes me seriously. I know what to do. I will become unavailable." The spouse asks, "Why have you not been around?" and the person replies, "I have been busy," while secretly trying to create anxiety and increase perceived value. The goal is not honest communication or service; it is to make the other person submit. That is manipulation, even if a popular book calls it emotional intelligence or corporate strategy.

Some people defend such ideas by saying they are useful in political or corporate environments. There are certainly situations in which you must understand incentives, alliances, and the politics of a workplace. But you can understand those realities and still be genuine. You can act wisely without becoming sinister. You can negotiate, protect proper interests, and recognize a person's motives without treating everybody as an enemy to be used. Scripture makes you wise as a serpent and harmless as a dove; it does not make you a schemer.

Even many people who read those books recognize the damage. Some say they had to detox from the mindset afterward. The frightening part is finding believers who swear by the system and insist, "You do not understand it." We understand enough to judge the fruit. If it trains you to scrutinize, manipulate, conceal, and use people, it is contrary to the steps of Christ. Do not be tossed to and fro by it.

The truth is that different personalities can all express the same character. An ambitious person can be loving, honest, patient, and responsible. A quiet person can be warm and friendly without becoming loud. An energetic person can exercise self-control. A cautious person can walk in faith rather than suspicion. Do not confuse personality with character, and do not let a human theory tell you that the flesh is simply who you are. We grow up into Christ in all things.

## Growing Up into Christ in Every Area

Ephesians 4:15 is key. He says, "But, speaking the truth in love, we grow up into him in all things." There are different areas of our lives in which we are supposed to grow up into him. In our material pursuits, we are supposed to grow up into him. Paul shows us that we are supposed to imitate him in material pursuits. There are steps he takes that pertain to his nature in that area. Am I taking the steps he takes in material pursuits? What about character? Am I taking the steps he takes?

What about my spiritual purpose? Am I taking his steps concerning healing and the demonstration of the things of the Spirit? Then there is this all-important and very large area: relationships. If you consider the number of verses Paul spends on relationships in the believer's walk, it is on the larger side. From Ephesians 5:22 to 6:9 he is talking about relationships: wives and husbands, parents and children, and employees and employers.

## The Relationships Believers Have

We saw the relationships we need to have, or are bound to have, as believers. In relationships in the faith of Christ, there are relationships common to humanity and others particular to the faith. We said relationships include the following persons or classes of people. First is God. Then there is the spousal relationship. I used Genesis, which is why the order is like this. Man's relationships begin with God, followed by the spousal relationship. Then we saw the parent-child relationship: a man leaves his father and mother.

He is a child of a particular father and mother. Then we saw siblings, at least a similar kind of relationship, in Cain and Abel. Then we saw the saints in Seth and those who called upon the name of the Lord. We can speak of local church members, those within your immediate circle of believers in the body of Christ. Then we saw leaders. Within local church membership, core friendships can arise and develop over time. They can come from among the saints generally, but particularly from local church membership.

So there are local church members; seventh, leaders; and eighth, secular relationships, including both colleagues and leaders. Ninth, we said acquaintances. Since we also want to mention core friendship, we can put acquaintances and strangers in one group and mention core friendship last. There are people who are acquaintances and others who are strangers. Then we can mention core friendships. I use "core" the way people use it to describe things such as core leadership or senior leadership.

That is your main domain. I do not want to use "spiritual relationship" here because that is a different matter. Leadership in church can be a spiritual relationship to you without being a core friendship. But people can have their own core friendship circle. These, then, are the relationships we are supposed to have. Because relationships are a major issue in this discussion, we said we had to examine them. We looked at them last year and saw the roles we are supposed to have in relationships.

Let us keep the categories clear because each one carries its own proximity and responsibility. Relationship with God stands first. Then there is the spousal relationship, where two people share a union that affects nearly every area of life. There is the parent-child relationship in both directions: children receive care, training, and inheritance, while parents eventually receive honor and care from mature children. There is sibling relationship, where people share family history and responsibility whether or not their personalities naturally align.

There are the saints generally and the members of your local church particularly. The universal body of Christ is real, but the local church is the circle in which you repeatedly gather, know people, serve, receive correction, and carry responsibility. You cannot use affection for distant believers as an excuse to remain cold toward the people sitting in your unit or extension. Local proximity gives friendship concrete work to do.

There are leaders in the church. A leader may have a spiritual responsibility toward you without being one of your core friends. Your role is still not cold formality. You receive, honor, communicate, support, and cultivate appropriate affection without confusing the leader's assignment or demanding a degree of disclosure that belongs elsewhere. The leader also relates to the people as more than a lecturer; pastoral work involves real affection.

There are secular relationships, including colleagues and leaders in school, business, government, and work. You share tasks, deadlines, pressures, and outcomes with those people. That repeated activity gives you opportunities to be friendly and to cultivate proper bonds. A believer should not be the obnoxious colleague who claims to care only about spiritual things while making ordinary work difficult for everyone else.

There are acquaintances and strangers. An acquaintance is someone you know to some degree; a stranger is someone you have not known before. Neither category removes the duty of affection. The expression differs because knowledge, trust, and commitment differ, but friendliness begins before close knowledge. Jesus could receive, converse with, and help a stranger without pretending that the stranger occupied the same relational place as the Twelve.

Finally, there are core friendships, committed bonds formed through agreement, shared persuasion, shared purpose, conversation, trust, activity, and time. Not everybody will occupy that place. The distinction is not an excuse to be unfriendly toward everyone outside the circle. It is the reason we say friendliness with all and friendship with some. In every category the believer's high point is friendship: a friendly disposition toward all and committed affection where agreement and purpose call for it.

The role the believer is supposed to have in every relationship is friendship, subdivided into friendliness with all and friendship with some. That is the high point of relationship: to be friendly and to be a friend. Some say, "We just coexist together." No, that is a bad example of a believer. I saw arguments on Facebook that I read almost for entertainment value. A woman said she wanted to cut her hair and had to get permission from her husband.

People asked, "Why should you get permission from your husband? That is not equality." I do not want to derail my focus here, but do not be foolish about these things. I remember when my wife told me how she liked my hair. I had cut it lower than we typically cut it because I had seen people cutting their hair very low. I cut my hair very low, and I thought it was okay.

I thought I looked good, as I should, but if your wife says she prefers something else, you should consider it. In this case she was my wife, and I agreed with what she wanted me to do. The point is that it happens on both sides. When it is the man asking the woman, people call it subjection and say many things, but there are compromises each party makes for the other. Some people did not like their husbands having beards, told them so, and the husbands changed just because of them. People immediately wonder, "What if my spouse says they do not want this beard I have kept for years?" I have not yet said what the answer is, but you cannot make a significant change in a close relationship without considering the other person. If you say, "I can just do what I think," you are looking for trouble. You do not have a good sense of how such a relationship should work. Of course, you can do your hair normally and tell the person because the person expects that of you.

You could even do it as a surprise, so the person says, "You are looking fine today." But if you suddenly do a skin cut without warning, you may be looking for war unless it is for health reasons. Let us be wise. Some people like arguments about things that do not make sense. Even as a man, if you want to make such a drastic change, consider the other person. Does the person think it is okay? People can feel embarrassed because of you. The person's sense of dignity is involved, so why would you just do it without consideration?

You might not mean anything by it, but it might mean something to the other person. Let us leave that. I do not want war in this teaching; I want peace. I was talking about friendliness and friendship. We began to see from the last session that there was a reason for the first part of this teaching.

The emphasis was that our role as believers in relationships is friendship: friendliness and friendship. I began to realize that we had not actually explained its expression or practice, which is the end goal of all Christian teaching. How exactly are we supposed to walk in those things? People have the sense, "I am supposed to be friends with them," but what are they supposed to do? Some get it wrong by overdoing the wrong things until it becomes wrong, while others do nothing because they do not know the right thing to do.

That gap remains from the earlier teaching and needs to be addressed. When we teach what the steps should be, we can judge whether someone is walking in them. "This is not it. This is not the step to take here. This is the step to take."

We began to examine the fact that the believer's walk is the endpoint of training in the church. That is why the next thing Paul says in verse 17 is, "This I say, therefore, and testify in the Lord, that you should no longer walk as the rest of the Gentiles walk." If you see the word "therefore," you must find out what it is there for. It is a conclusion from a premise already made. From the premise of the perfecting of the saints, your walk has to change. The Bible is very big on the word "walk."

We saw the word "walk" used about eight times in Ephesians. Ephesians 2:2 says, "in which you once walked." Ephesians 2:10 says, "We are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them." Notice that the works were prepared beforehand. The steps to take are already on the ground: take this one, then this one, then this one. There are already steps pertaining to your nature, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them. Walk in them now.

We saw Ephesians 2:10. Ephesians 4:1 says you should walk worthy of the calling with which you were called. Ephesians 4:17 says no longer to walk as the Gentiles walk. Ephesians 5:2 says, "Walk in love, as Christ also has loved us and given himself for us as a sacrifice." Ephesians 5:8 says to walk in the light, and Ephesians 5:15 says to walk exactly. By "exactly," I mean that there are definite steps. Then we began to examine the word *peripateo*. It means to walk about or walk through a place: *peri*, around or through, and *pateo*, to tread a path.

It means walking through a particular path by taking definite steps across that path in a particular way. You are taking definite steps, so you watch your step. Walking in the flesh is also taking definite steps, but in another way. Every time you walk in the flesh, you have taken steps after another nature. What steps did you take? You have to look at them and judge them. "Did I take the right step here? Did I take the right step there? What is the right step in this situation?"

What is the right step in that situation? Always know the right step. Sometimes you can find yourself having a misstep. You stepped out of the Spirit, stepped into the flesh, and took steps in the flesh. You must identify where it happened and what that step was. It might have been a step in your thoughts: "I first saw this; then it led to this, then to that, and then to something else." You must know the step. What are the steps supposed to be?

"I am supposed to do this, then see this in this way, and then do that." They are exact steps. We also saw the word "walk" used alongside the word "imitate," to mimic. *Mimetes*, from *mimos*, means to replicate. You are replicating what you have seen.

## The Exact Steps of the Believer

In the last part of the previous session, we focused on the word *peripateo*, showing its use across scripture and saying that there are exact steps we ought to take. What steps ought we to take in relationships? We have clearly seen that, in the faith of Christ, our role in relationships is friendship. Therefore, the steps we are meant to take are steps of friendship, or more specifically, steps of friendliness and friendship. To see and appreciate those steps, we must recall and properly examine what friendliness and friendship mean.

We are to take steps of friendliness and friendship, not steps of being obnoxious, suspicious, or untrusting. They are steps of friendliness and friendship.

The language of steps makes the teaching practical. It is not enough to affirm, "Friendship is my nature in Christ," while having no idea what to do when a person enters the room, when a colleague needs help, when a friend disagrees with you, or when a stranger asks a question. Nature supplies the capacity, but scripture also shows the path. At each point you ask, "What is the next step of Christ here?"

A misstep can begin before any outward action. It may begin in a thought: you interpret a person's silence as hostility, rehearse an accusation, then withdraw, then answer coldly, and finally create a conflict. When you review what happened, do not look only at the final argument. Identify the first step. "I saw the situation this way; that thought led to this feeling; that feeling led to this word; that word led to this response." Walking in the flesh also follows a path one step at a time.

Walking in the Spirit means judging those steps by the pattern already prepared. Perhaps the first step is to believe the best rather than assume evil. The next is to ask a clear question instead of nursing suspicion. The next is to listen, speak truth in love, and respond with patience. In another situation, the right step may be to reject a proposal firmly because friendship cannot join rebellion. Exactness does not mean every relationship receives the same response; it means every response follows the nature and wisdom of Christ.

That is why *peripateo* was used with imitation. You watch how Christ acted toward sinners, disciples, religious hypocrites, parents, friends, betrayers, and strangers. You also watch faithful believers such as Paul. Then you replicate the pattern. You do not invent a private version of Christian relationship and say, "This is simply how I do my own." You take the step shown in the word.

Sometimes the step is small: greet the person, ask how they are, sit with them, answer their message, remember what they told you, rejoice over their good, or offer practical help. Sometimes it is weighty: disclose a purpose, correct rebellion, carry a burden, risk your safety, or remain present in adversity. Whether small or weighty, the action is not vague sentiment. It is a definite step of affection.

So watch your steps. Ask after a conversation, "Did I take the step Christ would take? Did I speak as a friend? Did I become obnoxious, suspicious, or unavailable? Did I overdo something until it became wrong, or did I do nothing because I was afraid of doing too much?" The perfecting of the saints gives enough knowledge to judge the walk, correct a misstep, and return to the path.

## God Relates to Us as a Friend

Let us emphasize that the believer's role in relationships is friendship. Look at James chapter 2. If we ask what God's relationship with us is, there are several things we can say. God is our leader. God is Father to us. You can say God is husband or partner, like a marital partner. You can also say God is brother to us.

Jesus is not ashamed to call us brethren: God became a man to be our brother. Is God also a fellow member with us? Jesus is the head of the body, but we are also called joint heirs with him. God fits the role in every area of relationship. But what is he at the high point of all those relationships? A friend. Whether the relationship is spousal, parental, sibling, employer-employee, or something else, he has functioned in those roles.

Has he played the role of a child? In Luke 2, Jesus submitted to his parents. Even when he was going to die, he said, "Woman, behold your son," taking responsibility for his mother. We have seen him function in all these roles, and in all of them he functions as a friend.

Go to James 2:23. It says, "The scripture was fulfilled which says, 'Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.' And he was called the friend of God." "I am a friend of God; he calls me friend" was one of the songs from my early born-again days. The point is that scripture calls Abraham the friend of God.

Some people object that Abraham was not a friend of God and say there is only one Old Testament text that calls him God's friend. That text is 2 Chronicles 20:7. It is a prophetic commentary on Abraham's life and narrative, a relationship that was readily witnessed. I sometimes wonder why people pick at every issue, including things that are not necessary.

For example, you cannot wish away the reality of a personal relationship with God in scripture. I am not talking about every bit of nonsense people call a personal relationship with God, but God does seek personal relationship. There is something called being a friend of God. God is not your boyfriend, but God is your friend. There is personal relationship with God. There is friendship with God. I am a friend of God. Go and ask God; he will tell you.

Second Chronicles 20:7 says, "Are you not our God, who drove out the inhabitants of this land before your people Israel, and gave it to the descendants of Abraham your friend forever?" It is a commentary, but they saw the relationship. Your friend for how long? Forever.

## Ahab: Affection and Friendship

Your friend forever. We see Abraham called the friend of God. The Hebrew word used here is *ahab*, A-H-A-B. Quoting Strong's, it means "to have affection for, sexually or otherwise." It is used for loving the Lord: "You shall love the Lord your God." So it is not merely sexual, though it can be used in that sense. In Genesis 22:2, it means to have affection for. Notice that definition: to have affection for.

That is what it means to call someone your friend: you have affection for the person. If you are friendly, you are affectionate toward someone. You might not have a committed bond of affection because you do not know or like the person that much, but you can still be affectionate toward the person. Perhaps the person slips, stumbles, or falls, and you feel for them. You show affection. Genesis 22:2 says, "Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love." Abraham was affectionate toward him. He was friends with his son; they shared an affectionate bond.

Genesis 24:67 says Isaac brought Rebekah into his mother Sarah's tent, took her, and she became his wife, and he loved her. We have seen *ahab* in a parental relationship; now we see it in a spousal relationship. Isaac had been grieving for his mother, but now he found a bond and loved Rebekah. You might suggest the word refers merely to sexual relations, but the text says Isaac was comforted after his mother's death. It keeps contrasting this relationship with his relationship to his mother, which points to the kind of affection he had.

He found someone for whom he now had that kind of affection: a friendship. I used to wonder why the text mentioned his mother rather than his father. But Abraham was the one who loved his son Isaac, and by then Abraham had died. There is also that family politics in which fathers try to be liked by their children and allow the mothers to do the difficult work, so the children tend to like the fathers while the mothers sort everything out.

The father just says, "What do you need?" while the mother is correcting and disciplining. Let us leave that. Genesis 25:28 says, "Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob." That is affection. The text does not deny that they were more friendly with one child than the other. Yet the person Isaac preferred was not the person to whom God gave the covenant responsibility. Your affection does not determine God's assignment or supernatural responsibility.

I recently saw something from Michelle Obama on her brother's birthday. She called him "the most loved child of our mother." I laughed because I thought people always tried to hide such things. What I liked was that she did not seem to feel any kind of way about it. Her mother had apparently expressed that she liked the brother more, and Michelle could mention it without resentment.

People often say, "I do not like one child more," because they want to avoid sibling rivalry. I used to feel like the overlooked second child in my house. I do not know how true it was, and perhaps I used it to blackmail them to some degree. But there is often a special bond with the first child and another with the last. I was in the middle, simply one of the children who was there.

Over time, I think the case has changed, but that is another discussion. Let us return to Genesis 27:4. This issue of friendliness is about affection. Saying, "I love everybody in Christ," is not what we are talking about. That by itself is not friendship. "Love is patient; love is kind" is important, and you need that love in friendship, but it is not the same thing as friendship.

The expressions of love are part of the steps. Love is patient and kind; it will be present. But friendship is specifically about developing affection, giving affection until you have bonds of affection. Look at Genesis 27:4, 9, and 14. Isaac says, "Make me savory food, such as I love." It is about what you like. People may ask, "Which do you like better: doughnuts, meat pie, or chicken pie?" Those who know me know the answer is doughnuts. Adding "chicken" to pie does not change my preference.

There is something about a doughnut that I cannot explain. I would even prefer puff-puff to chicken pie. I also have affection for shawarma, but it is not like my affection for doughnuts. Doughnuts are a regular love. Shawarma is something you do not want every day; perhaps fifty-two times a year, once every week. Let me move on. The phrase is "such as I love." Look at verse 9.

Rebekah says, "Go now to the flock and bring me from there two choice kids of the goats, and I will make savory food from them for your father, such as he loves." She knew the food her husband loved and could make it for him. That was part of the affection in their relationship. Let us say that for the single ladies and move on. Look at Genesis 29:18: Jacob loved Rachel. That is not merely loving everybody in Christ.

It is affection. Jacob said, "I will serve you seven years for Rachel your younger daughter." That love was much: seven years, and then it became fourteen years for one person. I do not understand that kind of love. But an agreement is an agreement. After Rachel died, we do not hear that Jacob married again. I like Jacob; he is a strange kind of person. Abraham, even at his age after Sarah died, married Keturah. Let me leave that for marital matters. Look at verse 30.

"Then Jacob also went in to Rachel, and he also loved Rachel more than Leah." In verse 32, Leah conceived and bore a son and called his name Reuben, saying, "The Lord has surely looked on my affliction. Now therefore my husband will love me." But he did not have that affection toward her. Affection can be biased; it is what it is. Genesis 34:3 says Shechem's soul was strongly attracted to Dinah the daughter of Jacob, and he loved the young woman. Now look at Genesis 37:3-4.

To be someone's friend is to have affection for the person. Calling Abraham God's friend means God had affection for him and Abraham had affection for God. Genesis 37:3-4 says Israel loved Joseph more than all his children. That affection was biased. Are you supposed to show such a preference? I do not think so. Some of the patriarchs were extreme and too expressive about it. You should at least be equal in how you show love, even though you may have preferences.

The key is to be fair. Friendship is what it is, and affection is what it is. There are things about someone you may simply tend to like. The person does something you appreciate, or you relate more easily. That does not mean the person is better in God's purpose. You simply tend to relate more. There is a tendency to develop affection; it is not automatic. Both this word and the other word we are going to see are process words. They describe what is happening, not a fixed nature.

It happens that you like this person. Joseph was the first child of the wife Jacob loved, the wife he had long expected to give birth. Naturally and humanly, there was likely to be some extra sentiment. Verse 4 says, "When his brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, they hated him and could not speak peaceably to him." Now look at Genesis 44:20.

It says, "We have a father, an old man, and a child of his old age, who is young. His brother is dead, and he alone is left of his mother's children, and his father loves him." That is Benjamin. You see the same affection transferred to Benjamin. See Exodus 20:6 and 21:5. Exodus 20:6 says God shows mercy to thousands, to those who love him and keep his commandments. Can we say those who love him are also friends with him? Yes, that can be said.

When scripture says Abraham is God's friend, it means he is one toward whom God is affectionate, as Abraham is affectionate toward God. Exodus 21:5 says, "But if the servant plainly says, 'I love my master, my wife, and my children; I will not go out free.'" The servant would normally leave at the point of freedom and get on with his life. But if he says, "I love my master," and decides to stay, he has developed affection and a close relationship with his master. That is friendship. It is not automatic; there are reasons for it, and it is a process.

Something caused it. It is not merely nature; it is a process in which something is involved.

## Rea and Cultivated Relationship

Now let us see another word used for friendship in the relationship between Moses and God. Exodus 33:11 says, "So the Lord spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend. And he would return to the camp, but his servant Joshua the son of Nun, a young man, did not depart from the tabernacle." It is a comparative expression, but it reflects the friendship relationship going on between them.

The word for "friend" in this case is *rea*, R-E-A, which we looked at in the last teaching. It is derived from *ra'ah*. There is another similar word that can mean to see or foresee, but that is a different word. This *ra'ah* means to shepherd: to tend a flock, pasture it, or graze it. The shepherd tends and pastures the flock, and the flock grazes as a result.

That is *ra'ah*. When someone is called a *rea*, the person is someone with whom you cultivate a relationship. You tend to the relationship; you pasture it. You are friends because you have done that work. By extension, *ra'ah* means to associate with. I am still quoting Strong's: to tend a flock, pasture it, or graze, and by extension to associate with. I add the idea of attending to something. You give the relationship the attention it requires.

You give something what you are supposed to give it; you attend to it. *Rea* means an associate, more close or less close. That is Strong's language. So there is friendliness and there is friendship. Friendliness is a less close situation, but you are still attending to someone affectionately. In friendship you attend to someone more closely, with commitment. In Exodus 33:11, the word can mean a companion, fellow, or another of your kind, someone with whom you do things.

The first occurrence is Genesis 11:3. I find that interesting. It is as though scripture gives us a major lesson by letting many first examples be negative, warning us about the potential mishandling of association. The first spousal relationship, Adam and Eve, did not end well. The relationship was supposed to further purpose, but it negated purpose when they rejected God's instruction. Relationships have sometimes opposed what purpose was meant to be. John Wesley, for example, had a terrible marital relationship and lamented it repeatedly.

Go to Genesis 11:3. "They said to one another" means each individual spoke to his colleague, companion, or friend. Relationship and friendship helped produce the rebellion in Genesis 11, just as the spousal relationship was involved in the rebellion in Genesis 3.

## Relationships Must Serve God's Purpose

Relationships go a long way in fulfilling or frustrating God's purpose, and the closer the relationship, the greater the effect. Genesis 11 concerns friendship; Genesis 3 concerned a spouse. You generally cannot escape proximity with a spouse. So if you marry someone, or become closely committed to someone, who will not encourage you to fulfill God's plan, that relationship can discourage you from following God's plan, distract you from it, weaken your resolve, and fill you with fear, doubt, and questions.

Instead of giving you boldness to fulfill God's plan, it gets you into trouble. Relationship is about more than emotional or sexual companionship. Relationships are for purpose. I have heard examples such as Kenneth and Gloria Copeland and other ministers with their wives. One minister spoke about a time he was sick and weak before a meeting. His wife was quite forceful, which may not be how everybody should act, but she refused to let his weakness cancel obedience. She carried him to the bathroom and bathed him.

She dressed him, took him to the meeting, and left him there, saying, "If you want to die, die, but we are going to obey God." He had complained that he was sick, weak, and unable to go. She effectively told him, "You know enough of God's word not to die. But even if you want to die, you still have to obey God." That is a good friend. Their context was harsh and their faith was forceful, which not everyone should copy mechanically, but the friendship was directed toward purpose.

I remember complaining about ministry and lamenting over what was going on: "God, I do not know why we are going through this." My wife stood in front of me and said, "Devil, come out of him now." That is another forceful response, but the important point is clear: the reason for relationships is purpose. Do not get it wrong. Emotional and sexual companionship are not so unique that they cannot be found in many places. Do not exaggerate them. How many people have you liked in this life?

Some had a crush in primary school, another in junior secondary school, and another in senior secondary school. I had crushes in primary and secondary school, yet I never had a relationship anywhere. Attraction and emotional companionship are not enough to define purpose. Genesis 11:3 and verse 7 use the same relational word. God says, "Come, let us go down and there confuse their language, that they may not understand one another's speech." They would disagree about what they were discussing. God broke their friendship because friendship includes agreement. The separation was meant to stop their rebellion and return people toward God's purpose.

Even in primary school, a person could have a crush without knowing anything substantial about relationship. The crush might even know that you liked them, though nothing happened. Then in secondary school there could be another person, perhaps even two at different times. The emotions felt important in the moment, but they did not create a covenant, shared purpose, or enduring responsibility. By university, other people might show interest just when you are trying to focus on God and face your life. Attraction can come and go in several seasons.

I had perhaps one conversation that could have moved toward a relationship, and it eventually went nowhere. Later, seeing people I had once liked get married could produce the dramatic thought, "They have all left me behind. I alone remain, seeking the face of the Lord. Lord, send me my Rebekah." The humor exposes the exaggeration. Emotional desire is real, but it is not rare enough or reliable enough to become the marking scheme for a relationship. Purpose must remain the reason.

"No, this is not what we are supposed to do. We are supposed to follow God's purpose. I am not joining you in building another Babylon." Be careful about friendship, because every friend plays a purpose role in your life whether you like it or not. You must be able to ask, "What does this person mean to God's purpose and plan for my life? Am I getting better or worse? Am I becoming stronger or weaker? Am I being distracted or becoming more focused?"

Paul says in 1 Corinthians 7 that you cannot escape the distraction inherent in a marital relationship. Without it, your attention could be fully on something else. But despite that distraction, is there a work in the relationship that aids your focus? Does it make you do more and become more stirred to dream for the things of God? Do you have a shared passion? One unquestionable example is T. L. Osborn and Daisy. Ladonna was their daughter. T. L. and Daisy were passionate together and had the same heart for the lost.

They could preach in two different places at the same time in three-week campaigns. Through the year, sometimes they preached together and sometimes apart, but you saw the same passion in both of them. There are other examples like that. Believers must look at godly examples when it comes to friendship, not merely smile at worldly examples and use the wrong marking scheme. Some believers look at what people in the world are doing and use that as their measure. That is vain.

You say, "I like that relationship; their relationship worked out well." Who told you? What happened that makes you say a person's life worked out well? Did he become a billionaire? What will that mean from eternity's point of view? Did he further God's purpose? Is he saved? Is he growing spiritually? Did he give to the cause of the gospel? Did he reach the lost? Some people are billionaires and their wealth is a further testimony of God's message, work, and purpose.

But for other people, their wealth is not such a testimony. Do not use the wrong marking scheme. "We have a bond nobody can break. Nobody can come between us." That by itself proves nothing. Are you fulfilling God's purpose? The testimony should be, "Since we came together, look at how this person is doing more of God's will. Since I met this person, they have encouraged me." Ask anyone with whom I have been close in any form of committed conversation; that has to be the testimony.

Even if we have differences, the person should still be able to say, "Thank you; I became better." That is my concern. If we cannot be friends and you become better, then go and become better elsewhere. I will not keep a friendship that frustrates purpose. What do you think I talk about? What do you think I need my life to do? Of course, friendship does not mean every conversation must be about 1 Corinthians 12. There is room for ordinary things. That is all right; it is allowed.

There is a place for that. But let us be clear: relationships in the faith of Christ are to fulfill purpose. How you handle strangers is related to purpose. How you handle acquaintances, family members, siblings, and marital relationships is to fulfill purpose. You must know how to handle each relationship to fulfill purpose. That is the essence, and you do it by being friendly in all of them and a committed friend in some. Now return to Genesis 38:12: "In the process of time, the daughter of Shua, Judah's wife, died."

Judah was comforted and went up to his sheep shearers at Timnah, he and his friend Hirah the Adullamite. Instead of remarrying in peace, he went to do what nobody sent him to do. The point here is that he had a friend; they had cultivated a relationship. Exodus 11:2 uses the same word for a neighbor, someone who is "another" to you.

## Friendliness Toward Neighbors

The Hebrew word translated "neighbor" is actually the word for friend. You could translate it "your friend," someone toward whom you are supposed to be friendly. Anybody you call your neighbor is someone you should treat with friendliness. If you are in the same room, house, or immediate area with people, you should be friendly toward them. Exodus 11:2 says, "Speak now in the hearing of the people, and let every man ask his neighbor." See also Exodus 20:16-17.

The commandment says, "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor." Your neighbor is someone toward whom you are to be friendly, so do not bear false witness against them. These people may literally be strangers to you, but they fit the description of *rea*: people toward whom you should show friendliness. Someone may say, "Do not mind those people; they use drugs." They are still your neighbors, and you are supposed to be friendly toward them. That does not necessarily mean a committed friendship, but it does mean friendliness.

It is not true that there is a class of people toward whom you can never be friendly. Saying that is irresponsible for a believer. You are supposed to be friendly even toward a murderer. I am not saying you murder people together; I am saying you show the person's humanity proper affection. Exodus 20:17 says, "You shall not covet your neighbor's house." Exodus 21:14 says that if a man acts with premeditation against his neighbor to kill him by treachery, he is to be taken even from the altar. The person is still called a *rea*.

Deuteronomy 13:6 shows that even if your friend goes against Yahweh, the friend must be rejected. Under the old covenant it said to kill the person, which is not the application today, but rejection remains the principle. It says, "If your brother, the son of your mother, your son or your daughter, the wife of your bosom, or your friend who is as your own soul, secretly entices you, saying, 'Let us go and serve other gods,' which you have not known, neither you nor your fathers, of the gods of the people which are all around you, near to you or far off from you, from one end of the earth to the other end of the earth, you shall not consent to him or listen to him, nor shall your eye pity him, nor shall you spare him or conceal him."

It continues, "You shall surely kill him." Please do not kill anyone today; reject the person. Do not even conceal the rebellion. Look at the people listed: they are all people dear to you. The point is that you must not let affection make you participate in or cover terrible behavior. In a friendship you must be able to say, "No, I do not accept that. We cannot continue as friends in this direction." This is especially important in a marital relationship, where the bond is intended to last for life. You have to be very clear about what is nonnegotiable.

If I am moving in God's purpose and you refuse to come along, I will believe you have left. In a marriage, persistent rejection of that foundational purpose would amount to abandoning the union. Do not create room for what you should never accommodate. Now look at 2 Samuel 13:3: "But Amnon had a friend whose name was Jonadab." This is another bad friend. The remarkable thing is that Jonadab survived the disaster and was later still working around David's court.

When Absalom killed Amnon and the other sons were fleeing, Jonadab was with David and knew that only Amnon had been killed. That is a dangerous friend: he knew the plan and its outcome. In 2 Samuel 16:17, Absalom says to Hushai, "Is this your loyalty to your friend?" Hushai was pretending that he had betrayed David, whom Absalom called his friend. See also Psalm 35:14: "I paced about as though he were my friend or brother."

"I bowed down heavily, as one who mourns for his mother." He is talking about unfaithful friends who betrayed him. Verse 15 says, "But in my adversity they rejoiced and gathered together; attackers gathered against me, and I did not know it; they tore at me and did not cease." The people he thought were friends opposed him. Scripture speaks honestly about betrayal, but it does not tell us to abandon the disposition of friendliness. Psalm 88:18 says, "Loved one and friend you have put far from me, and my acquaintances into darkness." The word for acquaintances is related to *yada*, to know.

People you know are acquaintances; friends are those with whom you have cultivated affection. There is also being friendly, which means showing affection toward someone even before a committed bond exists. But friends are people with whom affection has been deliberately cultivated.

## The Wisdom of Proverbs on Friendship

Now let us see how Proverbs uses the word. Proverbs 6:1 says, "My son, if you become surety for your friend, if you have shaken hands in pledge for a stranger, you are snared by the words of your mouth." He is saying you should not enter a bond carelessly. Verse 3 says, "So do this, my son, and deliver yourself, for you have come into the hand of your friend: go and humble yourself; plead with your friend." If you have made a pledge, agreement, or contract, you must humble yourself and deal faithfully with it. Proverbs 17:17 says, "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity."

A friend is a *rea*, one with whom you have entered a committed affectionate relationship. I am not speaking only of marital affection, though a marital relationship should certainly be affectionate; its high point is still friendship. A friend loves at all times, not only in good times, and a brother is born for adversity. Verse 18 says, "A man devoid of understanding shakes hands in a pledge, and becomes surety for his friend." Do not be hasty in entering such a commitment.

Take note that a friend loves at all times, because another text makes a contrast with false kinds of friendship. Proverbs 18:24 says, "A man who has friends must himself be friendly, but there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother." Proverbs 19:6 says, "Many entreat the favor of the nobility, and every man is a friend to one who gives gifts." Is that truly a friend? He is saying people pretend to be friends with someone who gives gifts.

That is not necessarily friendship. People call someone "a nice guy" because he gives them things. That does not mean the giver should change his disposition or stop loving people. But if he is deceived by their flattery, he is the biggest fool of all. There are people who call me "Pastor, Pastor" because I used to give them something. I do not automatically believe such people are my friends. Some may genuinely be friends and have my interest at heart, but the gift-driven approach by itself proves nothing.

Others are simply not serious. Proverbs 22:11 says, "He who loves purity of heart and has grace on his lips, the king will be his friend." A leader seeks the friendship of people with sincerity, honor, purity, and gracious speech. Proverbs 27:9 says, "Ointment and perfume delight the heart, and the sweetness of a man's friend gives delight by hearty counsel." The sweetness of a friend appears in the affection he shows and in the counsel he gives. Conversation is already showing up in how friendship works.

Verse 14 says, "He who blesses his friend with a loud voice, rising early in the morning, it will be counted a curse to him." I do not fully know the context of that, so I will not force an explanation. Verse 17 says, "As iron sharpens iron, so a man sharpens the countenance of his friend." Friendship is meant to make you better, stronger, and more effective. God teaches us to relate to friends as he does. He presents himself as *rea*, one who loves affectionately.

He cultivates friendship and associates with us. Now let us see the major New Testament word for friendship. The expressions we have seen in all these passages are the expressions we should have as we take steps of friendship. What definite steps make these expressions happen toward a neighbor, a particular person, and God himself?

## Philos: Active Fondness

In the New Testament, the ideas expressed by *ahab* and *rea* in the Old Testament are largely subsumed in the words *philos* and *phileo*. *Philos* is the noun, and it leads to the verb *phileo*. The word *philos*, P-H-I-L-O-S, is used twenty-nine times in twenty-seven verses.

Strong's says it means a friend, one who is actively fond. The fondness is active; it is not merely the abstract honor of saying, "I value this." The person is present in the fondness you share. Suppose someone gives you a watch worth one million dollars. That amount gives the watch obvious value and honor even if you feel no personal fondness for it.

You do not need affection for the watch to honor it; it is worth one million dollars. But people in relationships may give each other a cheap bracelet bought from a roadside seller, with a small piece that says, "I love you." They say, "Keep this for my heart." In that emotionally intense moment, the recipient becomes happy and smiles over an ordinary bracelet.

If the bracelet gets lost, the person may search everywhere because of who gave it. The person is fond of it, not because the object itself deserves special honor. *Philos* is fondness; it can describe a friend, associate, or neighbor. The word concerns fondness whether the relationship is more or less close. Matthew 11:19 is key because it shows the Lord Jesus expressing friendliness and affection toward different classes of people.

## Jesus, a Friend of Tax Collectors and Sinners

"The Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, 'Look, a glutton and a winebibber, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!'" Jesus was friendly toward tax collectors and sinners. People saw it and commented on it. Any form of Christianity that makes you unfriendly toward sinners is improper. Scripture directs hostility toward brothers who deliberately and rebelliously walk in the flesh; delivering such a person to Satan is an adversarial act. If a professing believer embraces bad character in rebellion, I do not maintain friendship with that behavior.

What I have toward deliberate rebellion is adversity; *satan* means adversary. But someone who is not born again, or a believer walking in the flesh who does not know left from right or where to go, needs friendliness from believers. It was not incorrect when people called Jesus a friend of winebibbers, gluttons, tax collectors, and sinners in the sense that he was friendly toward them. He met them, ate with them, spoke with them, and was patient with them.

He was not out to condemn them, but he did not shy away from telling them what was wrong. He was friendly with them. Luke uses the word often. Luke 7:6 says, "Jesus went with them. And when he was already not far from the house, the centurion sent friends to him, saying to him, 'Lord, do not trouble yourself, for I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof.'" The centurion sent his friends to Jesus. Then look at verse 34.

"The Son of Man has come eating and drinking, and you say, 'Look, a glutton and a winebibber, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!'" That was how he related to them. In Luke 11:5-8, the Lord tells a parable about a friend: "Which of you shall have a friend, and go to him at midnight and say to him, 'Friend, lend me three loaves; for a friend of mine has come to me on his journey, and I have nothing to set before him'?" Someone toward whom you are affectionate has arrived, so you go to another person toward whom you are affectionate. These are cultivated relationships of fondness and affection.

The man answers from within, "Do not trouble me; the door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed. I cannot rise and give to you." Jesus says, "Though he will not rise and give to him because he is his friend, yet because of his persistence he will rise and give him as many as he needs." The parable teaches something about prayer and pressing through to the result in supplication, but it also assumes the ordinary reality of friends serving and receiving one another. Now look at Luke 12:4.

"And I say to you, my friends, do not be afraid of those who kill the body." Who was he calling friends? Verse 1 says an innumerable multitude had gathered, so that they trampled one another, and he began to speak first to his disciples. The title may extend to disciples among the multitude, but it is clearly addressed to disciples, not only the Twelve. He is teaching discipleship and calling the disciples his friends: "I say to you, my friends." Now look at Luke 14:10 and 12.

Our role in every relationship is to be friendly, and the exact steps we take are steps of friendliness and friendship. What are those exact steps? In every use of "friend" and "friendship," we see the cultivation of relationship. Attitude and disposition appear, but words and conversations are primary, including conversations about specific things. Why was Jesus called a friend of tax collectors? Because he spent time with them and conversed with them. We must ask what kind of conversations he had.

You cannot say, "I am also a friend of tax collectors," and begin discussing how to share a bribe. Find out what conversations Jesus was having. Tax collectors invited others and called him to a meal, and he brought the light of God's word. Friendship with strangers does not mean any conversation is proper. You may have disciples toward whom you are friendly who are, unfortunately, still using drugs. Someone may then say, "That man is a friend of those people."

In law school I was friendly with many people who were not living well. I do not deny it. I was friendly with them for every good reason, fully aware of who they were and what they did. I knew my role in their lives. You should be friendly toward people with the light of God's word against their darkness. That is the frame in which we read Luke 14:10 and 12.

He says, "When you are invited, go and sit down in the lowest place, so that when he who invited you comes he may say to you, 'Friend, go up higher.'" Then verse 12 says, "When you give a dinner or a supper, do not ask your friends, your brothers, your relatives, nor rich neighbors, lest they also invite you back, and you be repaid." Is Jesus saying you may never invite friends to dinner? No. He is teaching you to be charitable.

Do not let all your celebration and giving be directed only toward people from whom you know you will receive something back. That is not charity. If you want to show charity and love, include those who cannot repay you. Separate that from occasions when you simply invite your friends because you want to be with them. Do not read the Bible upside down. Now look at Luke 15:6, 9, and 29.

When the shepherd comes home, "he calls together his friends and neighbors, saying to them, 'Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep which was lost!'" In verse 9, the woman who found the coin says, "Rejoice with me." Friends rejoice together, and they also weep together. In verse 29, the older son says, "These many years I have been serving you; I never transgressed your commandment at any time, and yet you never gave me a young goat, that I might make merry with my friends." Luke 16:9 says, "Make friends for yourselves by unrighteous mammon, that when you fail, they may receive you into an everlasting home."

What was Jesus teaching in that parable? He was teaching about living your life for eternity, using present resources with the afterward outcome in view. Friendship is involved in how such a life is lived.

The friendship language in the parable may not seem direct, but the connection is still present. Now look at Luke 21:16, not verse 6: "You will be betrayed even by parents and brothers, relatives and friends; and they will put some of you to death." That still happens in parts of the world today. Luke 23:12 says, "That very day Pilate and Herod became friends with each other." They became friendly toward one another.

Previously they had been at enmity, hostile toward each other. Now they became friendly because they had a common purpose, though it was an evil purpose in this case. This shows that friendship forms around shared purpose. John 3:29 helps us see the practice of friendliness and friendship. In actual relationships we practice friendliness and friendship: rejoicing together, celebrating together, and also weeping together.

"He who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, rejoices greatly because of the bridegroom's voice." The friend rejoices greatly. They have affection toward one another. The practice of friendliness and friendship is therefore the practice of having affection and creating fondness among people. In John 11:11, Lazarus is the Lord's friend. Jesus had friendships with different people across the board. He developed bonds of affection with everyone with whom he was supposed to develop them.

Do not hear the word "fondness" and think only about a romantic relationship. I am talking about fondness in the church: affection toward a sister, a brother, a leader, a disciple, and the people with whom you serve. Some believers say, "I come, do my part, and go on my own." That is not a mature spiritual posture. You are supposed to develop fondness with the brethren. The body is joined and knit together, not merely seated in the same hall.

The purposes that will unfold from those bonds over the coming years may never happen if you refuse to cultivate them now. Imagine David without Jonathan. Jonathan's affection gave David warning and protection when Saul intended to kill him. Imagine Paul without Barnabas. Barnabas received him when other believers were afraid, brought him to the apostles, and helped place him within the work. God works through obedient relationships. Saying, "God would have found another way," does not excuse refusing the way his word tells you to walk.

You ought to have a bond of affection with everybody in your immediate local-church circle. That does not mean an identical committed friendship with everybody. It means no one in your unit should experience you as cold, suspicious, or entirely unknown. You have greeted this sister, spoken with that brother, served with another person, prayed together, discussed the word, rejoiced over progress, and learned enough to care intelligently.

As a church grows larger, the practical unit becomes important. You may not know every person in a large congregation equally, but you can know the people in your unit, extension, or service team. You can develop a level of connection and fondness with each one. You know something of what Sister Deborah is doing, what Brother Samuel is learning, and what Brother Timothy is trusting God to accomplish. You have had real conversations rather than simply exchanging names.

The initiative is on you. Do not say, "Nobody spoke to me," while making no move toward anyone. Jesus did not wait for tax collectors and sinners to become socially perfect before receiving them. Paul did not travel through cities without developing bonds. Take the initiative: greet, ask, listen, invite, serve, remember, and follow up. You are not trying to force instant intimacy; you are expressing affection and cultivating it honestly.

That cultivation also requires purity. Do not create a bond merely because you want influence, access, money, or a favor later. Do not pour calculated kindness on the ground so you can collect a debt in the future. The purpose is God's purpose, and the affection itself is genuine. If a practical favor later arises within the friendship, that is normal, but the friendship was not manufactured as a political investment.

You should be able to say, "There are people in church of whom I am genuinely fond. I rejoice when they rejoice. I care when they suffer. I want them to grow. I know how to encourage them toward God's will, and they can encourage me." That is not a distraction from spiritual purpose. It is one of the means through which purpose is fulfilled.

The initiative in this is always on you. Waiting for another person to begin while refusing to show affection is poor Christian character. John 11:11 says, "Our friend Lazarus sleeps, but I go that I may wake him up." Lazarus may first have been Jesus' friend, but the disciples also developed affection for him as they traveled and spent time together. They did not merely know facts about Lazarus; there were bonds of affection. You must develop such bonds.

"Let us go, that we may wake him up." Now John 15:13 is very key.

## Friendliness and Core Friendship

Here the Lord separates friendliness from committed friendship. We called him a friend of tax collectors and sinners because he was friendly toward them. Now he identifies those he considers core friends. John 15:13-15 says, "Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one's life for his friends. You are my friends if you do whatever I command you." He is not speaking only futuristically; he already calls them friends because they have followed him. "No longer do I call you servants, for a servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends."

"For all things that I heard from my Father I have made known to you." When he says, "Whatever I command you," check the context of what he commanded them. In chapter 15, and especially chapter 14, the issue is love for him: "You believe in God, believe also in me." They love God and love Christ. They now share the same persuasion and purpose. On that basis they have agreement and commitment, and are therefore friends. Because of that friendship, he makes things known to them in conversation.

He told them what the Father had told him; within the purpose there were no secrets between them. That degree of disclosure does not belong to everybody toward whom you are friendly. It belongs to core friendship. They had conversations because they knew one another deeply enough for trust, particularly for the Lord to trust them. "No longer do I call you servants, for a servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all things that I heard from my Father I have made known to you." That is John 15:13-15.

He calls people friends on the basis of shared persuasion, purpose, commitment, conversation, and trust. Core friends are particular people; everybody is not in the same relational category. We must separate issues and people with discernment. In John 17:6, Jesus says, "I have manifested your name to the men whom you have given me out of the world." There are people God gives you in this sense, people with whom core friendship develops.

"They were yours, you gave them to me, and they have kept your word. Now they have known that all things which you have given me are from you. For I have given to them the words which you have given me; and they have received them, and have known surely that I came forth from you; and they have believed that you sent me." See the relationship: reception of the same word, knowledge, persuasion, and purpose. He continues, "I pray for them. I do not pray for the world." He has a special relationship with them.

He separates them from others: "It is they for whom I am praying." This does not mean he has no concern for the world; later he prays for those who will believe through their word. But in this prayer he expressly identifies these people. That is core friendship. Jesus had core friendship. He was friendly to all, yet he prayed and disclosed purpose in a distinct way to these friends. What marks the difference? Look at their conversations; the conversations differed.

That difference matters because some believers treat every relationship as though it should carry the same information, access, and commitment. Jesus did not do that. He could preach the kingdom openly to a multitude, answer a sincere stranger, eat with tax collectors, and show compassion to the sick. But he did not disclose every detail of his purpose to every person in the crowd. He explained parables to the disciples, prepared the Twelve for his death, and entrusted particular responsibilities to people who had received his word.

Core friendship includes a history of received truth. "I gave them the words you gave me, and they received them." The friends share persuasion, not merely proximity. They know what you believe, why you are committed to it, and where the purpose is going. They have not merely heard your words; they have kept them. That creates the basis for more specific conversation and responsibility.

Core friendship also includes tested trust. Jesus says, "All things that I heard from my Father I have made known to you." Disclosure follows the relationship. In ordinary friendliness you may tell a stranger what is common knowledge and what can give light. In core friendship you may discuss details of purpose, weakness, decision, conflict, strategy, or responsibility that would be unwise to spread everywhere. The distinction is not secrecy for manipulation; it is stewardship of information within trust.

Prayer also reflects relational distinction. Jesus cared about the world, yet in John 17 he says, "I pray for them." He carries these particular people before the Father because they have been given to him in the work. Likewise, you pray for all people, but there are people whose names, pressures, assignments, families, and decisions you carry with unusual specificity because your lives and purposes are joined.

Shared activity deepens the bond. The people closest to Jesus traveled with him, heard him repeatedly, served the crowds, faced opposition, asked questions, made mistakes, received correction, and remained in the work. In the same way, people become close when they do ministry together, work together, solve problems together, and live through seasons together. Closeness is not created by announcing, "I am close to this person." It is revealed by the substance of shared life.

That is why a person should not borrow someone else's name to gain status. Saying, "I am close to Pastor," does not create closeness. Do you share persuasion, purpose, conversation, trust, prayer, and activity? Have you received and kept the word? Have you carried responsibility? If those realities are absent, the claim is empty and may be an attempt to influence people who cannot discern relationship. Know how to separate friendliness from core friendship without becoming cold toward anyone.

In one conversation he says what should be common knowledge, shining as light to everyone. In another he says exclusive things, specific details of purpose. Some people claim, "I am this person's friend. I am very close to this pastor," and believe their own claim. But do they know what closeness to someone actually means? It is not established by assertion. The people closest to Jesus during his ministry were the Twelve. That was the relational reality of his life.

In my own life, no people are closer to me than the core leaders. I do not share more purpose and activity with anybody else. I have good friends in other places, and those friendships remain valuable, but the leaders are the people closest to me. Jesus could be friends with John the Baptist and others, but the disciples and leaders around him were his closest friends. There is no magic to it. Some people are close because we do ministry, business, and life together.

We share many things. The truly close people do not need to announce, "I am really close to the pastor," to gain influence; if you have sense, you already know the relationship by its substance. People who advertise borrowed closeness may be trying to swindle or manipulate you. John 19:12 says, "From then on Pilate sought to release him, but the Jews cried out, saying, 'If you let this man go, you are not Caesar's friend.'"

## Paul and His Friends

Look at Acts 10:24 and Acts 19:31. Paul's friends are mentioned in these passages. Acts 10:24 says, "The following day they entered Caesarea. Now Cornelius was waiting for them, and had called together his relatives and close friends." There is such a thing as close friends. If you ask a believer, "Who are your friends?" and the person says, "I do not have any," that is bad. Having no friends is not spiritual, but having friendships you should not have is also bad.

Do not imagine that saying, "I have no friends," is spiritually bright. It is spiritually foolish. You are not developing any friendship that furthers what you are doing, and it is difficult to see how you will fulfill purpose that way. Even when I was in secular settings, I developed friendships with people there. We talked and stirred one another. Some worked under me, some were colleagues, and some were leaders. Why would I avoid friendship in the place where I actually serve? Look at Acts 19:31.

"Then some of the officials of Asia, who were his friends, sent to him pleading that he would not venture into the theater." These officials were Paul's friends. Imagine that he had no friends to warn and protect him. The text does not explicitly say whether they were believers, though they may have been. What is explicit is that they were his friends. Paul was friendly. God said Paul would get to Rome, but God used people and relationships along the way. If you follow God, go and develop bonds of affection with people.

Some ideas promoted under the guise of spirituality are foolishness. Develop bonds of affection, but develop them purely. Do not do it for politics or manipulation; do it for purpose. Acts 27:3 says, "The next day we landed at Sidon. And Julius treated Paul kindly and gave him liberty to go to his friends and receive care." One thing you cannot deny is that Paul had friends. Of all the people whose friendships are recorded in scripture, Paul appears to have had the most, though our Lord also had many.

Paul had friends left, right, and center, in many places. James 2:23 calls Abraham God's friend, while James 4:4 says we should not be friends with the world. Third John 14 says, "I hope to see you shortly, and we shall speak face to face. Peace to you. Our friends greet you. Greet the friends by name." John sends greetings among friends through Gaius. Friendship language runs throughout the New Testament.

Notice how practical Paul's friendships were. In Acts 19, friends among the officials of Asia used their position and knowledge of the situation to keep him from entering a dangerous theater. In Acts 27, Julius allowed him to go to his friends and receive care. Those relationships supplied warning, access, protection, refreshment, and material care. They were not sentimental labels. Affection took definite steps that helped preserve Paul in the purpose God had announced for him.

Paul's letters show the same pattern. He remembers people by name, describes their labor, sends greetings, commends messengers, receives gifts, gives instructions, and speaks openly about those who refreshed him or stood with him. Priscilla and Aquila risked their necks for his life. The Philippians shared with him in giving and receiving. Onesiphorus often refreshed him and was not ashamed of his chains. Luke remained with him. These are bonds of affection expressed in action.

Paul could also speak honestly when relationships failed. Demas forsook him, having loved this present world. Alexander did him much harm. At his first defense no one stood with him. Scripture does not hide those wounds, but neither did Paul respond by deciding to have no more friends. He continued to name faithful people, request their presence, send them on assignments, and receive their care. Betrayal did not cancel the Christian disposition toward friendship.

So ask, "Who are my friends in purpose? Who can warn me, care for me, travel with me, tell me the truth, strengthen my hand, and receive care from me? Whose purpose am I helping?" The answer should not be, "Nobody; I do everything alone." God may give the assignment, but the New Testament repeatedly shows the assignment moving through relationships. Develop those bonds purely, not for politics, and let them serve the purpose of God.

We still have to look at the word *phileo*, the last word we will examine today, because in the next session I want to get into the practice of friendship.

## Phileo: Personal Attachment

*Phileo*, the verb, means to be a friend to, to be fond of an individual or an object. It can describe a particular food for which you have developed a liking. I think my love for snacks began in primary school. I did not like eating early in the morning, but during the break I would go and buy something. I cannot forget a particular sugar-covered doughnut, and I have liked puff-puff from the beginning of time.

Back then I knew the different places where puff-puff was sold and could tell you which one was best. Puff-puff from one place differed from puff-puff elsewhere. There could be a particular side of a place where the portion was better, while another seller made it too small. That familiarity came from repeated experience. I had become used to the object and developed a liking for it. That is a simple illustration of the experiential element in *phileo*.

The same distinction appeared earlier with Isaac's food. "Make me savory food, such as I love." The affection is not a conclusion reached by abstract principle. The person has tasted it, returned to it, become familiar with it, and now feels fondness. In relationship, personal attachment likewise develops through experience: conversation, shared activity, receiving care, giving care, and repeatedly attending to the person.

To be fond of an individual or object, to have affection for, or to be a friend to: that is *phileo*. Quoting Strong's, it denotes personal attachment as a matter of sentiment or feeling. Strong's distinguishes it from *agapao*, which involves the deliberate assent of the will as a matter of principle, duty, or propriety.

You say, "I love everybody because that is the right thing. I am supposed to love everybody." That is *agapao*. "I love good and hate evil" is a principled love for what ought to be. But when you say, "I am fond of this person," that is *phileo*. What we are discussing in relationships is this personal fondness. You are supposed to become fond of people in church.

You are supposed to be fond of your parents. Perhaps that affection did not develop properly because of how you grew up or how they treated you. But have you not grown? Are you not wiser now? You can act wisely and let them see maturity in you, and affection can develop as a result. Be fond of your parents, siblings, relatives, and strangers. The Christian vocabulary includes love of family, love of brethren, and love of strangers.

Be fond of strangers, siblings, parents, and the brethren. That is *philadelphia*: fondness for the brethren, people who share the same womb. Become fond of the brethren rather than remaining suspicious of them. *Phileo* is actively sentimental and subjective, unlike *agape*, which is more often objective: this is what you ought to love and how things should be. You may love everybody in Christ by principle, being kind and patient toward them, without yet having personal fondness. But God also wants you to develop fondness.

In the faith of Christ, you are to develop fondness while expressing *agape* within it. A solitary, lonesome lifestyle is not Christian. Christianity is a *philos* lifestyle: you are fond and friendly. So we must see the exact steps. How are you friendly? How do you actively develop bonds of affection? Develop real fondness with people. Be affectionate; be their friend.

This is also why discipleship cannot be robotic. You cannot disciple someone by doing nothing except announcing, "Today I will teach you about the forgiveness of sins," treating the person like a machine receiving information. Artificial intelligence can present information, but discipleship is done by human beings who know, attend to, and become fond of the people they teach. You ask, "How are you today?" You know what is happening in the person's life. You cultivate the relationship while teaching the word. The point is not merely to transfer a lesson but to form a person in affection and purpose.

People sometimes speak about artificial intelligence as though it can become conscious in the same way a human being is conscious. But what do we mean by consciousness? An AI's awareness is instructed and programmed. Human awareness was not produced by a language model instructing us to be aware; we were created as aware persons. Artificial intelligence is human intelligence programmed to be expressed computationally. Human intelligence has biological, physiological, and psychological limitations that a computational tool may not share, so the tool can process and express patterns on a scale greater than one person can. But the intelligence embodied in the system still comes through human work, and ultimately the capacity belongs to the God who made human intelligence.

I can be in awe of what human beings have accomplished with AI while rejecting idolatry toward the tool. People deify, personify, and glorify it beyond its bounds, as though an independent being simply woke up. It is a tool. Whatever an AI does through its programming, human beings remain morally responsible for how they made and directed it. If people use a machine to kill someone, they cannot excuse themselves by saying the machine acted. In the same way, if they tell an AI system to harm people, the humans who designed and directed that use remain responsible. Do not create an idol and then fear that the idol will "take over" apart from human choices. Give proper credit to human ingenuity and greater credit to God for the intelligence he made possible.

But even an impressive tool does not replace the affectionate human bond in discipleship. Information is not the whole work. A disciple should know that you are fond of them, that you attend to them, and that you are present in their growth. That is the point here: develop real fondness with people. Be affectionate; be their friend.

Now let us see Matthew 6:5: "And when you pray, you shall not be like the hypocrites."

"For they love to pray standing in the synagogues and on the corners of the streets, that they may be seen by men. Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward." The word "love" there describes what they like and are fond of doing. They have become accustomed to public display. Now look at Matthew 10:37.

## Fondness in Every Relationship

Some relationships already exist structurally; they simply have to be there. But God's goal is for us to develop fondness within them. Even in a culture where marriage is arranged structurally and functionally, God's desire is that the spouses develop fondness for one another. Fondness for what? For purpose. The Lord takes no joy in people being married while remaining cold and unfond of each other. Scriptural fondness is directed toward purpose. Likewise, when believers meet strangers, they should show affection rather than coldness.

There should be affection even in a simple "How are you?" or "Thank you." There is a warmness in the way you receive the person. Matthew 10:37 says, "He who loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me. And he who loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me." They are fonder of those people than of Christ. Whom does he want us to be fond of most? Him. He wants us to be friends with him. Then our fondness in every other relationship will serve his purpose and demonstrate that our hearts are truly set on his purpose.

It is not children for children's sake, giving them everything they desire merely because they want it. Fondness toward them is for God's purpose, because Christ is the one of whom we are most fond. Matthew 23:6 speaks of people who love the best seats. Matthew 26:48 speaks of Judas's kiss. In the next session we will move fully into the exact steps, and conversations will be a major part of those steps. We will see conversations with strangers, acquaintances, spouses, leaders, and core friends, and the expressions of fondness proper to each relationship.

There are things you say and conversations you have. Some conversations can only be had in a marital relationship, and they need to happen there. Other conversations belong to core friendship or leadership. How do you develop fondness with your leaders? Not by confusing and distracting them. You become someone whose support is known: they know you have their back, and you know they have yours. That is scriptural. Many people had Paul's back. When he wrote to Philippi, Euodia and Syntyche were in conflict, but they and Clement were still his people who had labored with him in the gospel.

They had Paul's back repeatedly. Priscilla and Aquila were willing to lay down their necks for his life. Paul did not merely stand in a pulpit and teach them; he was fond of them. He related to them in ordinary life. He could laugh with them when their club lost and celebrate when it won, if Paul had cared about football. The point is shared life, not only formal teaching. But Matthew 23:6 shows that fondness itself can be misdirected: the scribes loved the best seats. So what are you fond of?

Matthew 26:48 says, "Now his betrayer had given them a sign, saying, 'Whomever I kiss, he is the one; seize him.'" The kiss was an expression of *phileo*, an outward sign of fondness, which Judas used falsely. In their culture, kissing was a form of affectionate greeting. Today in Nigeria that is not our usual practice, so do not turn the instruction into something else. Scripture teaches us to express affection to the brethren through a handshake, a greeting, a fist bump, or a brief hug. "Greet one another with a holy kiss" communicates a holy, culturally intelligible affection. Mark 14:44 records the same sign.

Express affection to the brethren in a holy and appropriate way. It is part of the steps. Luke 20:46 says, "Beware of the scribes, who desire to go around in long robes, love greetings in the marketplaces, the best seats in the synagogues, and the best places at feasts." Their fondness was for recognition: "Pastor, welcome!" They went where people would notice and greet them. Some people enjoy being hailed more than they care about the people greeting them.

Scripture did not tell you to become fond of yourself in that self-seeking way. If you are looking for recognition for yourself, it is no longer about affection toward people. Judas also expressed false fondness with a kiss. Now look at John 5:20.

## The Father, the Son, and Friendship

"For the Father loves the Son, and shows him all things that he himself does; and he will show him greater works than these." The Father is affectionate toward the Son and shares a bond with him. Again, disclosure appears in friendship: "I have made known to you." The Father shows the Son all things, including his purpose and plan. God is not only my God; he is my friend. He tells me what he is doing, and I am not expecting that to change.

He tells me what is going to happen and what I need to know. One day I already knew someone was going to ask me a question before the person stopped me. I have a working relationship with God; such communication is normal within that relationship. John 5:20 says the Father shows the Son what he is doing and will show him greater works. Will God guide you concerning your unit, extension, and work? Why would he not? He is your friend in the mission he gave you. Do not disqualify yourself by speaking wrong words about that relationship.

Sometimes the guidance is specific to a period: "This is what is happening here. When you go, this person will do this. When that happens, take this step." It does not mean God narrates every movement of every person. He tells you what you need to know for the work. In the incident I mentioned, I sensed that the person was going to stop me and ask a particular kind of question. Before I could tell him that I already knew he wanted to ask something, he stopped me and asked. I did not have every detail beforehand, but I had enough to recognize the conversation when it came.

I do not sell those experiences or make them a performance. The point is friendship in mission. The Father shows the Son what he is doing; Christ makes known to his friends what he heard from the Father; and the Spirit guides believers in the work assigned to them. Do not speak as though closeness and guidance are impossible for you while claiming that God has given you responsibility.

John 11:3 says, "Therefore the sisters sent to him, saying, 'Lord, behold, he whom you love is sick.'" Lazarus was someone of whom Jesus was fond. They probably shared meals, preferences, conversation, and time; they were friends. You should have people like that, primarily in the local church and also outside it. Verse 36 says, "See how he loved him!" Let me also reference something I said earlier. When you get born again and receive God's word and call, it must become obvious to everybody that you have changed, regardless of what they thought of you before.

Perhaps you were a permissive friend who went along with everybody. They must know that has ended because you have changed. I say that from experience. The way I was in SS1 and below differed from the way I became after SS2 and SS3. I did not move with people in the same way again. I was stuck with Jesus; his opinion was what mattered. Before, I disliked going against the grain or making people feel bad. But when I saw God's word, that line was drawn. I am grateful because it helped me develop part of the character I needed: you cannot be a pleaser of men and a servant of God.

John 12:25 says, "He who loves his life will lose it, and he who hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life." Some people are attached to everything in this world; what they love in their lives is material things. John 15:19 says, "If you were of the world, the world would love its own." People in the world cannot be as fond of you as believers in the church can be, because your core persuasion and purpose differ. You will be friendly toward them, but you cannot share the same committed bond they share with one another around worldly purpose.

You cannot have their exact bond because you are not going to the same places or participating in the same activities. Yet they should know, "This person is friendly toward us. If we have a question, we can talk to him." That friendliness can become a path by which they receive the light. But do not seek the core bond they have with each other around darkness. John 16:27 says, "The Father himself loves you, because you have loved me, and have believed that I came forth from God." The Father is affectionate toward you.

He seeks to develop relationship with you because you have loved Christ. John 20:2 calls John "the other disciple, whom Jesus loved." In our relationship with God and every other relationship, the major issue is that we develop fondness. Coldheartedness toward God or people is not of God. You cannot remain cold. You must be warm, fond, and affectionate. In your workplace you cannot be the cold person who stays apart from everyone. Affection should be an obvious part of who you are.

People should not say, "That brother is always on his own," as though isolation is the testimony of a believer. I could have been naturally inclined that way, but that is not who I am in Christ. John calls himself "the disciple whom Jesus loved," claiming that Jesus was affectionate toward him. Then in John 21:15-17 Jesus asks Peter, "Simon, son of Jonah, do you love me more than these?"

Is your affection, friendliness, fondness, and bond of affection directed toward Christ above every other thing? Can you answer yes when Jesus asks whether you love him more than possessions, food, people, your husband or wife, a close friend, a colleague, or your parents? Do you have affection for him? This is not merely the principled statement, "Generally, we have to love Jesus." Do you have an expressed and cultivated affection for him? Have you tended and cultivated that relationship?

Peter answers, "Yes, Lord; you know that I love you." Jesus says, "Feed my lambs." Again he asks, "Do you love me?" and Peter answers, "Yes, Lord; you know that I love you." Jesus says, "Tend my sheep." In other words, "Because you are fond of me, participate in my purpose. This is what I want you to do." The third time Peter is grieved and says, "Lord, you know all things; you know that I love you." Now see 1 Corinthians 16:22, Titus 3:15, and two texts in Revelation.

First Corinthians 16:22 says, "If anyone does not love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be accursed. O Lord, come!" It should be unbelievers who are not fond of him; it would be unfortunate for a believer to live in that category. Titus 3:15 says, "All who are with me greet you. Greet those who love us in the faith." They are affectionate toward us in the faith. Revelation 3:19 says, "As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten." The Lord rebukes those toward whom he is affectionate. Revelation 22:15 says, "Outside are dogs and sorcerers and sexually immoral and murderers and idolaters."

It continues, "and whoever loves and practices a lie." They reject the truth and become fond of falsehood rather than the truth of God.

## Cultivating Bonds of Affection

God wants us to be fond of him and of people. That is the practice we now want to examine. We look at friendship to emphasize the affection and fondness we are to practice and cultivate. The steps of walking in friendliness and friendship are steps of walking in affection, cultivating fondness with people, and building bonds of affection. You should be fond of everybody in the church, at least everyone in your unit, then as much as possible in your extension and the whole local church. The church is growing ceaselessly, mightily, greatly, and rapidly, but affectionate bonds are still possible.

It is not impossible to be fond of many people in church right now. You can be fond of this person, that person, and another person. You have affectionate bonds because you know each other and have shared conversation and activity. It is more than merely recognizing a face. If something happens, you know something about the person and have a proper relational response. You also learn to tell the right people the right things. When we examine conversation, we will see that there are things you cannot tell everyone and things you tell particular people.

"These are the people I want to tell this matter; those are the people with whom I can discuss that one." You know one another to that degree because you have cultivated fondness. Conversation is a major part of those relational steps. We will begin to see that as we examine the exact steps of friendliness in relationships in the faith of Christ.

## The Practice Before Us

We must find the exact steps we are supposed to take in our relationships in the faith of Christ. What steps of friendliness and friendship apply in a marital union, the local church, a sibling relationship, and every other category? You are not to ignore conversations or remain obnoxious toward people. You develop bonds of affection. Say, "I am developing bonds of affection with every person in the local church. I express affection toward all humanity." Whether the person is a stranger or someone you know, you express affection.

Suppose the person is not from your tribe, but from a tribe about which you have heard many terrible things. You are a believer, so you do not think through prejudice; you express affection toward the person. Suppose the person has done evil, is called a winebibber, tax collector, politician, or Zacchaeus who stole the people's money. You still express affection toward him as Jesus did. Expressing affection toward all people does not mean calling wrong conduct right. What is wrong remains wrong.

You express affection toward all people: strangers, acquaintances, relatives, parents, children, siblings, and people in your secular relationships. They know, "That person is warm and friendly. That is simply how he is; that is how she is." That is our testimony in Christ as we walk in relationships to fulfill God's purpose.

Apply this deliberately across every category. When you meet a stranger, do not begin with tribal suspicion, class judgment, fear, or indifference. Begin with affection. Look at the person, greet properly, listen to the answer, and respond as though a human being made in God's image is before you. You do not know whether that brief warmth will become the opening through which the person receives the gospel, practical help, correction, or a lasting relationship.

When the person comes from a tribe about which you have heard terrible generalizations, you do not let those stories determine your first step. The person is not a stereotype standing in front of you. You are a believer whose nature and path are defined by Christ. Express affection, judge actual conduct truthfully, and refuse both prejudice and naivety. What is wrong remains wrong, but the person still receives the friendliness proper to humanity.

Above all, cultivate affection for God. Do not settle for a merely principled statement that Christians ought to love Jesus. Speak with him, receive his word, attend to his purpose, obey what he commands, and value what he makes known. Let your affection for him govern every other bond. "Do you love me more than these? Feed my sheep." Fondness for Christ moves into participation in his purpose.

The next question, then, is exact practice. What conversations belong with strangers? What belongs with acquaintances, relatives, colleagues, saints, leaders, spouses, and core friends? What should be disclosed, what should be withheld, how should conflict be addressed, and how should affection be expressed in each setting? Those are the definite relational steps we will continue to examine. For now, establish the foundation: friendliness with all, friendship with some, affection cultivated for the fulfillment of God's purpose.

Do not leave this as a definition you can repeat without a walk. Look around your actual relationships and identify the next step. Who needs a greeting, a conversation, encouragement, correction, care, or clearer commitment? Where have you been cold? Where have you confused friendliness with agreement in evil? Where have you claimed friendship without cultivating affection? Take the exact step supplied by the truth, and let the testimony be that Christ has made you warm, friendly, affectionate, and effective in every relationship.

Developing fondness, cultivating affection, expressing affection. Let us bless and thank the Lord. Our Lord, we are grateful for what you do in us by your Spirit. We are glad that we are made effective in all our relationships.
