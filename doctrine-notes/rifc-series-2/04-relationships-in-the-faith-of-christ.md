## The Perfecting of the Saints in Relationships

So this is the final session of the teaching, a very important and practical session. We have already seen some practical things in the teaching on relationships in the faith of Christ: the practice of friendship and friendliness, or you could say the practice of being a friend, which is being friendly and having committed friendships. Now, in Ephesians 4:11, Paul said that the Lord Jesus himself gave the apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ.

Till we all come to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to a perfect man, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ; that we should no longer be children, tossed to and fro and carried about with every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men in the cunning craftiness of deceitful plotting; but, speaking the truth in love, may grow up into him in all things, who is the head, even Christ; from whom the whole body, joined and knit together by what every joint supplies, according to the effective working by which every part does its share, causes growth of the body for the edifying of itself in love. From Ephesians 4:11-16, we have established certain facts in this teaching. First, the reason for the church is to be perfected in the knowledge of the Son of God.

We said that God's desire for us to be perfected in the knowledge of the Son of God is how we are going to fulfill the purpose for which Christ died, rose, and established the church. We also noticed that this perfecting pertains to different areas of our lives. Verse 15 says that we will grow up into Christ in all things. That means there are different areas of our lives in which we need to grow, be perfected, and become effective. It is possible for a believer to be effective in one area and lacking in another. Look at First Thessalonians chapter 3.

We have said that a major part of the believer's life in which he cannot afford to be ineffective is his relationships. The opportunity to relate with people is often, for a man of purpose in Christ, an orchestration that ought to be taken advantage of. If you do not make maximal use of the relationships that cross your way, across the spectrum from strangers and acquaintances to relatives, siblings, and parents, you may miss the benefit or blessing those orchestrations of God are supposed to become to you down the line.

First Thessalonians 3:9-10 says, "For what thanks can we render to God for you, for all the joy with which we rejoice for your sake before our God, night and day praying exceedingly that we may see your face and perfect what is lacking in your faith?" I like that word, perfect. It is the same word, `katartizo`. There can be areas in which things are lacking in our faith. Relationships in the faith of Christ are a major area of our lives. We saw that in Ephesians 4 when Paul spoke about receiving knowledge for our growth and our walk. When he began to express what that knowledge and growth should do in our walk, a major part of what he spoke about was relationship. From Ephesians 5:22 to 6:9, he spoke about relationships.

He spoke about husbands and wives. He spoke about parents and children. He spoke about employers and employees. He spoke about other things like ministry, material pursuits, character, and things of the Spirit. But we saw that the one that took up most of his time clearly was relationships. So we said the purpose of this is to perfect the saints in relationships so as to fulfill purpose. There must be that mindset: the reason I have to understand relationships the way God wants me to, and to engage them the way I ought to, is to fulfill purpose. That grand sense of God's purpose that we are engaged in must affect the way we treat the relationships we have.

We then recollected what we saw in the earlier part of this teaching, in Relationships in the Faith of Christ (1). We saw the relationships we have in the faith of Christ.

## Friendship as the High Point of Relationship

God expects us to have relationships. We saw what those relationships are, and we particularly saw our role in them. We can sum up our role as friendliness and friendship, or the role of a friend. The high point of every relationship is being a friend. You are a parent: there is the responsibility of parenting, but the high point of that relationship is being a friend. You are a child: there is the responsibility of being parented, but the high point is being a friend. The high point of relationship between siblings is friendship. In the spousal or marital relationship, the high point is being friends. A person can marry without the intention of being a friend. Even in First Corinthians 7, Paul says, "Concerning the things of which you wrote to me: it is good for a man not to touch a woman. Nevertheless, because of sexual immorality, let each man have his own wife." But the high point God expects them to have is that they should be friends. So the high point of every relationship should be friendship.

We are now particular about the walk. In the Christian faith, knowledge is first an end in itself: the first goal is to know. But there is a second point: you need to walk. The word we saw for walk, `peripateo`, means to take definite steps in a particular path. You need to walk. It is used alongside `tupos`, a type. You are walking in the same way you saw someone walk, following the exact type. We also saw the word imitate, a word for replicating. You are to replicate exactly what someone did.

That means there is the issue of your nature, but there is also the issue of replicating steps. To walk is to take definite steps and replicate exact steps. I said it is not exactly the "What would Jesus do?" mantra in the sense of, "That is what I want to do." It is rather, "What would Jesus do? That is what I do." It is your nature, and you are saying, "That is what I do." It happens by faith. I replicate his steps as my nature by faith. The believer must always want to find out, "What are my steps in this situation and circumstance toward this person?"

In the last session, we mentioned seven expressions of friendship. Before focusing on those expressions, we want to look again at some of the relationships, not all of them, and point out what Scripture says about them. That will help us fully appreciate the expressions. We will look mainly at strangers and acquaintances; as we look at acquaintances, we will also see something about relatives. The other relationships already have greater clarity in the texts that speak about them.

We mostly know the texts that speak about spousal, parental, and sibling relationships. Strangers and acquaintances will give us details that we should pay attention to when it comes to the walk or expression.

## Strangers and the Duty to Receive Them

Now let's look at strangers first. The word is `xenos`, X-E-N-O-S, where we get the word xenophobia. I have said that some Christians are xenophobic, not in the sense of ethnic hatred or violence, but in that they are obnoxious toward strangers. That is not a Christian attitude. Christians are not obnoxious toward strangers. "I do not really do well with strangers." No, not the Christian. `Xenos` means foreign, alien, or novel, not the usual; and by implication, a guest. Sometimes you find it used for the host, perhaps as a reverse implication: the host is also foreign to the person who is foreign to him. So it can refer to a host or entertainer of a guest.

Let us look at the use of the word *strangers* in Scripture. It is important because it opens to us expressions of how we are to relate with them, expressions to which we will refer later. Matthew 25:34-35 says, "Then the King will say to those on his right hand, 'Come, you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world: for I was hungry and you gave me food; I was thirsty and you gave me drink; I was a stranger and you took me in.'" He is speaking to people who, as we will see, are of faith. In verse 38 they ask, "When did we see you a stranger and take you in, or naked and clothe you?"

You find the word again in verse 43, where he speaks to those on the left hand: "I was a stranger and you did not take me in, naked and you did not clothe me." People who are of faith are meant to have this expression; people who are not of faith may typically lack it. It belongs to the man of faith in Christ, the man with the Spirit of God, to receive strangers. We receive strangers. We relate well and affectionately with them. You cannot simply look down because the person is a stranger. That is not the Christian's step. You should already know what to do: look up, welcome the person, and relate affectionately.

Acts 17:18 says, "Then certain Epicurean and Stoic philosophers encountered him. And some said, 'What does this babbler want to say?' Others said, 'He seems to be a proclaimer of foreign gods,' because he preached to them Jesus and the resurrection." Then verse 21 says, "For all the Athenians and the foreigners who were there spent their time in nothing else but either to tell or to hear some new thing." The foreigners were not of Athens; they had come from different places. At one point Athens was like New York. Greece had been the superpower, and although by this time Rome was the superpower, Greece likely remained the intellectual superpower. There was real education going on there.

If you read the history of the Stoics, the Epicureans, and the others, they were active learners and teachers; they always had disciples. Now look at Romans 16:23, where the word is used for Gaius as a host: "Gaius, my host and the host of the whole church, greets you." It is used in Ephesians 2:12 of Gentiles as foreigners to the covenants the Jews had. Then in verse 19, we are no longer strangers and foreigners in the family of God and in God's purpose. Hebrews 11:13 speaks of the patriarchs as strangers and pilgrims in the lands where they walked.

Hebrews 13:9 uses the word in the expression "various and strange doctrines," and First Peter 4:12 says, "Beloved, do not think it strange concerning the fiery trial." Matthew 27:7 says that they bought the potter's field "to bury strangers in," referring to non-Jews. But apart from Matthew 25, the major text for our purpose is Third John 5. John is writing to Gaius: "Beloved, you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren and for strangers, who have borne witness of your love before the church." What you do for brethren and for strangers is Christian testimony.

Strangers are people who are new or alien to you. You receive them. That does not mean they become your committed friends immediately, but you are affectionate toward them, do well to them, treat them affectionately, and cultivate affection with them. Now let us consider acquaintances.

## Acquaintances and Relatives

In the Hebrew, the word rendered *acquaintance* is related to `yada`, the verb *to know*. It becomes the one who knows you, particularly by observation. The major text is Luke 2:44, which concerns Jesus's own relationships. Joseph and his mother, "supposing him to have been in the company, went a day's journey, and sought him among their relatives and acquaintances." We have seen strangers and acquaintances, but this text also introduces relatives.

A relative is defined biologically rather than socially. A relative may socially be only your acquaintance. A stranger is socially defined: the person is new to you. An acquaintance is also socially defined: the person knows you. A relative, however, is biologically defined. The word for relative or kinsman there is `syngenes`, a relative by blood or kin. It can sometimes refer more broadly to people of the same culture, such as Yorubas, Igbos, or Hausas; to countrymen, such as Nigerians; or to those much closer to you by blood.

The word occurs twelve times. Mark 6:4 says, "A prophet is not without honor except in his own country, among his own relatives, and in his own house." We will place relatives with acquaintances for this discussion, while retaining the distinction. Most relatives, socially speaking, may remain at the acquaintance level: you know one another, but you may not be friends. With some relatives the relationship goes beyond acquaintance into friendship, and some may even be core friends.

Luke 1:36 says, "Now indeed, Elizabeth your relative has also conceived a son in her old age." The angel called Elizabeth Mary's relative. God is not against family ministry. He called John and Jesus from within the same extended family. Do not use your words against yourself by declaring that ministry must not involve a family. We should pray that our relatives accept the faith, become serious with God, and serve his purpose. If a ministry can include anybody, why should it exclude your family? Your own family should be there, and what you are doing should be replicated with your siblings, your children, and your disciples.

Luke 1:58 says that when Elizabeth's neighbors and relatives heard how the Lord had shown great mercy to her, they rejoiced with her. Relatives will ordinarily be present at events such as a birth, a naming ceremony, or a wedding. Sometimes, because they are relatives, they become very entitled about what the couple must buy, whom they must invite, and how loud the celebration must be.

The two people marrying must agree about what they are doing. Family members may not agree, and the couple may have to talk with them, but it is the couple's wedding. If relatives want something added, they can be given room to provide what they want. A practical way to think about a celebration is to calculate the total cost per person present, not only the meal but the venue, seating, and the other costs. Anyone demanding a much larger guest list should understand what that number actually costs. The point is not that celebration is wrong. If you have expendable income, a wedding is worth celebrating. But the people getting married must remain responsible for the agreement, the budget, and the marriage that follows the wedding.

This is one of the places where relatives test whether the couple understands relationship and agreement. A relative may say, "We need people from this side and from that side. We must buy this, and the celebration must be loud." But are you marrying the family members, or are the two people marrying one another? The family members matter, and you may need to temper your wishes and speak with them honorably. Yet the final responsibility rests on the couple. It is their wedding and, more importantly, their marriage.

If someone wants two hundred additional guests, do not calculate only the food. Add the chair, venue, service, and every other cost attached to each person. If the total cost per guest is ten thousand, two hundred guests add two million. You can say, "If these are the people you insist on inviting, please provide for them." That is not hostility to relatives; it is clarity and agreement. Give relatives room to add what they want when they are willing to furnish it, but do not allow entitlement to replace the couple's responsibility.

Wedding culture itself can become highly impressionable. People are told that a wedding must look a certain way, that a proposal must involve a ring costing several months of salary, or that the day proves the degree of love. Much of that is marketing rather than a command of God. If you have expendable income and want a large celebration, celebrate. A wedding is worth celebrating. But do not enter bondage to an image, and do not let an event planner, master of ceremonies, or relative take control of the meaning of the day. Ask what is planned, agree on what will be done, and refuse humiliating entertainment presented as though every couple must accept it.

There is a balance. You cannot pretend family does not exist, but the balance depends on whether the people marrying are prepared to take responsibility for both the wedding and the marriage. If you are in charge of what you want to do, then make the agreement, communicate it, carry the cost, and cross that bridge with wisdom. Relatives belong in the relational picture, but biological relationship does not erase proper social boundaries.

Luke 14:12 says, "When you give a dinner or a supper, do not ask your friends, your brothers, your relatives, nor rich neighbors." In context, the Lord is teaching charity toward people who cannot repay you. The text still shows the ordinary relational categories of friends, brothers, relatives, and neighbors.

John 18:26 gives another use: one of the servants of the high priest, a relative of Malchus whose ear Peter cut off, said, "Did I not see you in the garden with him?" Malchus had a relative in the high priest's household. Acts 10:24 also says that Cornelius called together his relatives and close friends. Romans 9:3 uses the term when Paul speaks of the Jews as his countrymen and relatives according to the flesh.

Romans 16:7, 11, and 21 mentions specific people whom Paul calls his countrymen or kinsmen. It is like writing, "my fellow Nigerian," or naming someone from your own people. Scripture did not throw natural kinship under the bus. It is not something to hide or something that cannot be spoken of.

Romans 16:7 says, "Greet Andronicus and Junia, my countrymen and my fellow prisoners." Verse 11 says, "Greet Herodion, my countryman." Then verse 21 says, "Timothy, my fellow worker, and Lucius, Jason, and Sosipater, my countrymen, greet you." Scripture openly recognizes these natural relationships.

Now consider the word for acquaintance, `gnostos`: known, or one who knows. Again, it concerns knowing by observation. This is important because it will help us separate the conversations expected with different people. I will show you three classes of information in conversation that you must recognize. Luke 23:49 says, "But all his acquaintances, and the women who followed him from Galilee, stood at a distance, watching these things." These were not necessarily relatives, though relatives could be included. They were people Jesus had known in different places, possibly also through his ministry. Jesus had acquaintances; he was serious about relationships.

John 18:15-16 says, "And Simon Peter followed Jesus, and so did another disciple. Now that disciple was known to the high priest, and went with Jesus into the courtyard of the high priest. But Peter stood at the door outside. Then the other disciple, who was known to the high priest, went out and spoke to her who kept the door, and brought Peter in." That is one reason John could give us more information. You see why relationship matters in fulfilling purpose. The other disciple's acquaintance with the high priest opened the door for Peter.

Acts 1:19 says that what happened to Judas became known to those dwelling in Jerusalem; the people became acquainted with it. Acts 2:14 says, "Let this be known to you." You see the same usage in Acts 4:10 and 16, Acts 9:42, and Acts 13:38: "Therefore let it be known to you, brethren, that through this Man is preached to you the forgiveness of sins." Acts 15:18 says, "Known to God from eternity are all his works." Acts 19:17 says, "This became known both to all Jews and Greeks dwelling in Ephesus; and fear fell on them all, and the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified."

In Acts 28:22 they say, "We desire to hear from you what you think; for concerning this sect, we know that it is spoken against everywhere." They were acquainted with its public reputation. Then verse 28 says, "Let it be known to you that the salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles." Romans 1:19 is the final place, and we often use it in speaking of the gospel and the truth: "Because what may be known of God is manifest in them." What could be known was publicly shown. This is information that is manifestly public and observable. Take note of these facts.

## Knowledge Distinguishes Relational Levels

We have seen strangers and acquaintances. In those two socially defined relationships, the difference is knowledge. Strangers do not know you and you do not know them. Acquaintances know you and you know them; they are known to you. Knowledge and information distinguish relational levels: what people know about you and what you know about them. We are therefore listing the relationships we have in the faith of Christ partly in terms of what people ought to know about you and what you should know about them.

This issue of knowledge will return when we discuss conversation. Knowledge is ordinarily received through conversation and sometimes simply through observation. If we list our relationships in an order or hierarchy, from the lowest level of knowledge to the highest, we begin with strangers, then acquaintances, with relatives in that category when they are known to us only at that level. Most relatives are acquaintances in the social sense: there is the added fact of biological kinship, but socially you merely know one another. Some relatives, however, become closer friends or even core friends.

After strangers and acquaintances come secular relationships, then siblings, because there is usually more they should know about one another; then parents; then saints, meaning fellow believers generally; then local church members; then core friends; then spouse; then leaders. I debate the exact position of core friends and spouse because a core friend may in some cases be closer than a spouse. It depends on the actual depth of friendship.

Your leaders are supposed to know what they ought to know about you, even beyond what your spouse may need to know in the leadership context. Hebrews 13 speaks of obeying those who rule over you and submitting to them, because they watch out for your souls. This does not mean they should know every piece of information entrusted to you; we will divide the kinds of information and show what is not to be disclosed even to leaders. But where the circumstances and their oversight call for it, they should know. Lastly, of course, there is God.

## Seven Expressions of Friendliness and Friendship

Let us get on with the expressions. We have said that relationships are usually defined by knowledge and information. We will return to the role of knowledge when we reach conversation, but greeting comes first. From the role we play in relationships, which is friendship, we found seven major expressions of friendliness and friendship.

The first is greeting or salutation. The second is conversation. The third is revelation or disclosure. The fourth is building. I considered words such as training, teaching, molding, and sharpening, but *building* captures it best. Friends minister by the Holy Ghost to one another and edify one another. They can say, "No, that is not our way. This is what we are after. This is how we are supposed to be." They build themselves according to what they have agreed upon in God's purpose. This expression applies particularly to friendship, not merely friendliness.

The fifth expression is shared activity in shared purpose or pleasure. The sixth is support and help for the other person's vision, purpose, and pursuit. This is distinct from what is shared: the vision belongs to the other person, and you support it. The seventh is honor and material giving.

Gloria Copeland is a good example of friends building one another in faith. There were times when the Copelands went through issues together and said, "Let us believe God together." They listened to Kenneth Hagin's teaching, and I think she was the one who first heard him at a meeting and told her husband, "Come and hear what I am hearing." He became very disciplined about listening, even playing the teaching through the night. Later, when ministry pressures increased and he was becoming less positive under the challenges, he had someone beside him who would build him and build his faith. You want a friend who will do that.

Support for the other person's vision is related but distinct. I once used the example of a woman who is far wealthier and more ambitious in business than her husband. He is a pastor and does not appear interested in building the same kind of public or financial profile. Yet when she has something to do, he will go all the way to support her, drive her where needed, and be actively involved. From the way she speaks about their relationship, you can see that they have a strong understanding and a very positive relationship. That is supporting the other person without competing with the person's vision.

Another woman spoke of meeting her husband while she was still a normal, growing believer, simply trying to serve the Lord. As she began to understand what God wanted him to do, long before he was known, she saw how he studied through the night and sought God. She was concerned that she must not become the reason he failed to do what he was supposed to do. That is supporting the other person's vision. Now let us look at the expressions one after another.

## Greeting as Christian Culture

Number one is greeting and salutation. This is major, and it applies to every relationship, from strangers onward. After this meeting, do not enter a car, bus, house, or any shared space without greeting people. Affectionate greeting is a serious doctrinal matter in the Bible. It is primary, both at the first instance and continually. You greet when you first meet the person, and you continue to greet the person afterward.

The verb is `aspazomai`, A-S-P-A-Z-O-M-A-I. It comes from words carrying the ideas of union and drawing, so the sense is to draw together or draw close. I did not know the derivation before, but I found that I naturally do much of it: when I have not seen my father or mother for a while, for example, I hold the person closely. The related verb for drawing out or drawing toward is used of drawing a sword in Mark 14:47 and Acts 16:27. `Aspazomai` itself is used sixty times in forty-nine verses. From the first occurrence, we can see how important the Lord considers greeting.

Matthew 5:47 says, "And if you greet your brethren only, what do you do more than others? Do not even the tax collectors do so?" People who are unbelieving and do not learn the character of the Spirit greet only their own people. The Christian should be able to greet even an enemy affectionately: "How are you?" The person may have spoken ill of you or done evil to you, yet you can still say warmly, "How are you?" It is not easy, but that is the Bible's `aspazomai`.

Matthew 10:12 says, "And when you go into a household, greet it." You are the one entering, so you greet the household. The greeting is peace upon the house, like *as-salamu alaykum*: peace be upon you. *Salam* and *shalom* mean peace. This practice was taken from Scripture and from Jewish and Middle Eastern culture. Whether you meet in person, call, or send a message, learn to greet affectionately: "How are you? It is good to hear from you. I hope everything is well."

Mark 9:15 says that when the people saw the Lord after he came from the Mount of Transfiguration, "they were greatly amazed, and running to him, greeted him." Luke 1:40 says that Mary "entered the house of Zacharias and greeted Elizabeth." Luke 10:4 says, "Greet no one along the road." That instruction makes sense because greeting people along the way was normally their culture. They would stop and ask, "How are you? Is everything well?" But on that urgent assignment the Lord told them not to be distracted. The exception shows the ordinary practice.

Acts 18:22 shows why Paul had friends: "And when he had landed at Caesarea, and gone up and greeted the church, he went down to Antioch." He did not land, go to a mountain, announce that he was taking over the land, and ignore the brethren. He went to greet people. Later, when persecution came, he had relationships. Paul was always greeting.

Acts 20:1 says, "After the uproar had ceased, Paul called the disciples to himself, embraced them, and departed to go to Macedonia." That was `aspazomai`. He had a genuinely welcoming spirit. Acts 21:6 says, "When we had taken our leave of one another, we boarded the ship, and they returned home." The expression is that they greeted one another off. They had come to the ship, knelt down, prayed, embraced, and then left. That was Paul's heart and attitude. Before he left, he greeted people; when he arrived, he greeted the brethren and stayed with them. Stay with brethren. Make friends.

Acts 21:17-19 says, "And when we had come to Jerusalem, the brethren received us gladly. On the following day Paul went in with us to James, and all the elders were present. When he had greeted them, he told in detail those things which God had done among the Gentiles through his ministry." He went to greet the leaders. They did not skip greeting because there were important matters to discuss. They would first have asked, "How have you been? Was your journey good?" Then Paul told them in detail what God had done.

Notice the whole movement in Acts 21. In verse 6, Paul and the brethren greeted one another as they parted. In verse 7, after the voyage, "we greeted the brethren, and stayed with them one day." He greeted people before leaving and greeted people on arriving. He also stayed with them. He did not arrive and immediately withdraw for a private retreat as though time with brethren were a distraction from spiritual purpose. Time with the brethren was part of his spiritual life. Stay with brethren. Know them. Make friends.

Then in Jerusalem the brethren received them gladly, Paul went with them to James, all the elders were present, and he greeted them before giving the report. Greeting was not an empty preliminary to the real ministry. It expressed the relationship in which the ministry report could be received. The same Paul who could tell in detail what God had done among the Gentiles first knew how to ask how the people were and receive them warmly.

They would have greeted one another well: "How have you been? Was your journey good? It is good to see you. Thank God for everything. Please sit down." Only then did they begin the important discussion. It is bad character to skip that and claim, "I am very focused. I do not joke around." Repent and do the right thing. Greet people.

Acts 25:13 says that after some days King Agrippa and Bernice came to Caesarea to greet Festus. That is how cordiality is maintained. Do you maintain it over the phone? Sometimes simply say, "I wanted to greet you." That is enough. A greeting does not have to be an appeal for money or a request for something. Greet normally and warmly.

This applies in ordinary public situations too. Enter a bus or a cab and say, "Good afternoon, everyone." I had to repent of simply facing forward and putting in my earpiece. On one occasion I entered a ride and said, "Good afternoon. Good to see you," then realized that "good to see you" sounded strange because I did not know the person. But the point remains: greet people. Say, "Good afternoon, sir," or whatever is appropriate. Do not enter shared spaces as though no one else is there.

There are people who consistently do this. One person calls every month simply to say, "Happy new month. How is everything, sir?" That consistency matters. First Peter 5:13-14 says, "She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you; and so does Mark my son. Greet one another with a kiss of love." Greeting is not incidental in Christian culture; it is a repeated expression of affection and continued relationship.

Now let us go to Romans, which is the home of greeting. Romans chapter 16 repeatedly uses the word in verses 3, 5-16, 22, and 23.

Look at Romans 16 for yourself. Paul greets people one by one and mentions particular things about them. That is friendship. He does not simply say, "Greet all the brethren for me." He names them, knows their history, and remembers what they did. "Greet Priscilla and Aquila, my fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their own necks for my life." He is recalling events: "Thank you for that day. I am grateful for this and for that." Verse 5 says, "Likewise greet the church that is in their house." Verse 6 says, "Greet Mary, who labored much for us." Verse 7 says, "Greet Andronicus and Junia, my countrymen and my fellow prisoners." Greet them warmly; draw them close and let them know I am sending affection to them.

Verse 8 says, "Greet Amplias, my beloved in the Lord." Verse 9 says, "Greet Urbanus, our fellow worker in Christ." Verse 10 says, "Greet Apelles, approved in Christ." Verse 11 says, "Greet Herodion, my countryman." Verse 12 says, "Greet Tryphena and Tryphosa, who have labored in the Lord. Greet the beloved Persis, who labored much in the Lord." He is expressing emotion and recalling their record. "Greet Rufus, chosen in the Lord, and his mother and mine." Then he names Asyncritus, Phlegon, Hermas, Patrobas, Hermes, Philologus, Julia, Nereus and his sister, and the saints with them.

There is no way Paul could speak like that without affectionate relationships with these people. He knew them, knew things about them, and related one-on-one with them in different ways. If they were merely a crowd to whom he preached before running off, he would have nothing distinct to say about each person. That is Christianity. "Greet one another with a holy kiss. The churches of Christ greet you." The people with Paul were also sending their greetings. Then verses 22 and 23 say, "I, Tertius, who wrote this epistle, greet you in the Lord. Gaius, my host and the host of the whole church, greets you." Men and brethren, that is the Christian attitude.

Look at the heart in the names. One is "my beloved in the Lord." Another is "approved in Christ." Some "labored much in the Lord." Rufus's mother had become like a mother to Paul. These are not interchangeable names in a mailing list. Each greeting carries observation, memory, gratitude, honor, or affection. Paul recalls who labored, who risked something, who shared prison with him, who hosted him, and who stood approved. Greeting gave him a way to affirm what God had worked through particular people.

That is why "greet the friends by name" matters. A general greeting has its place, but there is another expression in knowing the person's name and remembering the person's part. "Thank you for what you did that day. I remember that you labored. I know what it cost you." Warm greeting draws the person close and preserves the history of friendship. Paul was not preaching generally and then running away from one-on-one relationship. He preached, stayed, knew, remembered, and greeted.

First Corinthians 16:19-20 says, "The churches of Asia greet you. Aquila and Priscilla greet you heartily in the Lord, with the church that is in their house. All the brethren greet you. Greet one another with a holy kiss." Learn to greet. Enter a bus or cab and at least say, "Good afternoon, everyone." Second Corinthians 13:12-13 says, "Greet one another with a holy kiss. All the saints greet you." Men and brethren, let us embrace this Christian culture.

After every church meeting, speak affectionately with at least one person you did not previously know. If you now know everybody, speak with someone again: "How is everything? How is that issue going? God is working. Your needs are met and your bills are paid." Encourage the person. You may say, "I have had this on my heart; perhaps you should do this." Do not form cliques in church. Everyone is your brother or sister, though you may have closer friends. Do not turn closer friendship into a camp that excludes others. Do not say, "I prefer to greet this brother; I do not like to greet that sister." That is not Christian brotherly affection.

Make the step definite: after a meeting, one person newly reached in brotherly affection. Do not wait until the person has a problem or until you need something. Ask a normal question, remember the answer, and greet the person again at the next meeting. If there is already an issue you know about, ask how it is going. If there is an encouragement in your heart, give it. This is how the church becomes a place of actual brothers and sisters rather than a room of people who receive the same sermon and leave as strangers.

Closer friends are proper; cliques are not. A closer friend is someone with whom you carry more knowledge, trust, and purpose. A clique is a camp that turns affection for some into coldness toward others. Have the core friendship, but remain a brother or sister to everyone. Greet across your preferred circle. Let the new person and the quiet person receive the same Christian warmth.

Philippians 4:21-22 says, "Greet every saint in Christ Jesus. The brethren who are with me greet you. All the saints greet you, but especially those who are of Caesar's household." The greeting goes in both directions. Colossians 4 continues the chain. Verse 10 says, "Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you, with Mark the cousin of Barnabas." Verse 12 says, "Epaphras, who is one of you, a bondservant of Christ, greets you." Verse 14 says, "Luke the beloved physician and Demas greet you." Verse 15 says, "Greet the brethren who are in Laodicea, and Nymphas and the church that is in his house."

First Thessalonians 5:26 says, "Greet all the brethren with a holy kiss." Second Timothy 4:19 says, "Greet Prisca and Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus." Verse 21 says, "Eubulus greets you, as well as Pudens, Linus, Claudia, and all the brethren." Titus 3:15 says, "All who are with me greet you. Greet those who love us in the faith." They greet you; you greet them. Philemon 23-24 says, "Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you, as do Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke, my fellow laborers."

Hebrews 11:13 says the patriarchs saw the promises afar off and "embraced them." The word is used figuratively there. Hebrews 13:24 says, "Greet all those who rule over you, and all the saints. Those from Italy greet you." Greet your leaders and pastors. First Peter 5:13-14 says, "She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you; and so does Mark my son. Greet one another with a kiss of love."

Second John 13 says, "The children of your elect sister greet you." Third John 14 says, "I hope to see you shortly, and we shall speak face to face. Peace to you. Our friends greet you. Greet the friends by name." Our friends greet you; greet the friends by name. Men and brethren, greeting people is Christian culture. When you first meet them, greet them. After that first meeting, continually maintain the greeting: "How are you? What is going on? I just wanted to reach out to you. I hope everything is fine." It is enough simply to greet.

We are speaking of affectionate greeting. Greet the person with warmth, not coldly, mechanically, or merely as a routine you must complete. Sometimes you are praying for the person as you greet: "I hope all is going well." That is Christian culture.

## Conversation and the Information It Carries

The second expression of friendliness and friendship is conversation. Greetings and salutations are themselves conversations, but we are separating this expression because it concerns the information carried in conversation. You will find that relationships are sustained through conversation, beginning with the first relationship, God and man. God speaks to man: "I made you. I want you to be in my family. I want you to have my life. I made the world for you as one who would be in my family. You did not have to exist; I am self-existent, but I wanted you to exist and to have fellowship with me. Choose this. Of the things I made in the natural, you may partake. Of the trees of the garden you may freely eat."

Do you see different kinds of information? God spoke about natural things and spiritual truth. We are going to see three classes of information. Conversation is what keeps relationship consistent, and revelation often occurs within it. God said concerning Abraham, "Shall I hide from Abraham what I am doing?" God does not relate to a friend by keeping everything distant. A friend greets you. A friend converses with you. Friendship is not merely in the mouth, on paper, or on a certificate; it is expressed in actions. "How can I do that without telling him? I have to go and tell him. He is my friend." That is friendship.

See how conversation begins in Genesis. God gives man a place, tells him what has been made, explains what he may freely eat, identifies the boundary he must not cross, and states the consequence. Natural creation, spiritual purpose, liberty, responsibility, and warning all enter the relationship through words. Man is not left to guess what kind of life God intends. God speaks because fellowship requires known truth.

Then with Abraham, disclosure marks the friendship. The Lord is about to act concerning Sodom and asks, "Shall I hide from Abraham what I am doing?" The disclosure has purpose: Abraham will become a great nation, the nations will be blessed in him, and he will command his household in the way of the Lord. God does not disclose merely to create interesting conversation. He speaks in relation to covenant, intercession, responsibility, and purpose.

Human friendship follows that pattern at its proper level. Greeting opens the person to affection; conversation carries knowledge; disclosure deepens trust; and the information serves purpose. If people only carry the title *friend* but do not greet, converse, know, or disclose anything appropriate, the friendship exists only on paper. The nature of friendship must produce the steps of friendship.

## Three Classes and Eight Kinds of Information

There are three classes of information. The first class is natural versus spiritual. The second class is general versus personal. The third class is observatory versus revelatory. Information can be natural or spiritual. That information, whether natural or spiritual, can also be general or personal. And that information can also be observatory or revelatory. The ability to distinguish which kind pertains to whom is critical in the faith. That is discernment. There are things you are not to discuss with certain people, and things you are to discuss with them. If we put those categories together, we have eight kinds of information in conversation.

Do not treat the three classes as three unrelated lists. Every actual piece of information falls on all three axes. It is either natural or spiritual; at the same time, it is either general or personal; and at the same time, it is either observable or revelatory. That is why the combinations produce eight kinds. "Natural" does not automatically mean public, and "spiritual" does not automatically mean private. "Personal" does not automatically mean secret, and "general" does not automatically mean free to disclose. You must apply all three distinctions.

For example, the fact that you work in a particular profession may be natural, personal, and observable. A confidential plan within that profession may be natural, general to the organization, and revelatory. A doctrine preached publicly from Scripture is spiritual, general, and observable. A leading of the Spirit concerning your own calling is spiritual, personal, and revelatory. If you collapse the categories, you will tell the wrong thing to the wrong person simply because you thought, "It is natural," or, "It is about church." Discernment asks what kind of natural or spiritual information it is, whom it pertains to, and how it became known.

I am going to list them because we will later list all the relationships we have mentioned and identify which conversations happen in each relationship and which do not. Keep the numbers the same, because I will refer back to them rather than repeat every name each time. Number one is natural general observatory information. Number two is natural personal observatory information. Number three is spiritual general observatory information. Number four is spiritual personal observatory information. Number five is natural general revelatory information. Number six is natural personal revelatory information. Number seven is spiritual general revelatory information. Number eight is spiritual personal revelatory information.

Natural information covers material pursuits, science, arts, commerce, natural philosophy or worldview, aesthetics, fashion, governance, economics, and social issues. Existential philosophy usually has spiritual implications, but there are many other questions of worldview that remain natural. They are legitimate subjects of conversation.

You can enter a bus, greet the people there, and join a conversation about governance. You are discussing natural information that is general rather than personal, and observatory rather than revelatory. It concerns matters that are publicly available and can ordinarily be known.

Spiritual information includes existential philosophy or worldview, salvation, spiritual growth, doctrine, ministry, matters of the local church, leadings of the Spirit, visions, revelations, and utterances. When you preach the gospel, for example, you communicate spiritual general observatory information. Salvation and doctrine have been made public in the Word; they are not private disclosures.

Existential philosophy belongs here because questions such as why man exists, what gives life meaning, and what man's final end is ordinarily carry spiritual implications. Salvation is spiritual information. Spiritual growth and doctrine are spiritual information. Ministry and local church matters are spiritual information. So are leadings, visions, revelations, and utterances. But those subjects do not all belong to the same people. The gospel is to be proclaimed publicly. Your private leading is not.

You can tell a stranger, "Christ died for our sins and rose again. You need salvation and a local church where you can grow." That is spiritual general observatory information. The truth has been manifested in the Word. But you do not then add, "The Lord told me when I was young that my work would be in another nation, and this is the vision I saw about it." That is spiritual personal revelatory information. The conversation moved from what God made public to what God disclosed personally to you, and the stranger has not entered the kind of relationship in which that disclosure belongs.

But a leading of the Spirit, vision, revelation, or utterance that is personal to you is spiritual personal revelatory information. There are only a few categories of people with whom you can properly discuss that: mainly core friendship and leadership. You must know the difference. Whether the information is natural or spiritual, some things are general and some are personal. Whether it is general or personal, some things are observatory and some are revelatory.

Matters specific to your local church are not ordinarily discussed with saints generally. There may be exceptional circumstances, but saying, "This is how we do this in our own church," may disclose spiritual general revelatory information. General doctrine is different because it comes from the Word and is publicly taught. But plans, instructions, and internal matters specific to a local church are not merely observatory. You have to be discerning. General information is information that does not pertain personally to an individual.

General information is not personal to the individual; personal information pertains to the individual. Then there is observatory and revelatory information. Observatory information is what people can know by observation. They may not have heard it from anybody; it is simply observable. Revelatory information is disclosed to you.

Even in natural, general matters, there is revelatory information. For example, as a lawyer, people may tell you things by virtue of your professional privilege. You do not divulge that information for any reason. As a doctor, people trust you and expose themselves to you. You do not go and announce that a person has a particular clinical condition. That is not the conduct of a believer. It is natural general revelatory information. Not even your church leaders have any business with it. The same applies when you work in a company and have access to information about what goes on there.

If you divulge such information indiscriminately and use it for casual conversation, you have a bad culture as a believer and need to repent. It is a bad understanding of the practice of friendship. Can someone call you a friend when you handle entrusted information that way? That is not friendship. If you are always drawing information out of people, always leaving your ears open for private information, something is wrong. If your pastor tells you what the church is going to do and you turn it into casual conversation elsewhere, do you not have other things to talk about?

Think about professional privilege. A client tells a lawyer what actually happened, including facts the client would not announce publicly, so the lawyer can give proper counsel. A patient exposes a condition to a doctor because treatment requires it. An employee receives an internal plan because the work requires it. None of them gave you material for entertainment at home. You cannot say, "I told only my spouse," as though marriage transferred every confidence placed in your professional office. Your spouse was not the lawyer, doctor, employee, or officer to whom the disclosure was made.

Even leaders in church have no automatic claim to that information. Spiritual oversight does not cancel a duty of confidence in another sphere. If the law, the professional duty, or the agreement requires you to protect the disclosure, protect it. You can pray about it because God already knows, but you do not convert prayer needs into gossip. There may be an exceptional circumstance involving danger, abuse, or a legal duty to report, but that is handled through the proper channel, not through casual conversation.

The same principle applies inside church. If a pastor tells workers what the church is preparing to do, ask whether it was a public announcement or information given for the work. Do not carry it into another circle merely because everybody there is a believer. Saints generally are not the same relational category as members of that local church, and members generally are not the same as the workers who were entrusted with a particular plan. Relationship and purpose determine where the information belongs.

Do not become the person who is always extracting disclosures. Some people leave their ears open whenever a private matter is mentioned, ask another question, and then carry the answer away. That is not the curiosity of a friend; it is a failure of character. Friendship makes a person safe. A friend does not need to know everything. A friend knows which question not to ask, which answer not to repeat, and which burden to carry without advertising that it was carried.

There are plenty of things to talk about. There is history. You can discuss something that happened in your field. You have left all those things and started talking about revelatory information entrusted to you. You cannot fulfill God's purpose that way, because fulfilling purpose is the reason for relationships. If you mishandle relationships and information, you cannot fulfill God's purpose that way. It is a terrible way to live.

## Discernment in Conversation

Believers must have discernment. Do not talk carelessly. Know what to say to whom and when to say it. Philippians 1:9 says, "And this I pray, that your love may abound still more and more in knowledge and all discernment." The word discernment is `aisthesis`, A-I-S-T-H-E-S-I-S: perception, discernment, or judgment. This applies both in the natural and in the spiritual. Even in natural matters, people will avoid you when you do not understand how to handle revelatory information. That is basic. When you mishandle it, you filter away trust. We must believe the best of people, but you should also make it easy for people to trust you.

You should make it easy for people to trust you. In Luke 9:45, the disciples "did not understand this saying." Jesus had just told them that the Son of Man would be betrayed into the hands of men. They did not understand, for it was hidden from them. They did not perceive it; they could not discern it, and they were afraid to ask him about the saying.

Learn to seek clarity in life. Do not assume. Something happened between me and a friend: I saw the person somewhere and greeted, but the person did not respond. It seemed strange because I could see the person looking at me. But knowing the person, I did not pass a negative judgment. I gave a positive explanation in my mind: probably it was because of this other thing. I accepted that possibility and left it. Later, when we spoke, I said, "I thought I greeted you." The person had not known that I was greeting. The point is that I had already chosen a positive interpretation rather than suspicion. Do the right thing. When you are not sure, ask. Do not sit down suspecting, "What is going on? Why is this happening?" Ask the person. That is part of discernment and friendship.

Discernment is not suspicion. Suspicion invents a negative explanation while lacking the facts. Discernment perceives what is actually present, withholds a false judgment, and seeks clarity when clarity is needed. I could see the person looking in my direction, but I also knew the person's character. So I refused to conclude, "This person deliberately ignored me." I supplied a positive possibility until I could ask. When the matter was explained, there was no needless offense to repair.

Apply that habit broadly. If a friend's message is brief, do not immediately decide the person is angry. If a leader asks a question, do not decide the leader distrusts you. If someone does not disclose a matter, do not decide the person has rejected the friendship. Ask what is appropriate, and remember that the person may be protecting information that does not belong to you. Love abounds not only in knowledge but in all judgment. It knows how to perceive, how to ask, and how to leave a matter alone.

So we said there are eight kinds of information: natural general observatory; natural personal observatory; spiritual general observatory; spiritual personal observatory; natural general revelatory; natural personal revelatory; spiritual general revelatory; and spiritual personal revelatory. The gospel and doctrine are spiritual general observatory facts. They have been put out there. They are not private matters.

Number four is spiritual personal observatory information. For example, the local church where you fellowship is personal to you, but it can ordinarily be found out; it is not necessarily private. Number five is natural general revelatory information. Information about your business or organization may be general rather than personal to you, yet still be a disclosure that is not meant to be given out. Number six is natural personal revelatory information. There may be things about your health, your plans, or your relationship with your parents that are personal to you and not observable. They are disclosures, and you do not speak about them carelessly to everybody. Number seven is spiritual general revelatory information.

Certain things may be said in your church about what the church is going to do. Those are not general facts about the body of Christ; they are specific disclosures to your local church. That is spiritual general revelatory information. We will now look at the different relationships and the information appropriate to each.

Number eight completes the list: spiritual personal revelatory information. This includes a leading, vision, revelation, utterance, or ministry matter specifically disclosed concerning an individual. It may also include a private spiritual struggle or an instruction received under oversight. This is the narrowest category. It does not become public because the content is spiritual. In fact, spiritual language can make careless disclosure more damaging because people attach divine authority to what you repeat.

Keep the eight numbers fixed as we apply them. One: natural general observatory. Two: natural personal observatory. Three: spiritual general observatory. Four: spiritual personal observatory. Five: natural general revelatory. Six: natural personal revelatory. Seven: spiritual general revelatory. Eight: spiritual personal revelatory. The point is not merely to memorize names. The point is to pause in conversation and know, "This is number six; this relationship has not reached that level," or, "This is number seven; both of us belong to the local church to which this instruction was given."

I should also mention that conflict handling and resolution are part of spiritual information. You are not merely discussing people on a natural basis; you are acting as part of your spiritual code of conduct as a believer. Once you have a concern, do not hold it in and say, "I did not want to say it." That is how people create a time bomb.

Something that would have been simple friction becomes a collision because you said, "I did not want to say it." When you speak at the right time, it is only friction; after you discuss it, you have applied grease and there can be ease. Talk. Say it immediately: "I have been concerned. I may be wrong, but what is going on?" That is friction, and it is healthy. Do not wait for collision. If you do not deal with friction, you will eventually have a head-on collision. At that point, the only solution is repair: you need help, you need to meet someone, and you need to discuss what has gone beyond the two of you. Trust has already been damaged because you did not resolve the matter while it was still friction.

## Conversation with Strangers

What do we discuss with strangers? Numbers one and three are the ordinary subjects. Of course, we greet everybody endearingly and affectionately: "How are you? I hope your day is going well. Good evening. I hope everything is well." Then we discuss natural general observatory information: events in the world, science, art, governance, economics, and social issues. You can strike up such a conversation with a stranger. We can also discuss spiritual general observatory information: salvation, spiritual growth, doctrine, the need to belong to a local church, and what the local church is about. The person is still a stranger, so the information is general and observable, whether natural or spiritual.

When you want to move a stranger into acquaintance, you will probably begin to discuss number two, natural personal observatory information. Beyond discussing what is happening in Nigeria while you are both in the car, you ask, "What do you do?" The person says, "I do this. I stay here." Those facts are personal but observable. You may exchange numbers and begin to know each other. You could have remained strangers if neither of you introduced personal information and you simply parted. But now, when you meet again, you can ask, "How is that work going?" The person knows your work or where you stay, and you have become acquaintances.

On the basis of number three, number four may also arise. A conversation about spiritual general information may lead to spiritual personal observatory information: "My own church is this church, and this is what we believe." Or you may use yourself as an example: "I do this to put that doctrine into practice, and I find that it helps." That is not personal revelatory information. It is not a leading of the Spirit, a vision, revelation, or utterance personal to you. It is spiritual personal information that can ordinarily be observed.

The movement from stranger to acquaintance is therefore not mysterious. Conversation introduces a little personal observable knowledge. You know the person's name, work, course of study, part of town, or local church. The person knows the same kinds of things about you. If you never move beyond general facts, you may have had a useful and affectionate conversation but remain strangers. If you exchange personal observable facts and retain contact, you become acquaintances.

Do not force the movement. Receiving a stranger does not mean demanding a phone number, a home address, or a private history. The person may remain a stranger whom you treated well, and that expression is complete in itself. But when conversation develops naturally and both people recognize a reason to remain in contact, personal observatory information gives the relationship a proper next step.

## Conversation with Acquaintances and Relatives

With acquaintances and relatives, just as with strangers, we can discuss numbers one and three. Because the relationship has moved beyond stranger, we will already be discussing number two; that is part of why we are acquaintances. As with the stranger, number four can also arise from a conversation about number three. If we want to move an acquaintance forward into friendship, however, we begin carefully to discuss number six: not spiritual information, but natural personal revelatory information. We disclose more about ourselves to the extent that we want the person to become a friend: "I am actually planning this." We are beginning to trust the person because we have observed that our views align and that we agree on important things.

You have seen that you agree on some things. "I think I can tell you this. I plan to do this and that. What do you think about it?" "Oh yes, I've actually done something like that before." But you cannot tell your plans to a stranger or just any acquaintance. The relationship has to progress. You may say, "What do you think about this thing I have in mind?" The person is moving from acquaintance toward friendship on the basis of trust.

That disclosure should be proportionate. You do not empty your whole life into the first encouraging conversation. You disclose enough to test and build trust: "I have this plan. I have not announced it, but I value your thought." Then watch what the person does with the information. Does the person understand it, protect it, counsel honestly, and remember the purpose for which it was given? Friendship develops as both people prove faithful in these smaller disclosures.

Relatives follow the same social rule. Biological connection does not automatically entitle a relative to every plan. A cousin may remain an acquaintance, while another relative becomes a trusted friend. Do not dishonor kinship, but do not confuse blood relationship with established friendship. Share according to the real relationship, and cultivate closer friendship where there is faith, agreement, and trust.

## Conversation in Secular Relationships

Now, number three: secular relationships. This may be a relationship between colleagues, or a relationship between a leader and another person. In secular relationships, the same rule for strangers and acquaintances strictly applies. Through that secular relationship, you may find that your values align on spiritual things, and that may move the person into friendship. Then you might begin to do the same things we said about acquaintances. But in secular relationships, number five may also occur: natural general revelatory information. Your boss may say, "I can trust this person with some things. I will tell you this." That is not something you tell somebody else. It was told to you for that purpose, whether in politics, business, or another field. You cannot say, "My wife and I are one," and then disclose it to your wife. You cannot do that.

You cannot disclose the information to your wife merely by saying, "My wife and I are one." If you are a doctor, you do not go home and tell your wife the medical conditions of the people who trusted you. That is not godly. If you are a lawyer, people bring complaints and disclose what they are going through so you can help them handle it. You cannot say, "I do not tell anybody except my wife," and then disclose it. You were entrusted with the information because of the professional relationship, and your spouse was not part of that relationship.

Natural general revelatory information can be discussed only among the people in the secular relationship who are privy to it. That is where you discuss, "This is what we are planning. This is our strategy. This is what we are considering and what we intend to do." Those matters may be general to the organization rather than personal to one person, but they remain revelatory information and must be treated as such.

If several colleagues are authorized for the plan, they may discuss it together because all are privy to it. The boundary follows authorization, not affection. You may like another colleague more, but if that colleague is outside the project, you do not bring the person in through conversation. You may trust your parents deeply, but they are outside the company. You may trust your pastor, but the company's strategy is not automatically a church matter. Be faithful where God placed you.

Sometimes a secular relationship grows into friendship because values align. Two colleagues discover that they share faith, integrity, or a common sense of purpose. Then personal information may be discussed according to the friendship. But keep the capacities distinct. When you speak as friends, you still do not disclose what one of you knows only as a professional. The friendship may deepen; the professional boundary remains.

## Conversation with Siblings and Parents

Number four is siblings. First, the rule for acquaintances applies. Depending on the level of friendship, number six can then apply: natural personal revelatory information. Depending on the level of spiritual friendship, other kinds of information may also come into play because a sibling may be a core spiritual friend. You may not have told other people, but this sibling is your friend: you are committed to each other and the person has your back. Of course, you ordinarily discuss observable things and establish trust before sharing a disclosure. You know, "I can tell this person." If the sibling is a core spiritual friend, almost the entire spectrum of appropriate information may be discussed, apart from entrusted information such as number five that does not belong to you to disclose.

The same basic rule applies to parents: begin with the rule for acquaintances, then let the actual extent of friendship govern personal disclosure. But number six, natural personal revelatory information, usually must be disclosed while you are still a dependent child. At that stage, you cannot hide a serious personal matter from your parents as though you are independent. When consequences arise, who will bear the responsibility? Your parents will. While you are dependent, number six is therefore ordinarily compulsory. When you become independent, disclosure may instead occur according to your friendship: "Dad, Mom, I think you should know this. Something is going on, and I wanted you to know." They may then counsel you about it.

Dependence is the key qualification. If your parents remain responsible for your health, school, housing, or the consequences of a decision, you cannot keep the relevant facts as though they concern only you. If you are ill, being threatened, in academic trouble, or entering an agreement they will have to carry, tell them. Personal revelatory information is not a license to hide responsibility. Their role as parents gives them a proper claim to what they need in order to parent.

When you are independent, the parental role changes but does not disappear. You may tell them because they are friends, because their counsel is wise, because the matter affects the family, or simply because affection makes you want them to know. "Something is going on. I have taken responsibility for it, but I wanted to tell you." That is different from compulsory disclosure by a dependent child. The information now follows friendship and relevance rather than direct responsibility.

The goal in both sibling and parental relationships is not secrecy. The goal is truthful relationship with proper boundaries. Some families know everything public about one another and nothing important because they never built friendship. Others demand every detail but cannot carry it safely. Build the kind of relationship in which trust can grow, counsel can be received, and personal information can be handled for purpose.

## Conversation with Saints

Now, number six: saints. Information from number one to number four can be seen in relationships with saints. But anything beyond that is not ordinarily seen there. That means revelatory information, whether natural or spiritual, is not discussed with saints generally. Doctrine is from the Word and is not private, but there are matters specific to your local church that are revelatory rather than merely observable. With saints generally, you can discuss all kinds of observatory information.

If a saint also becomes your friend, the rule of friendship applies. You may then discuss natural personal revelatory information, and, depending on how core and spiritual the friendship is, spiritual personal revelatory information: "I had this experience, and I have been thinking about it," or, "I am planning to do this." Among friends, someone may be surprised and ask, "Have I not told you?" Disclosure is expected because of that particular friendship, not merely because both people are saints.

## Conversation among Local Church Members

Number seven: local church membership. We must greet one another and have bonds of affection with one another. We have seen that information one to four applies. How do you have conversations with people? By questions and comments. Comments can be compliments, explanations, positions, or conclusions. You greet the person cordially and warmly: "How are you doing?" You can compliment something: "This is nice." Then you ask questions: "How is everything? What do you study? What do you do?" There is nothing wrong with that.

Questions and comments are ordinary tools of affection. A comment may be a compliment: "That was well done." It may be an explanation: "This is what that part of the meeting meant." It may be a position: "This is how I understand the doctrine." It may be a conclusion: "Then this is what we should do." Conversation does not require an extraordinary revelation every time. Much of friendship grows through faithful attention to ordinary things.

Do not ask questions mechanically as though you are completing an interview. Listen to the answer and make the next comment from what the person actually said. "You study this course? What does that involve? What part interests you?" Now you know something you did not know before, and the person knows that you were present in the conversation. At another meeting you can remember: "How is that project going?" That continued memory turns a first exchange into an actual relationship.

The person has real things to know about his or her life, and you are supposed to relate with the person. Ask, "What do you study? What do you do? What does that work involve?" You are not probing for private information; you are simply getting to know a fellow member. It is strange to demand that people relate in church and then become suspicious when someone asks an ordinary question. Just relate.

The boundary is that spiritual personal revelatory information is not for local church members merely because they are members. That belongs within leadership or core friendship. You cannot casually ask someone for private results concerning the person's ministry or a particular disciple. Ministry generally may be discussed; the private details of a person's ministry or disciples are personal and may be revelatory. Do not discuss people indiscriminately. Matters concerning a disciple belong with the appropriate leader: your unit leader, extension leader, or pastor. Keep them there.

Number five, natural general revelatory information, remains restricted to people who are already privy to it in the natural setting. Number six, a personal disclosure about your life, does not have to be discussed with a local church member generally. It may, however, need to be discussed with pastors or leaders. If something serious is happening, let them know. If there are parental issues or academic challenges, let the appropriate leader know. That is different from calling just anybody in the local church and disclosing it.

There are many proper subjects to discuss: "I study this course, and this is what it involves." You can ask more because you know something about the field and want to understand the person's view and direction. Or discuss doctrine: "Are you giving attention to this doctrine? You may need to spend more time on it. This is what usually helps me; you can try it." That is spiritual personal observatory information, and it can genuinely help the person. Do not create a problem that does not exist. There are things to discuss and things not to discuss; stay with the proper things and converse in bonds of affection.

Number seven, spiritual general revelatory information, is also available among members of the same local church. You may say, "Pastor gave us an instruction about this, and I plan to act on it. If you do not mind, perhaps we can do it together." Both people received the instruction within the same church. When there are so many right things to discuss, why insist on discussing things you should not?

Local church members can stir one another up around the instruction: "Have you started? This is how I plan to act on it. Let us pray or work on this part together." That is different from discussing a person's disciple, a leader's private counsel, or an internal matter neither of you was authorized to carry. Shared church instruction creates shared spiritual purpose; private oversight remains private.

The local church should therefore be full of conversation without becoming full of gossip. Members can discuss doctrine, meetings, service, growth, studies, work, family facts that are observable, and the assignments given to the whole church. They can encourage one another, explain what helped them, and ask proper questions. A lack of gossip does not require silence. It requires enough purpose and substance that you do not need another person's private life to keep a conversation alive.

This also gives room for natural help. Someone studies a field you understand, works in an industry where you know a useful resource, or is trying to solve a public problem you have encountered. You can say, "I have seen something related to that. Let me send it to you," or, "You may want to speak with this person." No private disclosure was required. You knew the person along observatory lines, listened, and used what you knew to help. That is friendship serving purpose in the ordinary life of the church.

It is also wrong to turn every local church conversation into a formal ministry report. A person is not merely a unit result, a disciple count, or an attendance figure. Know the human being. Know what the person studies, does, enjoys, and is trying to build. Then know the person's observable spiritual life: the doctrine receiving attention, the service being rendered, and the growth the person is pursuing. Affection carries both the natural and the spiritual.

## Core Friends, Spouses, Leaders, and God

Number eight is core friends. They must truly be core: you know them and trust them. You can discuss information one to four and six to eight, still according to the depth of each friendship. You may know, "I can share this information with this particular core friend." Number nine is your spouse. The spouse should be a core friend, so the same range ordinarily applies, though the actual depth of friendship still affects disclosure in some cases.

The range does not include information about other people that was entrusted to you. Keep another person's personal revelatory information to yourself. Do not discuss the lives of your disciples with your spouse if your spouse is not their leader in church. The fact that your spouse is one with you does not make your spouse the leader of everyone you lead. If you disclose such a matter, your spouse may begin looking differently at the person in church without having any authority or responsibility to address the issue. You have created suspicion and involved someone who had no business in the matter.

The appropriate leader knows what to do. The leader may address the matter or call the person and speak with recognized authority: "Brother, I want to talk with you about this." The person does not need more people knowing. There are too many proper things for spouses to discuss; do not select what will create problems.

Suppose a disciple tells you about a struggle. Your spouse may be a wonderful believer and your closest friend, but if your spouse has no responsibility for that disciple, disclosure gives the spouse knowledge without a role. The spouse cannot counsel as the person's leader, cannot correct with recognized authority, and may now carry an impression that affects ordinary fellowship. You have multiplied the burden without helping the disciple.

Take the matter instead to the leader who shares responsibility for the person. That leader can decide whether to speak, wait, pray, or involve another proper authority. Sometimes the leader will say nothing immediately because the person needs time. Sometimes the leader will call the person directly. In either case, only those who need to know are involved. That is how discretion protects both the person and the church.

Number ten is leadership. The range stated for spouses applies, but here what the leader ought to know is not left merely to the fluctuating level of friendship. It is close to an obligation. Trust them because of the place God and his messenger have given them in overseeing you. What they ought to know, make sure they know. I am not saying they must know everything: number five, for example, may be information you have no right to disclose. But when you recognize, "My leaders ought to know this," let them know.

Do not make the leader discover by observation what you had a duty to disclose. If an issue affects your ability to serve, obey an instruction, care for a disciple, remain safe, or fulfill an assignment, ask whether oversight requires knowledge of it. You may say, "I am not sure how much detail is necessary, but this is happening and I think you should know." The leader can then ask what is relevant and leave what is not.

Alignment also means reporting back. If a leader says, "Look at this again and return to me within a week," do not disappear because the answer changed. Return and say, "I checked it. I was mistaken," or, "I still see it this way, and these are the facts." Honest disagreement brought back into alignment is better than outward agreement followed by a private plan.

Lastly, with God, all information can be discussed, including what someone told you in confidence at the office. You do not disclose it to another person, but you can pray: "God, you know what they told me. Take control of what is happening in this office." God is supposed to know everything.

## Handling Conflict before Friction Becomes Collision

We have seen that spiritual conversation includes spiritual issues, natural conduct, and conflict handling. Conflict usually becomes more significant among friends and spouses because increased closeness creates more opportunity for friction. Even with acquaintances, however, you should resolve matters properly. Abraham called Lot and said, "Please let there be no strife between you and me." When friction appears, call one another to order: "What happened? Let us resolve it. You can go this way, and I will go this way."

Jesus gives the same principle: if your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault between you and him alone. Do not keep it inside. If he does not hear, take one or two more; then, if necessary, tell the church. The first movement is direct conversation, not wider disclosure.

Among friends, there may already be an agreement that, "We cannot keep doing this whenever something happens. We are committed to one another, so we must learn how to live with one another." Friends do have differences; they do not wish the differences away, but neither do they abandon the relationship. They agree on how to handle the next disagreement. That is conflict handling and resolution.

Where there is close contact, there will be some friction. I am not speaking of collision. Do not allow collision. When friction appears, say, "I am feeling something about this, and I will not pretend otherwise." Then resolve it. Agree that once either person begins to feel troubled, the person will speak. Do not move on while both parties merely hide disagreement. Talk until there is genuine understanding, unless you are looking for a time bomb. Once all parties genuinely understand and agree, you have applied grease to the friction and created ease.

Resolution does not always mean both people prefer the same option. It means the actual concern has been named, each person understands the other, and they know how they will proceed. "When this happens again, do not walk away; tell me." "If I need space, I will say so rather than punish you with silence." Those are practical agreements. They keep friction from becoming a head-on collision.

Do it early. A small matter can be discussed between two friends in minutes. After months of unspoken interpretation, the same matter may require leaders, counsel, and the repair of damaged trust. Speaking early is not being quarrelsome. It is part of the spiritual code of friendship. You value the relationship enough to protect it from accumulated offense.

## Revelation, Building, Shared Activity, Support, and Honor

Having said that, let us look at the remaining expressions. We are almost winding down; we want to get to direct instruction, which is about taking initiative. When I go anywhere, I greet. I do not wait to be greeted or merely expect others to greet me. Concerning my core relationships, I ensure that I regularly keep up with people. That means regularly greeting them, reaching out, discussing issues, and staying in touch.

Look at the epistles of John. He did not always have to write, but he wanted to address an issue and also said, "I want to see your face." It was a way of keeping in touch: how would he know what was going on if they did not remain in contact? It was the same with the elect lady. He had heard that something was happening, warned her to be careful, and then said that he hoped to see her face. Friendship requires that continued contact. You have to keep in touch. You must do that.

The third expression is revelation. In relationships, you must know when to reveal things. That is how friendship moves forward. And there are things not to reveal. "This is me. This is who I am. These are some things about what I am trying to do. In the natural, these are my goals and plans. This is what I know by God's calling on my life. These are things that could possibly happen."

That is revelation, and it is how relationships move forward. The fourth expression is building, which may include training, teaching, chastening, and rebuke. Building applies mainly to core friendships, though it can also occur in natural or secular friendship. Two students, for example, may help one another academically: "What about this? Have you considered that?" When I know the person's vision and goal, I want to give the person tools, resources, and help to get there. I care about the person's character and spiritual purpose. I want the person to be built as God desires so the person can do what God wants.

You must believe that there is a purpose for the person's life, something God wants to do, and be invested in its happening. Building does not merely occur as things come up. You pray and act intentionally: "What result is this person's life supposed to have? How will that happen?" When you see something obstructing that result, you pray about it, help, instruct, and edify the person.

The fifth expression is shared activity in shared purpose or pleasure. This can and should be planned. Friends may plan to eat together, study together, pray together, serve together, or pursue a cause together. The point is that they actually share activity rather than calling themselves friends while never doing anything together.

The sixth expression is support and help for the other person's vision. You cannot be competing with your friend. Support is similar to building, but building concerns the person's own growth, spiritually and naturally. Support lends partnership to the person's cause: your presence, time, thought, truth, resources, prayer, or practical help. You ask, "What are you trying to do? What do you need here? How can I be present for your success?" Whatever you can properly contribute to the person's success is part of friendship.

The seventh expression is honor and material giving. That also should be planned. You must deliberately plan building, shared activities in purpose or pleasure, and honor expressed through material giving. Support for a person's vision may sometimes arise as you go rather than through a fixed plan, but it must still be real. This is not only marital; it is friendship, and it applies equally in church. When someone tells you what he or she is doing, are you thinking, "What can I do to help? How can this work? What have I heard or read that may help? What will this person need?"

Honor may be verbal, practical, or material. You remember a birthday, celebrate a result, give toward a need, or provide something connected with the person's purpose. Material giving is not payment for friendship. It is an expression that says, "I value you, I remember you, and I want to contribute to your good." Because it is easy to postpone, plan it. Decide ahead of time that your friends will not know your affection only through words.

Shared activity also needs breadth. Pleasure is legitimate: friends may eat, rest, travel, play, or enjoy an interest together. Purpose is equally necessary: pray, study, serve, plan, and work together. If every shared activity is pleasure, the friendship may never carry calling. If every shared activity is work, affection may become exhausted. Friendship can hold both shared purpose and shared pleasure in their proper places.

Building may require words the person does not immediately enjoy. A friend can teach, correct, rebuke, or say, "That is not the agreement we made before God." The aim is not control. You are not molding the person after your private taste; you are helping the person become what both of you recognize from God's Word and calling. The same friend must also be willing to receive that ministry in return.

Also distinguish what you are doing for yourself. If you and a friend are doing business together, that is partnership, not merely help for the other person's vision. It is a joint pursuit in which both of you are invested.

You are both jointly invested. If it is help, let it be help. Always be clear in life; that is why agreement matters. If two friends are doing something as a business, they are business partners. It is not one person merely helping the other as a friend. They know each other as friends, but in that matter they are business partners. Do not muddy the waters by calling it friendship when it is convenient and then refusing the responsibilities of partnership. Separate the issues.

Support for another person's vision is different from a shared project. In a shared project, both parties are jointly invested in the same pursuit. In support, the vision belongs to the other person, and you lend what can help: your presence, time, thought, resources, prayer, counsel, or practical partnership. You ask, "What are you trying to do? What do you need here? What can I contribute to help this succeed?" That is part of being a friend.

Building is also distinct from support. Building concerns the person's own growth, spiritually and naturally, so that the person becomes able to do what God wants. Support concerns the cause or pursuit the person is carrying. A true friend is interested in both: the person must grow, and the person's God-given work should succeed. Neither expression is limited to marriage. It applies to committed friendship and to relationships in the church. When someone tells you what he or she is doing, you should sometimes be thinking, "What can I do to help this person? Is there something I have heard, read, or learned that would help?"

## Definite Commitments in Friendship

This is where we want to wrap up. You will find that every part of relationship in the faith of Christ, every expression of friendliness, involves conversation. We have seen the expressions, but now you must take definite steps of conversation. The first step is a commitment to greeting. You must commit to greeting. As touching all humanity, this is my commitment: I will always greet people warmly. That is basic. Anywhere I am, I will warmly greet people. I will affectionately greet people in church after meetings. After a commitment to greeting comes a commitment to cultivating friendships by conversation. That begins in the local church.

I will ensure that I know people in my local church in the natural general and personal observatory sense and in the spiritual general and personal observatory sense. I want to know what they do, what they think about, and what they are pursuing, without probing into disclosures that do not belong to me.

I once heard a young man who works around Lagos start-ups speak against the usual idea of networking. Many of the friends who later recommended him for opportunities, including a job, did not look influential when they met. In his own humorous description, they all looked like young men without a future. They became friends not because one was trying to extract an opportunity from the other, but because they shared interests: football, art, books, reading, and similar passions. In those conversations they also saw that they thought alike and expected to go somewhere in life. Later, those ordinary friendships became materially helpful. That is different from approaching every relationship with the question, "What can I get from this person?"

Know people in the natural and spiritual, general and personal observatory sense. Build connection that way. You have committed to greeting all people affectionately; now commit to knowing people in the local church along observatory lines. You know, "This is what you do; perhaps this may help you." You also know observable spiritual things about them.

Carry this practice into secular relationships. Know people in your class or workplace. Some relationships may remain natural. With some people, spiritual conversation may arise, and you may be led to preach the gospel. Try to know people to a proper degree and be affectionate toward all. In the church, greet all the brethren and know as many as possible in your unit, extension, and the wider church. In a class or workplace you may not know everyone at the same depth, but cultivate appropriate friendship with as many as you can along observatory lines.

That is decent and Christian. You are in church, so you talk to people and know them over time. It is not strange; it is normal. That is what Paul did and why he had friends in many places: he discussed the right things with the right people. The purpose of practicing friendliness and friendship in Christ is to fulfill purpose: your purpose, their purpose, and God's purpose together.

So the first commitment is greeting and salutation across all humanity. The second is cultivating friendship with all members of the local church along observatory lines. The third is cultivating appropriate friendship in natural or secular relationships, mainly through natural observatory information but possibly also through spiritual conversation.

The fourth, and very important, commitment concerns core friendship. You commit to cultivating and maintaining core friendships. That is work. You have to keep them deliberately. If communication wanes and you stop maintaining conversation, the relationship eventually disappears. People say, "Their relationship is strong," but those people made it strong. It did not happen by magic. Do not say, "I do not really have strong relationships; that is just how I am." You have not worked at them.

Two people can be co-leaders or co-workers in church and build a relationship that advances the course of the gospel in that local church because they chose to do so. They inspire one another because they chose to. I remember a friend who lived farther down the same area where I lived. At night, before he went home, he would come to my place and we would talk about ministry. We encouraged one another and stirred ourselves up. The next day you wanted to pray and act: "We must do this. You must deliver this, and I must deliver that."

At one point we kept saying, "We must break sixty," meaning sixty people in attendance. We believed that if we did not break that barrier, we would remain stuck at that level. So we challenged one another, assigned responsibilities, prayed, worked, and eventually broke sixty. Those conversations were part of the work. You have to cultivate friendship deliberately. Call people on their birthdays. Send something when appropriate. If the friendship is not developing, it may be because you are not doing the work. Core friendships must be developed, and you must be committed to them.

The number itself is not the universal point. The point is that we shared a definite ministry objective and held one another to the steps. One person had something to deliver; the other person had another part. When one failed, the other did not pretend the goal had never been stated. We returned to it: "We must break this barrier. What do you need to do? What do I need to do?" Friendship gave the purpose a human structure of prayer, encouragement, correction, and action.

This is what it means to know the result your friend is supposed to have. The result is not merely, "We remain in contact for many years." What is the person meant to become? What work is the person meant to fulfill? What weakness must be overcome? What resource or discipline may help? You do not take over the person's life, but you refuse to be indifferent to the outcome.

Regular conversation should therefore include more than updates. Ask, "How is the thing God placed in your heart? What step did you say you would take? What happened with the challenge we prayed about?" Listen without turning every call into an inspection, but do not let affection lose purpose. Sometimes the friend needs comfort; sometimes the friend needs a reminder; sometimes both of you need to pray again.

Maintain honor as well as correction. A friendship in which one person only points out faults will become heavy. Remember labor, celebrate faithfulness, give thanks, and say what you see God doing. Paul could correct churches strongly and still name individuals as beloved, approved, fellow workers, and people who labored much. Building includes strengthening what is right, not merely attacking what is wrong.

## Full Alignment with Leadership

Lastly, you must be fully aligned with the leadership of the church. We have spoken about relationship with God: maintain a strong relationship with him, discuss all things with him, pray over them, examine them from the Word by the Spirit, and be led by him. But you must also be fully aligned with God's appointed leadership. I say *fully aligned* because, although discussion and friendship are involved, the defining issue in this relationship is alignment. The intention even in leadership is friendship, but friendship with leadership must not exist outside alignment. People who truly want to align become friends with you. They explain what is happening, ask questions, and are honest. Generally, those people get results.

I look at them and see what they are doing because their heart is there. They ask questions. I may say, "What you said is not clear. Look at this again, decide whether you should change it, and report back to me within this period." They are fully aligned with the leadership.

There have been people who came back and said, "Sir, I want to look at it again. I am not sure it is like this. I think I should wait and check for a week." Then they returned and explained what they found. I watch people like that, and generally they get results. Their heart is in it. They ask a question, examine the matter, and report back. That is alignment.

Their first answer was not treated as a performance they now had to defend. They were free to say, "I need to check again," because alignment values truth more than appearance. Then they did the work and returned within the period. That return matters. A person can sound humble by asking for more time and still be unaligned if the person never reports back. Alignment closes the loop.

When I watch such people, I see that their heart is in the instruction. They do not merely say yes in the room. They take the question home, examine the facts, make the correction if needed, and bring back a clear answer. That is why they often get results. Leadership can actually help them because the conversation remains open from instruction through action to report.

The conversation in leadership is that they are aligned. If something is not right, they ask a question. If there is a problem, they say so. They may say, "I do not know this person, but I have been thinking about it and I am not sure everything is all right. I think you should know that this is going on." That is trust and alignment. The opposite would be someone working against the leadership, doing something other than what was agreed, plotting a different plan, or playing a game. That would be a problem.

If someone is working against leadership, withholding what should be disclosed, plotting another plan, or using the relationship for a private game, that is a problem. In leadership, there must be alignment. If I hold you as a leader, I will not hold back what I ought to tell you. Withholding information would suggest competition or a different game plan. I do not play those games. I will tell you what you ought to know.

The relationship must be clear: "This may not be the same vision as another assignment, but this is my home, and you should know what I am doing." There is no gimmick. "This is what we do every day. On this day, we do this. This is what is happening." If there is a problem, you can identify it because the plan is open. When it comes to leadership, be fully aligned. Check it daily and weekly.

## Local Church Focus, Discretion, and Purpose

Ask yourself: Am I doing these things in my relationships? Am I greeting all people and showing affection to all? Am I cultivating friendships with members of my local church? Am I cultivating appropriate relationships in the natural? In your workplace, what kind of human being are you? Cultivate relationship along natural observatory lines. Those conversations may later become spiritual, but they begin mainly in the natural. Know what the person is trying to do personally: "This may be the way forward. You should probably speak with this person." When you learn what the person is going through, you may be able to say, "You should speak with your local church leadership. This practice also helped me; perhaps it may help you."

Someone once told me he was losing time through constant phone use and did not understand why he kept using it even when tired. I sent him a voice note and explained that when a person becomes disappointed with the results of life, the person often looks for distraction. The issue was not merely, "Stop using the phone," or, "Time yourself." The better question was, "What do you want to be doing with your life? Plan that, rather than making the whole plan about not using the phone." It seemed basic to me because I say it in church, but it helped him understand what was happening. Sometimes a simple spiritual observation can help someone you know in the natural.

When people seek more specifically spiritual direction, I often direct them to their own church. Someone may say, "Give me a prophecy," and I say, "Ask your church leaders." I may explain what such an experience typically means, but I return the person to the proper leadership. The larger point is simple: relate with people. Relate naturally with people generally, with fellow saints beyond your congregation, and especially with people in your local church.

Take your local church seriously. That is where God called you. Your local church does not become better because you focus on other local churches. It fulfills its own purpose as you focus on what God is doing in your church. What you focus on grows. That is why I deliberately focus on my local church. I may appreciate what another church is doing, have no problem with it, and even give toward it. But if my attention remains there, I will not do what God told me to do here.

Ask, "What is God saying here? What is God doing here? What is God telling us to do?" That is where your attention should be. This is your local church, and this is how you fulfill God's purpose in it. You should still have proper relationships with believers elsewhere, but always draw the line and know the difference.

This does not mean isolation or competition with other churches. Be thankful for what God is doing elsewhere. Learn what is publicly available, pray for the saints, support a worthy work when led, and maintain relationships across the body of Christ. But appreciation is not assignment. Another church's emphasis cannot replace the instruction God is giving through the leaders responsible for you.

Attention is a resource. If most of your conversation, observation, excitement, and criticism are directed toward churches to which you do not belong, your own church receives what is left. Then you wonder why local relationships are weak and local assignments do not grow. Focus is not hostility to others. It is stewardship of the place where God called you.

Know the brethren here. Carry the instructions here. Help the work here. Ask what God is forming in this house and what part you have in it. Your purpose will still connect with the wider body, but it must have a real place of obedience. A believer who is everywhere in conversation and nowhere in responsibility will not fulfill the purpose of relationship.

Maintain your core friendships. Cultivation is not enough; you work at them so they become what they are supposed to be. As you cultivate relationships in the local church, some people will become more closely aligned with you. Maintain those core friendships. Know what information to share with whom, distinguish the categories, and have discretion.

The same work applies to your spouse. Plan conversation and shared activity in core and spousal friendship: "We have to talk like this. We have to do this together." In everything, exercise discretion. Many things lose value when they lose discretion. Many things do not need publicity. Treat what is supposed to be secret as sacred. Do not look for the world's validation of your relationship. If the relationship is what it is, keep it in its proper place. That is Christian and decent. Scripture teaches believers to mind their own business and live their own lives. Our life is not a show.

Public display can consume the energy that should have built the actual relationship. You begin to ask how the friendship appears rather than whether the friend is growing, whether trust is strong, and whether God's purpose is being fulfilled. A photograph, public greeting, or celebration may be appropriate, but it must not become proof that the relationship exists. The private work must remain primary.

Do not use a respected friend for clout: "I want people to know that I know this person." Do not turn access into a public asset. That changes your posture from service to self-validation and may make the other person unsafe with you. Treat sacred things as sacred. Let the weight, value, and worth of the friendship remain in what it actually accomplishes before God.

Your life is not a film that people are watching; it is a pursuit of purpose. Focus on that. You cannot look up and down at the same time. You are either acting in a film or living in reality. Do not turn what is supposed to be reality into drama. Do not use your core friendships for drama or clout. Do not say, "I want people to know that I am connected with this person." Do not use friendship to chase validation. Treat what is meant to be secret as sacred, and let the relationship serve its actual purpose.

## Work at Friendship

Do not do that. Men and brethren, I have said what I need to say on relationships in the faith of Christ. I want us to practice friendliness as a people and as a church. It was important that we taught this before marital matters, because marital matters are only a subset. This is about how to treat and relate with people generally as friends. Commit to affectionate greeting and salutation, to a welcoming and hospitable spirit. Commit to hospitality. Commit to cultivating friendships and being friendly in the local church. Commit to being friendly in the natural. Know people along observatory lines, naturally and spiritually, including saints generally. Then commit to maintaining core friendships. Maintain them. Work at them. Ask, "How can we make this better?" It is work.

It is an assignment. Have a goal: "I want this friendship to become this with this person. How can I and this particular leader work together? How can I and this particular worker work together? I have seen that our commissions align. How can we make it work for God's purpose?" Paul and Barnabas made it work, even though there was a problem later. See how Barnabas worked at it. He went to look for Paul and brought him in. It did not merely happen by magic or because God placed them together. Relationship is cultivated. You have to have faith and trust. Drop suspicion and believe the best. Even when you see contradiction, hope against hope.

Barnabas did not sit in Antioch saying, "If God wants Paul here, God will bring him." Acts says Barnabas departed for Tarsus to seek Saul, and when he found him, he brought him to Antioch. For a whole year they assembled with the church and taught many people. God had called Paul, but Barnabas still took relational steps: he looked, found, brought, stayed, and worked. Orchestration did not remove cultivation.

Apply that to the worker or leader with whom you recognize shared commission. Do not only admire the possibility: "We could do much together." Ask what conversation, trust, schedule, shared activity, or clarified agreement would make it real. Perhaps you need to visit, call regularly, pray together, share a ministry target, or resolve an old misunderstanding. Faith takes definite relational steps.

There may later be a disagreement, as there was with Paul and Barnabas, but that does not erase what cultivation accomplished. Their earlier work strengthened the church and advanced the gospel. Do not use the possibility of future pain as an excuse never to build present friendship. Believe the best, deal with contradiction faithfully, and work at the relationship for God's purpose.

Say, "It will be great. It is God's will and purpose. This will work out right. The person will turn out this way." Have the right heart toward your friends and build genuine friendship. You may see that a friend is becoming worldly. Stand in the place of prayer, and also talk to the person. You may not simply say, "You are becoming worldly," but you can say, "I am concerned. I notice that you are putting attention on this. God called us. We are supposed to do this together, and we are going to do things in the nations of the earth. We cannot be going this way." Speak by faith. See the future and what God has called the person to do.

Even if the person is no longer listening to you in the same way, the important thing is not that the person must listen to you. The person may listen to someone else. What matters is that the person follows God and does not miss God. Be that friend.

Work at it. Strive to see your friends turn out the way they are supposed to. Relationship in the faith of Christ is work. It is practice. It is taking definite steps all the time. You must spell out those steps. There must be definite commitment. Know the commitment ahead of time, and do not drop it.

Core friendship is a long-term commitment: commitment to regular conversations, commitment to knowing the result the person is supposed to have, commitment to pursuing that result, commitment to supporting the person to get that result, and commitment to building the person. In core friendship, you have to know the person's purpose. You cannot run away from that, and you must be committed to making it work. You must know the result the person is supposed to have in life and be committed to seeing it happen. The person must do well naturally, do well spiritually, fulfill God's purpose and plan, and not keep shaking and faltering.

Long-term commitment does not mean controlling every decision. It means remaining committed to the God-defined result through changing seasons. At one time the person may need practical help; at another, prayer; at another, a hard conversation; at another, room to carry responsibility personally. The form of support changes, but the concern remains: this person must do well naturally, do well spiritually, and fulfill God's purpose.

Know enough of the person's purpose to recognize what strengthens or weakens it. If the person is called to teach, is the person growing in the Word and learning to communicate? If the person carries responsibility in ministry, is character keeping pace with opportunity? If the person is building something in the natural, is spiritual life being sacrificed to ambition? A core friend watches these movements in love and speaks before a small concession becomes the direction of a life.

You may see your friend defaulting again and again, making concession after concession until the person's life has become a concession. You cannot be unconcerned and merely watch. A Christian life that is being conceded to the devil through all sorts of compromise should concern you. If you are not praying and speaking, you are not being a friend. A friend says, "No. There is another way. This way you are going is not the way. You do not have to go this way."

Men and brethren, what would the church be like if we were all like that, if we were actually friends? What would happen to the weak brother? How would the strong become stronger and the weak be made stronger? What would it be like when the countenance of each one is sharpened by another?

We would all walk effectively in what God wants us to do. What would it be like when your interest is not selfish and personal, but is actually the good of the other person? That is what Jesus wants. That is what the Spirit is working in us. Let us pray for ourselves concerning all our friendships.

## Prayer for True Friendship

Lord, concerning all the friendships that I have, help me to be a true friend. Help me to practice friendship. Help me to commit to friendliness toward all men and to be a genuine friend to the people you have given to me. Help me to be a true friend. I pray that I will be a genuine friend as I see in God's Word. I do not only have the nature of friendship; I take the steps of friendship. I take the steps of friendship. I take the steps of friendship. I commit to all men in friendliness and affectionate hospitality. I take the steps of friendship. I commit to the members of my local church. I am committed to knowing them and helping them as much as I can.

I take the steps of friendship toward the people I know in the natural and the saints I know generally. I take steps to know them, to be affectionate toward them, and to help where I can. Most importantly, I take the steps of friendship in my core friendships: to maintain them, to know the results they are supposed to have and the things they are supposed to fulfill, and to be dedicated to their fulfilling them. Lord, it is not on my watch that they will falter, fail, shake, or fall. I am standing for them. I am praying for them. I am trusting for them. I am believing for them. I trust your Word and your power, your Spirit at work in them. There is a way.

I know you are working in them and guiding their steps by the power of the Holy Ghost. They are going to turn out the way the Lord desires them to turn out. I trust his power and his name. They will go one step after another. I will be involved in building them, even through shared activities, not only activities for pleasure but activities for purpose. I will aid them, guide their steps, help them get it right, and help them do the things the Lord wants them to do. I will support their cause and help to see their vision fulfilled, spiritually first and also naturally. Lord, I am that true friend. Lord, I am like you. Lord, I am like Paul, like Abraham, and like the men of Scripture.

I refuse strife. I know what to do to maintain friendships, to ensure that conflicts remain friction and do not become collisions. I am a true friend by the power of the Holy Ghost, by the nature and the steps of my new nature. I am a true friend in all that I do. Lord, I am fully aligned with leadership, and I am a genuine friend to every person you have made a leader to me. I am fully aligned to the cause and vision I share with them. No games, no tricks. Lord, I walk in the path you have set for me, working with partners in the work of the ministry, taking my place and fulfilling my role. Lord, I am playing my part. I am a true friend.

My testimony before all will be, "That is a friend." To the brethren, that is a friend. To leaders, that is a friend. To strangers, that is a friend. Genuine friendships, Lord, not friendships patterned after the world, not political or manipulative friendships, but spiritual friendships by the Spirit of the living God. That is who I am, Lord. That is what I have. That is what I do. True friendships all the way.

By the testimony of our friendships, let God's purpose be fulfilled. Let the kingdom be expanded. Let the church grow. Let godly character be formed. Let the vision and purpose you have given each person be fulfilled. I pray for that brother and for that sister. On my watch, under your Word and by your Spirit, let every friend fulfill purpose. Lord, I pray for myself and for my role in the lives of my friends. I am a true friend, like Jesus and like Paul. I have the nature of a friend, and I take the steps of a friend. I take the steps of friendship in Christ.

## Closing

Lord, we are grateful for what you are working in us by your Word, and for the steps you have guided us to take. We commit to those steps one after another, to imitate you just as we find in your Word, in the name of our Lord Jesus. Amen. Men and brethren, God bless you. Love you. Believe the best for you, and do well to you.
