# Relationships in the Faith of Christ (1)

Father, I am so glad that I am your child and a part of your family. In this season, gather with us in your mercy and let us receive of your word light, truth, and revelation of Christ and his work in all its fullness, and of his fullness. In the name of the Lord Jesus. Amen.

We are looking at relationships in the faith of Christ. This part of the teaching is the practice, or walk, of friendliness and friendship. Let me say from the start that the intention of this teaching is not simply marital matters, though it may be useful in such endeavors. The inspiration of the teaching and the reason behind it is something much more important than that.

## The Church Exists for the Perfecting of the Saints

Paul says in Ephesians 4 that the Lord Jesus himself gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, and the pastor-teachers, for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ, till we all come to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. We should no longer be children, tossed to and fro and carried about with every wind of doctrine, but, speaking the truth in love, should grow up into Christ in all things. From him the whole body, joined and knit together by what every joint supplies, according to the effective working by which every part does its share, causes growth of the body for the edifying of itself in love.

We read that text almost all the time as a church, and I will explain why it is quite an important text. I consider it perhaps the most effective text with which to begin a Christian meeting because it captures in full many things that our faith involves. It captures what happens from the point of being in the faith up to the very end of this age, you might say, in our walk in the faith. You can always link that text with every other detail in scripture about what the Christian faith is about.

Today we are using that introduction to express an important thought. The reason the Lord Jesus gave the leadership of the church, what we call apostles, prophets, evangelists, and pastor-teachers, is singular. It is very important to note that there is just one reason, and that reason is the perfecting of the saints.

Correct the mindset that says he gave them for three separate reasons: for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry, and for the edifying of the body of Christ. That is not correct. There is one reason. The other two things, the work of the ministry and the edifying of the body of Christ, are applications, one leading to the other. The perfecting of the saints has an effect on the work of the ministry, and the work of the ministry has an effect on the edifying of the body of Christ.

So what is the reason for the church? There is that singular reason: the perfecting of the saints. If that is the case, then the bulk of our attention should be on the perfecting of the saints. Interestingly, that is also true about Paul in this text. Three of the verses following verse 12 focus on the perfecting of the saints, while verse 16 focuses on the work of the ministry and the edifying of the body of Christ. That shows where our attention ought to be.

As a believer, when I am in church, my attention should be on the perfecting of the saints. I am there to be perfected as a saint. Ephesians 4:13-15 starts to expand upon that issue.

Paul's distribution of attention is important. Verse 12 names the perfecting of the saints, the work of the ministry, and the edifying of the body of Christ. Then verses 13, 14, and 15 keep explaining the perfecting of the saints: coming to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, no longer being children tossed by doctrine, and growing up into Christ in all things. Only in verse 16 does he return explicitly to every part doing its share and the body edifying itself in love.

So the text itself places the weight where our attention should be. The leadership gifts are not given merely to gather crowds or perform ministry in front of passive saints. They perfect the saints. Perfected saints then do the work of the ministry. Through that work the body is edified. One produces the next. If the saints are not being brought to the point God has marked, the chain is interrupted at its beginning.

## Perfecting Is First About Knowledge

He says, "Till we all come to the unity of the faith." The perfecting, the *katartismos*, means bringing someone to a particular marked completion point. What is the point to which we are being brought? He says it is the unity of the faith. He calls it the knowledge of the Son of God. That is quite important. It is about knowledge.

The point God is leading us to is a point in knowledge, an end point in knowledge: the unity of the faith, the knowledge of the Son of God. Then he calls that the perfect man, the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. Very clearly, the level of growth God expects us to have now that we are in his family and in this faith is defined by knowledge.

You do not find it defined first by certain conduct, though this text is really about conduct too. It is defined first by knowledge because the knowledge is going to lead to the conduct, as we shall see. God is keen on the knowledge that we have. Do you have the knowledge of the word that he wants you to have? If I say that a brother or sister has developed to the measure of the stature of Christ, I am saying the person has that knowledge of the faith.

When it comes to the Christian faith, knowledge is an end in itself. Of course, it is also a means to another end, to the works and expressions we are supposed to have. But knowledge is first an end in itself. You need to know. He wants you to know. You have to ensure first that you know. There is no evading the knowing. There is no circumventing the knowing. There is no way around it.

You are not to know partially. You are not to know mostly. You are not to know almost completely. You are to know entirely. God is big on knowing, on knowledge. He wants us to know. Verse 13 speaks of the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, and verse 14 says that we should no longer be children.

Notice that he has not yet said anything other than knowledge. He has not yet said conduct or works. Knowledge defines your level of growth. When you know, you have this level of growth, and that makes you a child no longer tossed to and fro. The goal there is first knowledge, knowledge that brings stability.

Sometimes people think you are too critical or too particular about this detail and that detail, dotting the i's and crossing the t's. But you have to be precise. You have to be precise about the outline of the message, the content of the message, and the explanation, so that you are sure you know what you know well. You should be able to define things and see things the way you are supposed to. Otherwise you will remain a child, tossed to and fro by every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men in cunning craftiness and deceitful scheming.

Knowledge is not incidental to growth. Paul does not first say that a mature person is the one who has performed a certain visible activity. He says we come to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God. He calls that the perfect man and the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. Then he describes its result: stability. The person is no longer a child carried in every direction by whatever teaching arrives next.

That is why you cannot settle for partial knowledge, mostly correct knowledge, or almost complete knowledge. You are to know entirely. You need to know the outline so that one truth is not detached from another. You need to know the content so that the words actually say what God says. You need to know the explanation so that you can distinguish one issue from another. Precision is not needless criticism when the alternative is instability.

There is no evading the knowing. There is no path around it where conduct somehow develops independently of truth. Knowledge is an end in itself because God wants his children to know him and know what he has done. It is also the means to another end because the knowledge supplies the steps of the walk. First know. Ensure that you know. Then let what you know produce its proper expression.

## Truth Rather Than the World's Categories

When we do not see things or know things the way we are supposed to, other people will influence how we see and do things. We live in a funny world today where, rather than believers looking into God's word to find their identity, you see them looking at the world and at the different things it tells them. People speak of different personality types, melancholic and the rest, and begin to define themselves by those things.

Of course there is room for individual personality, but we are talking about character. This is something you must get straight: character in scripture, for the believer and by the Spirit, is the same. Character is the same. Personality may differ. Perhaps a person has a passion for soccer; that and many other influences can become part of the person's personality. But character is the same. We must separate those issues.

One psychologist puts "doctor" behind his name and tells you that you are this, and you begin to confuse yourself. Another philosopher says something else, and you confuse yourself again. It has become so bad that believers sometimes discuss mental health and mental state in ways shaped more by what they have read than by truth. There is no question about the reality of mental unhealthiness, but some people expose their minds to something, receive its influence, and then begin to act out what they read.

Nothing is without spiritual influence in this world. Demons get involved. Information is not neutral. You can allow an influence because of the information you receive. So we must be careful. Paul says that we should no longer be children tossed to and fro by every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men in cunning craftiness and deceitful scheming. Tossed to and fro, tossed to and fro, by every wind of doctrine.

Then verse 15 says, "Speaking the truth in love, may grow up into him in all things." For the believer, then, our concern is: what is the truth about this issue? Notice that he says "in all things." There is truth about all things, truth about the different areas or aspects of our lives. There is what the truth says about every area of life.

There is a way Christ is in the demonstration of the Spirit. There is a way Christ is in the walk of the Spirit. There is a way Christ is in his relationships. There is a way Christ is in his material pursuit. There is a way Christ is in the pursuit of his spiritual purpose. We are to grow into Christ in all things by hearing what the truth has to say about everything.

The phrase "in all things" means that no area of the believer's life is to be surrendered to the world's categories. We do not receive our identity from a personality scheme and then tell scripture what kind of character is possible for us. We hear the truth about character. We hear the truth about mental and emotional life. We hear the truth about material pursuit, spiritual gifts, purpose, and relationships. In each area we grow up into Christ.

Personality can account for preferences, interests, and modes of expression. One person likes soccer; another does not. One speaks more quietly; another is more animated. Many influences enter that level of life. But the fruit of the Spirit is not divided into personality types. Love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control are the character of the Spirit for every believer. Character is the same even where personality differs.

## Why Christ Perfects the Saints

There is an area of our life about which we need to hear the word of God. Before I get to it, let us ask the question we often ask: why exactly is the Lord Jesus particular about the perfecting of the saints? He says we come to church to be perfected as saints in these different areas of our lives. Why is he particular about that?

We can answer by looking at what leads up to the instruction. Ephesians 4:7-10 shows that the fact preceding it is that Christ died and was raised from the dead. "To each one of us grace was given according to the measure of Christ's gift. Therefore he says: When he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, and gave gifts to men."

The ascension follows his descent. He first descended into the lower parts of the earth, and the one who descended is also the one who ascended far above all the heavens, that he might fill all things. He is speaking of Jesus' death and then his resurrection, spiritually and physically.

The Lord gave the apostles, prophets, evangelists, and pastor-teachers for the saints after he died and rose again. That means whatever he is interested in here must be related to why he died. Jesus died, and the next thing he does is give people to perfect us as saints, for the work of the ministry and the edifying of the body of Christ.

Why did Jesus die? Often the answer is, "He died because of our sins." He did die because of our sins, but that is an incomplete answer. Why, having died because of our sins, did he then say the saints should be perfected? Jesus died to fulfill, to get done, God's purpose.

## Redemption and God's Eternal Purpose

Ephesians 3:10-11 says that this is "according to the eternal purpose which he accomplished in Christ Jesus our Lord." Man rejected God's purpose, and that rejection is sin and should lead to damnation. Christ had to die to bring us back to God's purpose. Pay very close attention: the perfecting of the saints is for the fulfillment of God's purpose.

Notice the phrase, "the eternal purpose accomplished in Christ." There is an eternal purpose God has, a purpose he has always had. He has not always had sin, so his purpose exists independently of sin. Before sin became a reality through man's rejection of God's purpose, it was only a conceptual possibility. God's purpose was already there.

What was accomplished in Christ and brought forth the reality of the church is God's eternal purpose, the purpose he always had. Redemption fulfilled God's purpose. In that light, the Lord says the apostles should perfect the saints. The perfecting of the saints is for the saints to fulfill God's purpose. The perfecting of the saints is for the saints to fulfill God's purpose.

When saints are perfected relative to their spiritual purpose, that fulfills God's purpose. When they are perfected relative to their material life, corrected to put things in order, not walk in fleshly conventions, but be diligent and thorough, that also fulfills God's purpose.

I am not saying the Bible teaches technology itself. It may refer or allude to people running for a reward, but it is not intended to teach the technical skill of running. Is it going to teach you how to handle marketing, how to get billboards, do radio, or put in a call to action? No. But it teaches the character that will make you learn those things and be effective as a believer.

The scripture may use a runner as an illustration of how we pursue a reward, but that does not make it a manual on athletics. In the same way, it does not give the technical curriculum of every profession. What it gives is the believer's governing character: diligence instead of sloth, thoroughness instead of carelessness, order instead of confusion, honesty instead of manipulation, and the willingness to learn what the work requires.

When the saint is perfected in those things, the material life is brought under God's purpose. When the saint is perfected concerning the gifts of the Spirit, the exercise of those gifts is brought under God's purpose. When the saint is perfected concerning relationships, the choice, maintenance, and use of those relationships are brought under God's purpose. Nothing in the perfecting of the saints is an isolated self-improvement project. It all returns to the eternal purpose accomplished in Christ.

## Relationships Must Fulfill God's Purpose

When the Bible teaches us about our material life, it is so that we fulfill God's purpose. The same is true of the gifts of the Spirit. So when Ephesians says that, speaking the truth in love, we will grow up into Christ in all things, relationships are included in those "all things." One area in which God's word teaches and trains us is our relationships, so that we can fulfill God's purpose.

We are not teaching marital relationships in this session, but if the Bible teaches you about marital or any other relationships, it is so that you fulfill God's purpose in them. Nothing taught in scripture for the perfecting of the saints is outside that purpose. Fulfilling God's purpose and plan for our lives must be on our hearts. That is the reason for the teaching, and that is important to know.

Sometimes people get lost in different things. If you ask a believer why he wants a marital relationship, the honest answer, at one level, may be what God has wired into human beings: the desire for sexual and emotional companionship. That may be the inspiration for it, but what is the reason?

The reason for a marital relationship cannot stop at sexual and emotional companionship. It has to be to fulfill God's purpose. You are going to be in that relationship to advance God's purpose. Ephesians 4:11 shows that the church perfects the saints for God's purpose. When the church gives us the knowledge of the word, the knowledge of the Son of God, and the teaching of the unity of the faith about relationships, it perfects us in relationships so that we fulfill God's purpose.

That purpose must govern the choice itself. Everybody has the natural desire for companionship, so do not make the choice on that desire alone. Do not reduce the matter to finding a man or woman who looks attractive to you, as though the whole issue were a body or outward appearance. I am not saying appearance has no place at all. I am saying there is something more serious than that. The relationship is for God's purpose, and the person you choose is someone with whom that purpose must be served.

Sometimes believers put attention on the wrong things and then think they are spiritual merely because they have adopted a certain outward standard. A person can spend all day looking in the physical mirror and be occupied with himself, while neglecting the mirror of the word. James describes the one who looks into the perfect law of liberty, continues in it, and does not forget what he sees. That is the mirror that should hold our attention. We should look into the word, keep it in memory, continue to look into it, and walk in it.

So the question is not merely whether a relationship supplies an ordinary human desire. The question is whether I am being perfected as a saint in relationships so that I can fulfill God's purpose. That is why the church teaches the knowledge of the Son of God in this area. That is why this subject is more serious than the limited way people often approach it.

## The Bible Is Loud About Relationships

One thing we clearly and definitely cannot miss as we look at God's word is that the Bible is loud about relationships. If you look through scripture and ask which areas of life appear, character will show up: put off the old man, put on the new man, do not lie to one another. Material life will show up. Things of the Spirit will show up. Different areas of life will show up. But you cannot miss the fact that relationship is a huge part of human existence and of God's design in his purpose and plan. You cannot ignore it. You cannot. It is impossible.

Here is the interesting fact: how much you understand about relationships in Christ affects how much of God's purpose and plan can be accomplished through your life. Look at the wide reach of the gospel across different locations, and then look at the relationships needed across those places for it to be done.

The extent and reach of God's purpose through our lives can be limited because we do not engage in relationships the way he wants us to. It is not true that God will simply make every particular thing happen somehow, regardless of people. He can desire that something should happen, and it may not happen in that particular way because someone fails to do what he ought to do faithfully.

Look at the gospel's movement through the New Testament. Its reach across cities and regions did not happen through isolated individuals who had no obligations to one another. There were people who sent and people who went, people who received workers into their homes, people who carried messages, people who supplied material needs, people who labored together, and people who remained faithful when another person was absent. The wide reach required a wide network of rightly handled relationships.

That is why a failure in relationship can limit the reach of purpose through a particular life. If someone will not receive, join, send, support, forgive, communicate, or remain faithful as the word requires, something God intended through that partnership may not happen through those people. God can raise another person, but the person who refused the step has still lost his own participation.

God, being God and knowing that someone will fail, can find another person and another way. He will still get what he wants done, but that does not remove the first person's responsibility. Think of Saul. God wanted his kingdom established through him, but Saul did not continue faithfully. First he was told that his kingdom would not continue beyond him; later he was told that God had taken the kingdom away. God can expect certain things to happen in certain ways, and when people fail, he finds another way.

I also think about Jonathan and David. I do not think Jonathan should have died. He was a good man and very sincere, and in many ways he was aligned with David. But there came a point when he should have recognized that his father was in rebellion against Yahweh and should have left. The work and the kingdom had moved. There was a relational choice to make, and unfortunately he did not make it. He suffered, I think, to the degree of that choice.

That is life for you. God is not going to force anything on anybody. Everything has a lot to do with you. Do not make excuses and assume that what God planned in partnership with you must still be done through you. He can find someone else and another way to get it done. We must be careful and discerning about what the Lord says in his word concerning relationships in Christ. As I looked at this myself, I saw places where I was failing and things that must be decisively corrected and changed.

## Relationships as an Area of Perfecting

One core area of the perfecting of the saints is the perfecting of the saints as touching relationships. Again, this is not merely about marital relationship, though that is included. Let your mind think fully about relationships: relationships you are supposed to develop, relationships with disciples, classmates, and other people that can have far-reaching implications for God's purpose.

The moment you understand that all relationships are for God's purpose, it should affect your attitude around and about them. As we proceed from Ephesians 4, we see that relationships are a major part of the areas in which God perfects us as saints.

## The Relationships of the Believer

From Ephesians 5:22 all the way to 6:9, Paul is talking about relationships. Even before that, Ephesians 5:21 speaks of church members relating with one another, "submitting to one another in the fear of God." Then he zooms in on natural relationships: "Wives, submit to your own husbands"; husbands should love their wives. In chapter 6 he moves to children and parents: "Children, obey your parents" and, "Fathers, do not provoke your children."

Then he moves to the master-servant relationship, or more generally secular relationships. Bondservants should be obedient to their masters according to the flesh. Masters should do the same things toward them, giving up threatening, knowing that their own Master is in heaven and that there is no partiality with him. Paul shows us that relationships are a major area of life in which we are supposed to be perfected.

We already looked at the different kinds of relationship a believer has in the faith of Christ. There is relationship with God. There is marital or spousal relationship. There is parental or parent-children relationship. There is sibling relationship. There is relationship with the saints generally. There is local church relationship. There is relationship with leaders. There are secular relationships. There are acquaintances. And there are strangers.

The local church relationship is distinct from relationship with saints generally. There is a bond you have with all saints, and a closer bond with those of your local church. That is your local church. That is what God is saying and doing in your own church. Those are the people God is working with you among. Know your church.

That distinction matters. All saints belong to Christ, and there is a bond with all of them, but the local church is the actual company among whom you are planted and with whom you are worked on. Those are the people who hear the same instruction with you, carry the same local responsibility with you, and participate with you in what God is doing there. You should not treat that relationship as interchangeable with a general acquaintance with Christians elsewhere.

There is also relationship with leaders. That is not swallowed up by the category of saints generally or even by the category of local church members. Scripture gives definite instruction about how believers relate to those who lead them, just as it gives instruction about spouses, parents, children, siblings, and civil authorities. These are distinct relational positions, and the believer must know how to show up in each one.

In secular relationships you have both colleagues and leaders. You may be a citizen among fellow citizens, but there are civil leaders. You may be a student among other students, but there are teachers. You may be an employee with colleagues and an employer, or an employer with employees. A believer will find himself relating across this spectrum, and there is scriptural instruction about it.

There is a way to act as a believer in secular places. As a citizen, you have responsibilities. Carry them out. Today they include paying what is due and voting as part of civic duty. Romans 13 was written under a form of government in which those believers did not vote, yet Paul still said they must do their duty. You have a responsibility to participate where you find yourself, to do your part, make your contribution, reflect the outcome you prefer, and help ensure the order that should be. The believer must know how to show up in all these relationships.

The ten categories help us see the breadth of the walk. Relationship with God is not relationship with a spouse. Relationship with a spouse is not relationship with a parent or child. A sibling is not simply another saint. All saints are not members of your local church, and every local church member is not one of your leaders. A colleague, civil officer, teacher, employer, or employee stands in a secular relationship. An acquaintance is not yet a friend, and a stranger is not yet an acquaintance.

The distinctions do not exist so that we can become cold or legalistic. They show the responsibilities appropriate to each position. A wife submits to her own husband, not to any man she sees. Children obey their parents, not every adult as though the relationship were identical. Bondservants and masters each receive instruction fitting their own place. The believer needs truth precise enough to recognize the relationship and then take the steps appropriate to it.

## Friendliness Toward All and Friendship With Some

We identified the relationships in which a believer will be involved. The second thing we saw was our role in those relationships: friendliness toward all and friendship with some. Believers are supposed to be friendly toward all. They are supposed to be friends with some. Listen carefully to that distinction.

Being friendly toward all does not mean you have to be friends with all. The Bible distinguishes between being friendly and being a friend, even though the words overlap in ordinary usage. I can call almost anybody a friend when I simply mean someone with whom I am friendly, almost like a Facebook friend. During a Muslim holiday I can say, "my Muslim friends," and mean Muslim family members or other people with whom there is mutual friendliness.

But in a teaching on the practice of friendliness and friendship, you need to understand the terms more carefully. As a Christian, you must be friendly. You may say you do not want to be, but you have to be. You cannot be cold and withdrawn and excuse it as merely who you are. That is walking in the flesh. We are to be friendly toward all.

Friendliness is the disposition and conduct that keeps the believer open, warm, respectful, and ready to do good even where there is no commitment of close friendship. It is what you owe the stranger, the acquaintance, the person at work, the fellow citizen, and the saint you have only just met. It does not require pretending that every person has the same access to your life. It does require that the character of Christ be visible toward every person.

Friendship in the stronger sense involves agreement and commitment. Two people discover that they agree in values and purpose and come to a settled mutual bond. They know, "I have your back, and you have mine." That cannot exist with everybody at the same depth, and scripture does not demand that it should. So the Christian posture is not friendship with all, but friendliness toward all and purposeful friendship with some.

## The High Point of Relationship Is Friendship

We said that the high point of all relationship is friendship. Look at how God wants to be with us. God wants to be our friend. In God's word, the high point of parental relationship is that parents and children become friends. The high point of leadership and followership is friendship. The Lord reached that point with his disciples at the final supper: "No longer do I call you servants; I call you friends."

So our role is friendliness toward all and, with some, committed friendship. In the stronger sense, friendship is arrived at by agreement and commitment. It may happen subtly, but the parties come to know: we agree on these things, and therefore we commit ourselves to one another in this way. I have your back; you have my back. We have developed a bond. It is not just familiarity. We know we agree, and we are committed.

Agree on what? Agree on values. Agree on purpose. Students may become friends because they want to do well in class. They discover the commitment they share, know what they agree on, and become friends around it. But the believer's friends in life must agree around what life itself is about. The people with whom we develop friendship should share that purpose.

The Bible is quite big on friendship. The word "friend" is notably used. God calls Abraham his friend, his friend forever. What does God want to be to those he created? He wanted a friend, a family friend. He wants us to be his family, but not merely a family held together by structure.

In some cultures marriage was approached mainly as a structural matter. Families arranged it, the structure held the people together, and personal friendship was not treated as the point. Today many societies have moved from that kind of structure to individual decision and choice. That can make things more difficult when people make the choice only around personal desire and never develop friendship with one another.

Then people can live in the same family while each person minds his own business and reserves friendship for people outside the home. That is not the high point in God's word. The high point of spousal relationship is friendship. The high point of parent-child relationship is friendship. The high point of leadership and followership is friendship. Structure has its place, but the structure is not the destination. The destination is a purposeful bond in which the people are genuinely friends.

Some people are not friends with their fathers, and some parents are not friends with their children; they are merely doing the structural duties of family. Is that God's plan? No. God wants a family of people of his kind with whom he is friends. From God's own example, the high point of relationship is friendship.

For some relationships, such as with strangers, the expression is friendliness. In many secular relationships it is friendliness; we may loosely call them friends, as in "my friend at work." With saints generally there is friendliness and a bond, but among local church members there should be people with whom you form real friendship. You should also be friends with your leaders. The high point of relationship is friendship.

## Knowing the Words Is Not the Walk

Now we come to an important issue. We saw what the relationships are and the reality of each one. We saw how they develop and how to engage in them. We also saw our role, friendliness and friendship. But I noticed gaps after that teaching because knowing the details and knowing the words does not resolve the heart.

Knowing what the relationships are and knowing our role does not mean we are doing it. People can mouth the words, "Relationships in Christ are friendliness and friendship," without practicing them. So the practice of friendliness and friendship must be the issue, because every Christian teaching is for its practice.

The aim, goal, essence, and end point of Christian teaching is practice, what Paul calls a work or a walk. Knowledge itself matters; thank God for knowledge. But we want that knowledge unto the walk. Therefore the walk must also be taught.

## Christian Teaching Must Produce a Walk

Look immediately after Ephesians 4:11-16. Verse 17 says, "This I say, therefore." Whenever you see "therefore," find out what follows from what has just been said. What follows from the perfecting of the saints and growing up into Christ? Paul says, "That you should no longer walk as the rest of the Gentiles walk." What follows is a walk. A former way stops and ceases. You no longer take those steps.

The Gentiles walk in the futility of their mind, having their understanding darkened and being alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance in them and the blindness of their hearts. Notice that their walk is from their mind. Because they reject the truth, they do not have the knowledge that should produce the right walk, and they give themselves over to uncleanness. Do not walk like them.

Then Ephesians 5:2 says, "Walk in love." In Ephesians you cannot miss the emphasis on walk. The aim and goal of our nature and of our understanding of God's word is that we walk. So the issue becomes: do we have the walk of what we have been taught? You can speak correctly about character and Christ, but where is the walk? There must be a walk.

Ephesians 2:2 says that in the past we walked according to the course of this world. The reality of the former nature was found in the former walk. When we now see you doing what you do, we must ask: is this the walk of the Spirit? "Walk in the Spirit," "walk in the light," and "walk in Christ" mean that you take the steps belonging to that nature.

The way you spoke, the way you looked at someone, and the way you responded are steps. Someone may be gloomy in a fleshly way, with a fallen countenance like Cain, moving toward malice. That is not the step of the Spirit. Where did you get that step from? It is a wrong step. Another person is cheerful, smiling, bright, and warm toward people. That is a step of the Spirit.

Ephesians 2:10 says we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them. Your nature is for you to take steps. There are good works prepared as a path, and you should be taking the steps of those good works.

Notice the movement through Ephesians. In 2:2 there is the old walk, according to the course of this world and the prince of the power of the air. In 2:10 there is the new walk, in the good works God prepared beforehand. In 4:1 there is the worthy walk, corresponding to the calling. In 4:17 there is the command not to walk as the Gentiles. In 5:2 there is the walk in love. In 5:8 there is the walk as children of light. In 5:15 there is the careful, exact walk of the wise.

Paul is not changing subjects each time. He is showing that nature and knowledge issue in steps. The old nature had steps. The calling has steps. Love has steps. Light has steps. Wisdom has steps. Therefore relationship in Christ also has steps. It is not enough to say, "I have love in my nature." The question is what love does at the next point in the relationship.

Suppose someone speaks to you harshly. There is a step the flesh wants to take and a step the Spirit takes. Suppose a brother is weak in conscience. There is a step mere knowledge might take in pride and a step love takes for the brother's sake. Suppose a stranger enters your space. There is a step fear or self-occupation takes and a step hospitality takes. The walk is found in those actual movements.

This is what the teaching must eventually show in detail. What does a believer do toward a stranger? What does a believer do when that stranger becomes an acquaintance? What steps develop friendliness into friendship among saints? How should a local church member act toward the people God has joined him to? How does a believer relate to leaders, and how does a leader relate to those he serves? There are answers in the pattern of Christ and in the faithful examples scripture gives.

The exact expression may carry the color of personality, but the moral step remains definite. One person may greet with more words and another with fewer; both can communicate warmth. One friend may serve in one practical way and another in a different way; both can express the same faithfulness and love. The variety belongs to personality and circumstance. The unchanging direction belongs to Christian character.

## Walk Means Taking Exact Steps

Ephesians 4:1 says to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called. There are steps that pertain to your calling. You were called out of the world and out of darkness into light, so take the steps of your calling. Ephesians 5:2 says to walk in love as Christ loved us. Notice that it is in a particular way. We are to walk as Christ loved us. There are exact ways to walk and exact steps to take. Follow a particular path.

The same thing applies when a believer wants to form a godly marital relationship. There are steps. You do not panic and say, "My marital clock is running out," and then abandon wisdom. You do not assume that desperation suspends the will of God. Wake up and get serious with God's way. You are asking another person to live with you, not for one year or two years, but through life. That is not a small matter, and it is not something to enter without knowing the steps.

Ephesians 5:15 says, "See then that you walk circumspectly," or exactly, "not as fools but as wise." Then it says, "Do not be unwise, but understand what the will of the Lord is." Taking wise steps is taking steps in the will of God. There must be exact steps. You do not merely know that marriage is good; you learn the path by which a believer forms that relationship in wisdom, love, purity, accountability, and the purpose of God.

That is the force of walk. It is not a vague claim that you belong to Christ. It is taking the next fitting step, and then the next one, along the path that belongs to your nature. In a matter as consequential as marriage, the steps must not be replaced by fear, pressure, impulse, or the sense that time is running out.

Ephesians 5:8 says, "You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Walk as children of light." That is nature and walk together. The fruit of the light is in all goodness, righteousness, and truth. Those are definite steps. There are definite steps in accountability and communication: what you say, what you disclose, what you intend to do, and how you carry another person along. Silence and evasion can be steps in the flesh; openness and truth can be steps of the Spirit.

Then Ephesians 5:15 says to walk exactly, not as unwise but as wise, and verse 17 says, "Do not be unwise, but understand what the will of the Lord is." Taking wise steps is taking steps in the will of God. There must be exact steps. You are not wandering through the Christian life. You are walking carefully as one who understands the Lord's will.

## Exact Steps Toward Strangers

What we want to do in this teaching is look at the exact steps in the practice of friendliness and friendship. What do I do when I meet a stranger? The Bible is particular about friendliness toward strangers. The word *philoxenia* is hospitality, friendliness toward strangers, and *philoxenos* describes someone hospitable, someone friendly toward strangers.

That stands opposite to xenophobia. The extreme expression is hatred and violence toward foreigners, but there are believers whose ordinary posture toward a stranger is also phobic and closed. That is abnormal for a Christian. A Christian is to be open toward the *xenoi*, foreigners and strangers. We saw that posture in Abraham.

Abraham gives us the picture. He saw people who were strangers to him and moved toward them with hospitality. He did not say, "I have just met someone new, so I do not know what to do." A believer should know what to do. Scripture has already shown the posture: receive the stranger, show warmth, make room, and act with hospitality. That is a definite Christian step.

This does not mean every personality must express warmth in exactly the same style or volume. One person may naturally be quieter than another. But quietness is not the same as being phobic toward people, obnoxious, cold, or habitually closed. You can prefer quiet and still be kind, open, and friendly. Personality may affect the mode of expression; it does not cancel the character commanded by God.

When you meet a stranger and withdraw in hostility, you took a wrong step. Do not rename bad character as personality. Character for the believer is supposed to be the same even where personality differs. There is a way the Spirit steps toward a stranger. There is a way love steps toward a stranger. There is a way hospitality steps toward a stranger. Those are the steps we are learning.

Do not call a consistently obnoxious posture mere personality. That is bad character; change the bad character. Personality may differ on many other things, but Christian character must be the same. If you met a stranger and treated the person wrongly, you took the wrong step. This is Christianity we are teaching. There is a way you should be toward a stranger.

So there are exact steps. When you say, "I am walking in the Spirit," "I am walking in the light," "I am walking in Christ," or "I am walking in love," you are saying that you are taking steps. They are definite steps, not a vague inward claim.

## A Definite Path Through Every Situation

The word translated "walk" is *peripateo*. The teaching traces the idea of *peri* as around, through, or across an area, and *pateo* as treading a path. The point is not merely the abstract act of moving one's feet. It is traversing a definite path, going from one point to another through a particular area. The path makes the steps definite.

You should know what to do from here to there in a situation. If you repeatedly say, "I did not know what to do," you are not yet letting the word teach you the path. Even preaching the gospel has a walk in the Spirit. When people reject you, what do you do? You should already know that rejection is part of the journey and that you are still to express love toward them. There is a step to take at that point.

To walk, then, is to take definite steps through a particular path. I am not merely saying that a person is moving. I am saying he is moving across this place, concerning this matter, and through this situation. He knows the way to go from one point to another. That is the force the teaching draws from the word: travel, tread, go through a path.

The path also implies continuity. One correct response in isolation is not yet the whole walk. You go through the matter from one side to the other. You know how to begin, how to continue when the response is difficult, how to correct a wrong turn, and how to arrive at the fitting end. The word maps the journey so that you are not reinventing Christian conduct at every new pressure point.

In preaching the gospel, for example, the path includes speaking the truth, meeting rejection without hatred, continuing to desire the person's salvation, and knowing when to move on without malice. In friendship it includes approach, conversation, demonstrated faithfulness, agreement in purpose, commitment, correction, forgiveness, and continued service. Walking means taking the fitting steps through that whole area.

It is like chess. I had reached a rating of eight hundred and then started losing until I was back in the seven hundreds. God willing, the way I am going, I will reach one thousand. I do not know who introduced me to it, but I have committed myself. It can be an intellectual addiction; once you enter, it is difficult to come out.

That is one reason chess became an illustration to me. The board is limited, but within those limits the positions are dynamic. You have to navigate the changing position by taking a definite move. You cannot move a piece anywhere you like and still call it chess. Each piece has a nature, and that nature determines the moves available to it. Then every actual situation requires discernment about the fitting move.

Life is like that in this respect. It is limited by truth and by the nature we have received in Christ, but it is dynamic in the situations that arise. The ability to navigate the dynamism of a game can illustrate the need to navigate the complexity of life. You do not invent another nature when the position becomes difficult. You know the nature, see the path, and take the fitting step.

So when I am walking, I am taking definite steps through a particular path. It means doing exact things. The personal mode of expression may differ because personality differs, but what is done will be definite. This is what believers do when they meet strangers. This is what believers do relative to acquaintances. This is what believers do toward local church members. The character and direction are not vague.

## Walking Is Imitation

Philippians 3:17 says, "Brethren, join in following my example, and note those who so walk, as you have us for a pattern." The word *tupos* means a model or pattern. There is a particular pattern to follow. Walk the way I walk. You have the map. You can picture putting your feet where the soles of the person ahead of you have been. You are taking the same steps.

That is walking in the Spirit. What would the Spirit do? What step does light take? What step does Christ take in this situation? You take that step. The Bible is loud about walking because the walk is the end point of the knowledge and teaching of God's word.

Ephesians 5:2 says, "Walk in love, as Christ also has loved us and given himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma." Christ's love took definite steps. But the command to walk as Christ loved us follows verse 1: "Therefore be imitators of God." Imitation means doing as the person does.

How did Christ walk when he was here? Walking, in the sense we are discussing, is imitating and replicating exact steps. You are not morally innovating. You are reproducing the pattern. The word for imitator is related to the word from which we get "mimic." The believer is repeatedly instructed to imitate, to replicate what is faithful.

Paul can say, "Imitate me," because he immediately qualifies it: "as I also imitate Christ." The human example does not replace Christ. It makes Christ's pattern visible in an actual life. The believer can watch how a faithful person handles money, pressure, rejection, correction, work, prayer, church order, and relationships, and then follow the faith expressed in that conduct.

First Thessalonians joins the two levels: "You became followers of us and of the Lord." The Thessalonians received the word in much affliction with joy of the Holy Spirit, and then they themselves became examples to other believers. The one who imitates a faithful pattern can become a pattern. The steps pass from Christ to the apostle, from the apostle to the church, and from that church to others.

That is also why Hebrews says to imitate those who through faith and patience inherit the promises. You do not merely collect their conclusions. You consider the path by which they inherited: the believing step, the patient step, the repeated step when the promise was not yet visible. Hebrews 13 adds that we consider the outcome of the leaders' conduct and follow their faith. Scripture expects conduct to be observed closely enough to be reproduced.

First Corinthians 4:16 says, "Therefore I urge you, imitate me," and 1 Corinthians 11:1 says, "Imitate me, just as I also imitate Christ." The idea of replicating people is utterly scriptural. Paul imitates Christ, taking the steps Christ takes, and tells believers to take those same steps. Christianity is imitation: of Christ, and of people insofar as they imitate Christ. We are speaking of taking definite, exact steps after the nature we have received.

First Corinthians 4:16 says, "Therefore I urge you, imitate me." First Corinthians 11:1 says, "Imitate me, just as I also imitate Christ." The idea of replicating people is utterly scriptural. Paul is imitating Christ, taking the steps Christ takes, and he tells believers to take those same steps. Christianity is imitation: first of Christ, and then of those who faithfully imitate Christ.

That does not mean copying superficial personality. It means following the nature and conduct. How does Christ handle relationships? How does he think about people? What does he do when he is received, when he is rejected, when someone is weak, when someone sins, when someone needs correction, and when someone needs mercy? See how he does it, and then learn to practice that in your own relationships. "This is what Christ would do; these are the steps we take here."

How does Christ handle relationships? We shall see how he does it and then learn to practice that in our own relationships. What does he think? What does he say? What steps does he take? We should be able to say, "This is what Christ would do; these are the steps we take here." The Bible is loud and clear on imitation. Walking in the Spirit includes imitating the steps of the Spirit.

## Imitating Christ and Faithful Believers

First Thessalonians 1:6 says, "You became followers of us and of the Lord." That is Christianity: imitating the steps. It is related to asking what Jesus would do, but it is more than an isolated question. You know the nature you have and the steps that pertain to it. "This is who I am, so these are my steps."

In our relationships we are like God by nature, but what are our steps? If we know our nature without knowing our steps, we will be frustrated. We will say, "This is who I am; why am I not walking like that?" It is because we have not learned the exact steps or watched how faithful people went through the path to the other side.

First Thessalonians 2:14 says the believers became imitators of the churches of God in Christ Jesus. Churches can imitate churches. If one is an example, others follow. "This is how they do things, and this is how things should be done." If they give in a faithful way, imitate it. If they have godly order, imitate it. Do not fight the pattern merely because you want to be different.

Sometimes we struggle to be different when we should strive to be the same. In character we are to be the same even though personality will differ. That difference in personality is part of the beauty of life. People dress differently, prefer different things, and pick up different modes of expression from many influences. But within those expressions there must be the same unchanging character. We must distinguish personality from bad character, even when the line appears thin.

Hebrews 6:12 says not to become sluggish, but to imitate those who through faith and patience inherit the promises. Faith and patience have observable steps. God is definite about salvation by faith in Christ, and he is definite about the local church and the order in which believers walk. When it comes to the Christian walk, there are things that are definite. The nature is definite, and the steps are definite.

Hebrews 6:12 says that we should not become sluggish, but imitate those who through faith and patience inherit the promises. Notice the instruction again: imitate. Look at people who have walked through the path by faith and patience and take the steps they took. God is definite about salvation, definite about faith in Christ, and definite about the local church. In the same way, when it comes to the walk, the nature is definite and the steps are definite.

First Peter 3:13 asks, "Who is he who will harm you if you become followers of what is good?" The word carries the thought of becoming imitators of what is good. Third John 11 says, "Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good." The believer is not merely told to admire what is good. He is told to reproduce it.

Second Thessalonians 3:7 says, "For you yourselves know how you ought to follow us, for we were not disorderly among you." Verse 9 says that they made themselves an example of how the believers should follow them. Paul could point to the order of his own life and say, "This is how you walk." The instruction is definite because the example is visible.

When it comes to handling material things, do not confuse yourself by making a worldly figure your moral pattern. Find the Christian who is following the Lord in that area and imitate him. Paul had a spiritual life and a material life. His whole conduct could be examined. The question is not who has the most visible success, but whose steps accord with Christ.

This is how we do to strangers. This is how we do to local church members. This is how we develop friendship amongst them. This is how it leads to, you know, from friendliness with them to friendship with them. These guys can live for friendship with them to maybe spousal relationship with them. These are the steps. These are the steps. Okay.

The point in all these texts is the same. The walk of the Spirit has both a nature aspect and a step aspect, and both are exact. There is an exact nature, and there are exact steps. This is the step here; this is the step there. The example makes the teaching visible.

## Know Whose Steps to Follow

Hebrews 13:7 is very key: "Remember those who rule over you, who have spoken the word of God to you, whose faith follow, considering the outcome of their conduct." Know whose steps to follow. Consider their conduct, their lifestyle, their behavior, how they navigate things and approach relationships. Follow the faith expressed in that order of life.

Third John 11 says, "Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good." In context, Diotrephes loves to have the preeminence, the love of being first. That desire for exclusivity, to be the first and the best, is something to beware of. Do not imitate it.

It saves a person much stress to understand that faithful Christian character is not supposed to be a private specialty. Sometimes people try to make a faithful practice seem like evidence that one person is uniquely special. But the right thought is, "I am trying to be what everybody should be." I am not trying to establish a private distinction. This is how Christianity should be.

There are steps. There are steps. There are exact steps. *Peripateo* is to take those exact steps after your nature, and scripture is loud about it. The Christian faith is about taking exact steps.

## The Scriptural Call to Walk

The word can refer to ordinary physical walking, a person walking here or through a place, but the New Testament repeatedly uses it for the believer's spiritual conduct. John speaks of walking in the light and not stumbling. Romans speaks of walking in newness of life, walking according to the Spirit rather than the flesh, and walking properly as in the day. Romans 14 then applies the same word to a particular relational conduct: if your brother is grieved because of your food, you are no longer walking in love.

The word is used repeatedly across scripture. John 8:12 says that whoever follows Christ shall not walk in darkness but have the light of life. John 11:9-10 speaks of walking in the day and not stumbling because one sees the light. John 12:35 warns that the one who walks in darkness does not know where he is going. These are not disconnected metaphors. They show a path determined by light.

Romans 6:4 says that, as Christ was raised from the dead, we also should walk in newness of life. Romans 8:1 and 8:4 speak of those who do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit. The word walk can identify the nature according to which a person takes his steps. Romans 13:13 says, "Let us walk properly, as in the day." Again there is a proper, definite way to walk.

Romans 14:15 says that if your brother is grieved because of your food, you are no longer walking in love. Do not say, "I only ate food; what does that have to do with steps?" You took a wrong relational step. For some believers, eating a certain food was still associated in conscience with the worship of an idol. You may be right that the food itself is clean, yet the way you act toward your brother can still be wrong. The question is not only whether you possess correct knowledge, but whether you walk in love with that knowledge.

Questions about outward practices can expose the same issue. Someone asks, "Is anything wrong with a tattoo?" At the level of the physical mark alone, you may answer that the mark itself is not the whole issue. But something can be wrong with the person, the motive, and the cultural meaning of the action. In a context where the action represents rebellion and identification with a fleshly identity, you cannot ignore that representation and say, "That is not what I mean."

How will the people around you know that is not what you mean? Why are you making that drastic change, and what identity are you trying to display? More often than not, the inspiration is not merely fashion. It is the desire to identify with an identity, and that desire may itself be fleshly. The issue becomes the step you are taking and what it communicates to people. You may no longer be walking in love. You may be taking steps in the flesh.

The chain continues. Second Corinthians says not to walk in craftiness and says that we walk by faith, not by sight. It distinguishes living in the flesh from warring according to the flesh. Galatians commands us to walk in the Spirit. Ephesians speaks repeatedly of the former walk, the walk in good works, the worthy walk, the walk in love, the walk in light, and the careful walk. Philippians contrasts faithful patterns with those who walk as enemies of the cross. Colossians calls us to walk worthy of the Lord.

Second Corinthians 4:2 says that we do not walk in craftiness. There are maneuvers and manipulations by which a person may try to obtain a result, including in money matters, that are not steps of the Spirit. Second Corinthians 5:7 says, "We walk by faith, not by sight." Second Corinthians 10 contrasts walking in the flesh, in the ordinary fact of human life, with warring according to the flesh. Galatians 5:16 says, "Walk in the Spirit, and you shall not fulfill the lust of the flesh."

Ephesians repeats the word: we once walked according to the course of this world; we are created in Christ Jesus for good works that we should walk in them; we should no longer walk as the Gentiles walk; we should walk in love; walk as children of light; and walk carefully, as wise. Philippians 3:17-18 distinguishes those who follow the apostolic pattern from those who walk as enemies of the cross. Colossians 1:10 calls us to walk worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing him.

Colossians says to walk in Christ and to walk in wisdom toward outsiders. First Thessalonians speaks of how believers ought to walk and please God and how they should walk toward those outside. Second Thessalonians contrasts order with walking disorderly. First John contrasts walking in darkness with walking in light and says that whoever abides in Christ ought to walk as he walked. Second John speaks of walking according to his commandments, and Third John speaks of walking in truth. Across scripture there is a loud call for the believer to walk.

Colossians 2:6 says, "As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in him." Colossians 3:7 refers to the things in which they once walked when they lived in them. Colossians 4:5 says, "Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside." First Thessalonians 4:1 urges believers to abound more and more in how they ought to walk and please God; verse 12 says to walk properly toward those who are outside. Second Thessalonians 3 speaks of those who walk disorderly and of the apostolic order they should imitate.

First John 1:6-7 contrasts walking in darkness with walking in the light. First John 2:6 says that the one who says he abides in Christ ought himself also to walk just as Christ walked. Verse 11 speaks of the one who walks in darkness. Second John 4 rejoices to find children walking in truth, and verse 6 says, "This is love, that we walk according to his commandments." Then Third John again speaks of walking in truth.

Across scripture there is a loud call for the believer to walk. Therefore there is a loud call to take exact steps. The scripture does not call us to walk and then leave the path unknowable. It shows the exact steps for different areas of life: steps in light, steps in love, steps in wisdom, steps by faith, steps toward those outside, steps in order, and steps in truth. Relationships are one of those areas.

So the believer does not approach relationships by saying, "I will do whatever feels natural to me." He asks what the truth says, what Christ's pattern shows, and what faithful believers have demonstrated. He knows there are steps when meeting a stranger, steps when cultivating an acquaintance, steps when forming friendship, steps toward the saints and the local church, and steps toward leaders. The path is not vague simply because the situations are dynamic.

We have a nature in Christ, and that nature has a walk. We have light, and light has steps. We have love, and love has steps. We have the Spirit, and the Spirit has steps. Therefore we can learn to move from one point to another in a relationship without leaving our nature behind. That is the burden of the teaching: not merely to define friendliness and friendship, but to practice them by taking exact steps.

Would scripture call us to walk and then refuse to show the steps? No. The loud call to walk is a loud call to learn and take exact steps.

## Walking in the Truth

Third John 3-4 says, "I rejoiced greatly when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth. I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth." They testified not only that the truth was in them, but that they walked in it.

The truth in you includes the nature you have and the knowledge of its steps. The Spirit of God has steps. You know your nature, learn the steps of that nature, and therefore walk in it. That is what it means for the truth in you to become the truth in which you walk.

We have exact steps to take in our lives, brethren, and exact steps to take in relationships. We shall begin to see what these exact steps are as we look at what our relationships ought to be: the steps toward strangers, acquaintances, saints, local church members, leaders, and friends, and the steps by which a purposeful bond develops.

Say this: I take exact steps after my nature in Christ in all my relationships, and by so doing I fulfill God's purpose and plan for my life.
