# Understanding the Book of Revelation

## Revelation Is a Single Revelation

We're looking at understanding the book of Revelation. Perhaps the first thing that needs to be mentioned, as I like to do when we're looking at this, is that it's not Revelations. If we read the material, it didn't seem like John took a break throughout. It's one of the quickest materials you can read, aside from the epistles. It's not as smooth a sail to read Matthew in one go, maybe Mark, but Revelation is a book you can read in about an hour. I've forgotten the calculation I used to make, and it depends on your speed. You can read it well under an hour, but some might argue that it's about reading speed. Revelation is not that long.

He saw it as a vision, so you can read and imagine it, but I still believe the vision was a bit longer. It wasn't different revelations he had. He didn't take a break and go into another one. It was a vision, and there were visions in the vision. In that single revelation there were several visions: he finished one scene, entered another, was taken by an angel into another, and on like that. It's the book of Revelation, a single revelation. That's the first thing we want to establish.

## Eschatological Truth Shapes the Believer's Disposition

The second thing we need to do as we proceed is remember that if we're going to fulfill God's purpose, we need the knowledge of God's word. The knowledge we need has an entire scope of doctrine. One thing we identified that Paul talks about in the doctrine of our faith, Ephesians chapter 1, is the last days. We looked at that in the last teaching in level three, “Concerning the Last Days.”

Eschatology is part of the doctrine of the church, and it has an implication for our attitude, disposition, outlook, and worldview. If it is not present, we are going to lack that attitude. Some instructions the Lord gave depend on your having eschatological truth on your mind. When he tells you, “Be faithful, for the master is coming at an hour you do not know,” if you don't have eschatological truth on your mind, you won't have that disposition.

Think about the history of the church, even in a nation like Nigeria. Churches that tend to be more eschatological somehow have a particular godly disposition in their membership. We might say it's about teaching that character. On one hand that might be true. On the other hand, when you don't have eschatological truth, you will lack certain dispositions.

There's a kind of anticipation we saw the apostles have that we are always supposed to have. Every generation of Christians must in their time have the disposition that it is close, it is near, it is about to happen. They are working towards that in carrying out the mission of the church, their spiritual assignments, and in their attitude. He who has this hope will purify himself. There will be certain things present with you when you have the right eschatological belief as a Christian.

In the same breath, wrong eschatological beliefs can be very costly. People can sometimes be trapped. There was a known case back then where some people stopped going to class and went to a mountain because they were expecting the Lord. They had been given a particular revelation that he was coming so soon. This is not new; it's almost like the Montanist movement. Should we even talk about the one that was worse, that Jones man where they drank Kool-Aid? Wrong eschatological beliefs can be costly because of the force eschatological truth has. It can be compelling, and if you believe the wrong things, it can be very expensive. We need to know the right things.

## Sealed for the Day of Redemption

Let's establish eschatology as an important part of Christian doctrine, then get back to Revelation. Ephesians 1:13: “In him,” Christ, “you trusted after you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation; in whom, having believed, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise.” That word “sealed” was with the intention that something is to be unlocked later. You were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise, who is the earnest of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession.

He gives us a timeline: the earnest of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession. There's a timeline between when people begin to have the Spirit of God in them regeneratively and that redemption. Remember I made the case for the Spirit of God being with people in a non-regenerative sense before the work of redemption was accomplished. That's something we need to examine in level four.

Today, when you have redemptive faith, the Spirit of God is in you regeneratively. Before Christ, people of redemptive faith weren't regenerated as we are. They were still in a state where they were dead and needed to be made alive. There was a temporary, interim provision of God for them until their redemptive faith was fulfilled in Christ's work.

Now, when you have regenerative faith, there is still a distance. When you have the Spirit of sonship, you are still waiting for sonship, the redemption of your body, but you've already received the Spirit of sonship in you. Ephesians 4:30 speaks of this timeline: “Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption.” Again, there's the Spirit of God being in you regeneratively and what is going to be done. You were sealed for that day.

It's a powerful text affirming what you might call the preservative role of the Spirit in a man. The Holy Spirit was given to you towards that. You were sealed for the day of redemption.

Romans 8:15 first says, “You did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but the Spirit of adoption.” That's something that has happened, not something we're expecting. We have received the Spirit of adoption whereby we cry, “Abba, Father.” But in verse 23, we who have the firstfruits of the Spirit groan within ourselves, eagerly waiting for adoption. What Spirit do we have? The Spirit of sonship or adoption. Yet we are waiting for adoption or sonship.

That means there's something about our inheritance not yet fully seen. We're eagerly waiting for what belongs to us, the inheritance that is ours. Something of it we haven't received, yet we've received the Spirit of sonship, the Spirit of those who are to receive an inheritance. Immortality, incorruption, new heavens and a new earth: there's something of our inheritance we haven't yet gotten.

We groan within ourselves, eagerly waiting for the adoption, the redemption of our body. When he says you are sealed until the day of redemption, you can see the redemption Paul intends is bodily redemption, the glorification of our body.

## The Last Days in the Redemptive Pattern

This timeline is what is referred to as the last days. When we looked at “Concerning the Last Days,” we saw several mentions in the epistles and four Gospels, the New Testament texts, but none clearly explained the last days. From some we could make a guess or inference. First Corinthians 15 speaks of the last Adam. The last Adam is Jesus, so perhaps that points to the beginning. Other texts say he was manifested in these last days for you. It seems Jesus' presence has something to do with the last days, but those texts don't clearly explain it.

The text giving us the way to answer definitively is Acts chapter 2. Peter stood up and said this was what the prophet Joel prophesied: “I will pour out my Spirit in the last days, says God.” When we read Joel, he didn't use the words “last days.” He used “afterwards.” Peter was the one who used “last days.”

We practically read the whole book of Joel in that teaching. The “afterwards” Joel meant was after people sinned, became condemned, had judgment meted out against them, then repented and were restored or redeemed. After that period of sin, condemnation, judgment, repentance, and restoration, he says, “Afterwards I will pour out my Spirit on them.” It's post-restoration, post-redemption.

Peter takes up the same thought: through what Christ has done, upon the redemption he has accomplished, God pours out his Spirit. By putting the texts together, we can explain the last days as the days where the Christ is present, or when his redemption is fulfilled and his Spirit given. You can choose to count the last days from redemption being fulfilled or the Redeemer being present. We saw this redemptive pattern of eschatology play out repeatedly in Scripture.

We examined several patterns, even down to the Olivet Discourse, which is for the church. But now there's Revelation, a book on its own. I don't want to get into arguments about which John wrote it and a stronger consensus leaning away from John the apostle. I'll put that on your mind, but it's better for level five, where we can insert the different arguments. For now, I want to address the very substance of the book.

## The Father Gives the Revelation to the Son

Revelation is undoubtedly the most eschatologically focused material. Once you understand it, you might argue whether it should be thought of only eschatologically, but it is strongly focused that way, almost entirely. Its title is Revelation because John had a revelation, apokalypsis, and we're told of that revelation. “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants things which must shortly take place.” Jesus gives a revelation to John.

You can find the eschatological pattern in almost every book of Scripture, arguably. Read the Minor Prophets and you see it a lot. Read Genesis and you see it. I gave you about seven patterns running across Scripture. You can read your own life in your generation in God's eschatological pattern. But this book is heavily focused on that, and heavily contentious. We'll see why.

Revelation 1:1 says, “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants things which must shortly take place. And he sent and signified it by his angel to his servant John.” You see almost three levels of the message. There's a message intended to be delivered, the revelation of Jesus Christ, and God gave it to him to show his servants. They're deliberately making a distinction here between the Father and the Son. We're told the Father gave the revelation to the Son.

Don't forget that in the incarnation of the Lord Jesus Christ, his function excludes him from certain divine privileges. Particularly eschatologically, he told us no one knows that day or time, not even the Son. It's not strange to frame it this way in light of his incarnation and role. He's not walking in certain divine privileges. Philippians 2 speaks of an emptying of himself, a kenosis.

Matthew 24:36 says, “Of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, but my Father only.” Mark 13:32 makes it even clearer: “Of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.” This framing at the beginning is important because we're speaking of divinely granted privileges, things God wants us to know.

Revelation 1 says it's the revelation of Jesus Christ. Who is giving it? The Lord Jesus, with things he has by divine privileges, not only because he's God, but now even in his humanity where he has decided to lay aside some privileges. He's now to make those things known.

## What Is the Message, and How Is It Communicated?

After that general introduction, the first question is what the book is about, what message it's communicating. The second is how it was communicated. There is clearly an intended goal. The Father gave the Son something to communicate to his servants, plural, before we're told it's communicated to one servant, singular. The content is intended for believers.

He's using “servants” as Luke 12 does, speaking of faithful servants and those who aren't faithful, judged accordingly when the master comes. There are things he wants us to know. God gave him things shortly to take place so several people could know them. The Son, having received the message from the Father, sent and signified it by his angel to his servant John.

This singular and plural is part of the original, not the creation of translators or a decision of textual critics. As for textual critics, I doubt there is a manuscript exception in this situation. It isn't a major decision to debate. When they use “servants,” they aren't taking initiative to decide it. God wants his servants to know, so believers ought to know.

We could argue he wanted believers at that time to know, but things here are useful beyond that time. By the ending, you can see the intention is for as long as this age continues. It's a book for us. The message was communicated to one person, but for the servants.

He could have said he sent the message to his servant, but he said he sent and signified it. That's a key, deliberate word. From the beginning he tells you the manner in which the message is communicated. But let's return to what the message is. Verse 1 says things which must shortly take place.

We can argue that the material concerns immediate events, and truly, as we get into it, we can see immediate events. But in terms of eschatological patterns, we can argue it concerns imminent events. “Shortly take place” can be read as immediate, rightly so, or imminent: it can happen, it will happen soon, and it can happen at any time. Everything necessary for it is ready. It contains immediate and imminent events.

John bore witness of the word of God, the testimony of Jesus Christ, and all he saw. Blessed is he who reads and those who hear the words of this prophecy and keep what is written, for the time is near. There is the issue of imminence. John then addresses the seven churches. Another place telling us what the material is about is Revelation 1:17–19, where the Lord himself speaks before his angel signifies the message.

## The Message Includes Past, Present, and Future

“When I saw him, I fell at his feet as dead. He laid his right hand on me, saying, ‘Do not be afraid. I am the first and the last.’” The prōtos and eschatos. “I am he who lives, and was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore.” He is the one who lives, yet came as human to die. Having accomplished redemption, in this new sense as human, he is alive forevermore. He has brought immortality to light. “I have the keys of death and Hades.”

Verse 19 says, “Write the things which you have seen, the things which are, and the things which will take place after this.” You may argue it's the short experience in that vision: what he's just seen, what he's presently seeing, and what will follow. That would also be right. But you will find the material contains details of the past, present, and future. Put that in mind.

What is the message? Things that will shortly take place, given by mirroring or pointing to the past, showing the present and future as well. He's sent to talk about immediate or imminent events God wants them to know. But the message also has the context of those he's writing to. These are Christians under Roman persecution, concerned by it, even though different places had different experiences.

In chapters 2 and 3, when he speaks to the churches, there are all kinds of experiences. Some places had looseness, others oppression and persecution. It's an important detail that this was written at a time when they were under Roman persecution, to people under it. There was one empire, the Roman Empire, like a one-world order. That detail is necessary for the message as we proceed.

This is one teaching in level three. I'll compress many things and won't have time to be patient with every explanation. I'll mention some things without explaining them because there isn't time here. We will wait for level five for some explanations.

The message concerns things shortly to happen, but it will show past events. For example, it will speak of the death and birth of the Lord Jesus, things that have happened, things happening, and things that will happen. The major goal is things shortly to take place, verse 1, but it does so by referencing things already past.

It's not everything you read that is something that will happen. It would be strange to read Revelation 12 as someone who can think and imagine some of it hasn't already happened. The tone says it has happened, not that it happens when John sees it or will happen later. This is very important in understanding the book's message.

## Sēmainō: Communicating Through Signs

How is that message presented? Revelation 1:1 says, “He sent and signified it by his angel to his servant John.” There's a message the Father gave the Son, and the Son sends that message. In sending it, he signifies it through the ministry of angels.

“Signify” is sēmainō. Whatever way I pronounce it, it isn't my language; we're using it for hermeneutical purposes. It occurs six times in six verses, once in Revelation, at 1:1. The noun is sēmeion, signs, as in signs and wonders. It's to show something that indicates another thing. Let's look at some instances to help us.

It's used three times in the book of John. If you assume this is the same John, that makes even more sense to examine. John 12:32 says, “But I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all to myself.” Verse 33 says, “This he said signifying what death he would die.” Interestingly, he didn't mention death there. He said “lifted up,” but what he's saying concerns his death.

He's using “lifted up” to signify what death he would die. Probably it points to crucifixion as being lifted up, or that he will die and be raised, being lifted up. It shows what manner of death, with resurrection following. Whichever you're saying, crucifixion or a resurrection, he didn't mention death. He used something that points to it.

When you use “signify,” there's the reality and the sign. There's an intended goal and a sign pointing to it. The reality you want to talk about is death, and then there's the way you express it. John 18:31–32 uses the same word. Pilate tells them to take him and judge him according to their law. The Jews say it isn't lawful for them to put anyone to death, “that the saying of Jesus might be fulfilled which he spoke, signifying what death he would die.”

John 21:18 says, “When you were younger, you girded yourself and walked wherever you wished; but when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish.” Verse 19 says he spoke this signifying by what death Peter would glorify God.

Did he tell him directly what would happen? No. There is the reality, a more direct expression, and another thing mentioned indirectly to signify it. Church history keeps the record that Peter was crucified upside down. It wasn't the way he wanted, not something of his own. Nobody goes and crucifies themselves upside down. It was persecution. He was pointing to the manner of his death.

When we see sēmainō, we see that Revelation will not express or show things exactly. It won't spell things out precisely as they are. It uses figurative descriptions. There will be a lot of figurative description in Revelation.

## A Woman, a Dragon, and the Reality They Indicate

Sēmeion, the noun, is used several times in Revelation, I think in seven verses. The word itself is used seventy-seven times in sixty-nine verses, but we'll focus on Revelation. This helps us see what the angel being sent to signify means.

Revelation 12:1 shows this practically. In many of the visions, someone was creating the scene for John. This is one role of the ministry of angels: a figurative description of something yet to happen or things that have happened, for communicating a message.

“A great sign appeared in heaven.” This is not the literal thing. They can later tell you the literal thing, but what is mentioned here is not it. “A woman clothed with the sun.” That's not literal. Do you know the sun and a woman? No human being can be clothed with the sun. The moon is under her feet, a garland of twelve stars on her head, and she is with child, crying out in labor and pain to give birth.

The person saw a sign, a pointer, not the reality. It's an indication of something. The sign is not the reality; it points to the reality. It isn't a clear expression of what the thing is but an indication. You need to find what the thing is from the indication used: this indication means it is likely talking about this thing.

It's figurative, where we have the idea of a figure of speech. If someone says, “That guy is a lion,” you know what they mean. They're not speaking expressly. You could write, “This he said signifying the boldness the guy had.” Calling him a lion is a sign.

Revelation 12:3 says another sign appeared, a great fiery red dragon. Was that really a dragon? The dragon described another entity. He eventually tells us in verse 9: the dragon was cast out, that serpent of old. He's using another sign. Initially, John saw a dragon.

In the same way, people say if you see God's throne, you'll see four beasts and wonder how creatures like that are around it. You won't necessarily see that. It depends on what the angels signify to you and how you receive. You can see awesome creatures, but it doesn't have to be one having one kind of head and another having another kind. That itself was a sign collapsing the seraphim of Isaiah and cherubim of Ezekiel into one.

This point about how the message was communicated is probably the most important. The material is not literal. From the beginning he's clear that he will communicate by signifying. The use of signs is repeated over and again.

Other places use sēmeion for the false prophet's signs and wonders, Revelation 13:13–14. I don't want to focus on those. Revelation 15:1 says, “I saw another sign in heaven, great and marvelous: seven angels having the seven last plagues.” Is it true they literally have seven last plagues in seven bowls? No. “I saw a sign.” It points to an unleashing of wrath.

Revelation 16:14 uses “signs” for demonic power, and 19:20 for the false prophet. But three texts clearly show this material's figurative manner: 12:1, 12:3, and 15:1. From sēmainō in 1:1 we already know it is written to signify. That's important because signifying begins in chapter 1, where he starts telling you of the seven spirits of God.

People then go to spirit of wisdom and such things. You could bring that thought into it, but he's talking about the Spirit of God. It's difficult to recall every single detail on this issue here. But such is the manner in which we have Revelation.

## The Roman Context of the Message

We've seen that eschatology is an important part of doctrine and Revelation is its most emphatic material. It was written during Christian persecution by the Romans under their empire. All the places addressed were under the Roman Empire, considered the known world. It was written particularly for those experiencing persecution or aware of their brothers and sisters experiencing it.

You must feel the burden of those experiencing persecution. Not doing so isn't supposed to be the case; we have to repent of that. We've seen the book's message and its context under Roman persecution. Rome didn't persecute Christians at the beginning. The first persecution was by Jewish authorities. Roman persecution was a later issue.

There was the Jewish revolt or rebellion against Roman authorities and their suppression around AD 66–70. Earlier, Paul was confident enough to appeal to Caesar. I'm thinking now that the persecution started before AD 70, maybe around 64. But this period was long drawn out and sustained. It changed at different times, but was centuries long, depending how you look at it, before they began to have relief or reprieve. Be aware that these people were under the Roman Empire and persecution.

The message is figurative from the start. Now we move to the outline of the book. I can't exhaust every explanation. I want to give an outline, talk about it as we go, and that will be it.

## The Introduction to the Book and the Letter

The first part of the outline is Revelation 1:1–20, introduction. The first introduction, verses 1–3, is the writer introducing the material and what led up to it. Blessed are those who read, hear, and keep the words of the prophecy, because the time is near. That's why I say the servants are those in this age. As long as “the time is near” remains applicable, as it does to us, it is a book relevant to us for eschatological truth.

The second introduction is to the letter itself, after that preamble. “John, to the seven churches which are in Asia.” Grace and peace from him who is, was, and is to come, from the seven spirits before his throne, and from Jesus Christ, the faithful witness, firstborn from the dead and ruler of the kings of the earth.

The signs have already started with the seven spirits of God. They will come out later in the book, and John starts to speak that way personally. Then, “To him who loved us and washed us in his blood, and made us kings and priests to his God and Father, to him be glory forevermore.” “Behold, he is coming with clouds, and every eye will see him, even those who pierced him, and all the tribes of the earth will mourn because of him.” There is an eschatological detail already.

## Several Patterns of Coming Do Not Replace the Final Coming

There's an argument about the coming of the Lord I heard recently, but I'll present it. It matters because of those with Adventist kinds of theology, like Jehovah's Witnesses, and others whose beliefs or leanings may come from this. There's an understanding that “the coming of the Lord” may not only mean the second coming at the end. It can describe God coming to judge, with several comings of the Lord in Scripture.

When the Lord came with two other angels to Abraham before Sodom and Gomorrah, you can call it the Lord coming in clouds with angels to judge. That's an eschatological pattern. I find nothing wrong with that theology. What is wrong is if you don't clearly teach the true, existential coming at the end.

In that sense, can we say there are times the Lord has come even in our time, temporary acts of judgment? Yes, probably, but be careful. Where would you want to say that? There are times when you feel some justice has not happened. Where you see some justice or consequence today, you can call it the coming of the Lord. Maybe God sends an army against another and destroys it, expressing good's victory over evil. Evil, death, is being killed. The death of death is life, showing what God seeks at the end.

There are injustices still happening, but such instances can speak of the eventual justice to come. Every judgment you may call a coming of the Lord before the actual coming is, in comparison, weak, not sufficient. What happened compared with what should happen justly is weak. What should happen is much more severe.

I suspect, from what I've seen and heard, that some people think of several comings in Revelation because they see several endings. I don't think that's the best reading. I suspect they read chronologically, see several endings, and explain them as interventions in judgment at different times. I don't think that's correct, and I'll make a strong case against it.

There is no doubt that we can read several comings of the Lord in Scripture. But don't mistake them for the one true coming. It's like reading several redemptive figures and thinking that is actual redemption. Don't mistake them for the one true redemptive work Christ did, or for the bodily redemption and glorification, indignation and destruction, at the end.

## Be Careful About Calling a Disaster a Specific Judgment

I think we must be careful when people experience all kinds of judgment. There's an outbreak somewhere, and someone points to a vice or immorality associated with that place and calls it God's judgment. There is a way Joel would handle an experience in his time using eschatological truth. We should allow the biblical letters their role in teaching biblical truth through their present experiences, but stick with saying the truth overall.

The Lord had that carefulness when something fell on those people in Luke. Were they the only ones who did evil? We can say something is unjust and will be judged, but be careful around specific claims. That approach can alienate people from God's love. They should know his justice too, but they'll point to other evils where there is no apparent justice, or a child having a situation. We must be careful with those examples.

Matthew 10:14 says, “Whoever does not receive you nor hear your words, when you depart from that house or city, shake off the dust from your feet.” Verse 15 says it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city. Do you know how bad Sodom and Gomorrah was? Will its people still experience judgment? Yes. Indignation and consumption at the end will be worse. Their judgment on that day of the Lord will be worse. It will be severe.

Matthew 11:20 says he began to rebuke the cities where most of his mighty works were done, because they didn't repent. “Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! If the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes.” It will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon in the day of judgment than for them. The judgment experienced isn't comparable to the severity at the end.

Capernaum, exalted to heaven, will be brought down to Hades. Had Sodom seen the mighty works done there, it would have remained. But it will be more tolerable for Sodom in the day of judgment. You could call Tyre and Sidon's judgment a coming of the Lord, an advent, a parousia. You could call Sodom and Gomorrah or Noah's flood that.

You see the comparison in Jude and 2 Peter too, bringing out Genesis 6 and what happened in Edom as the Lord coming to judge. Can you call them the day of the Lord in an eschatological pattern? Yes. But the true one, in the light of the gospel's existential truth, is what happens at the end: indignation and consumption.

The idea that the darkened moon and such language just mean a war happening and people being destroyed isn't a wholesome reading. It's true you can see that pre-fulfillment application, but it doesn't remove the true judgment, the true day of the Lord to come. You might hear these leanings out there. You can read the complete eschatological pattern in several biblical events and even today, yet the true pattern has the Lord Jesus at its center, and his coming will conclude it.

## The Coming in Clouds and Christ Among the Churches

I mentioned that because Revelation 1:7 speaks of his coming in clouds. There's an argument that Jesus told Caiaphas he would see him, so it means he was coming soon. People explain that the judgment around AD 70 was like the coming of the Lord, and therefore it's already done: Jesus has come and is now with us. Be careful. You can take an eschatological pattern and make it eschatological truth. The pattern playing out doesn't make it the actual central thing.

Can you see AD 66–70 and such events as the Lord coming in judgment against people rebellious to gospel truth? Yes. But Christian persecution wouldn't be read as the coming of the Lord. You would read it as the wickedness people do to the good before they experience their judgment. If judgment begins in God's house, what will be the lot of those outside? That's how you would read it. That's why I say be careful.

After the introduction of the book and letter, Jesus makes his own introduction: “I am the Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end,” who is, was, and is to come. Then John speaks about himself, their brother and companion in tribulation, kingdom, and the patience of Jesus Christ. He is on Patmos for the word and testimony of Jesus Christ. This is part of the context: they are experiencing tribulation and persecution. He was in the Spirit on the Lord's day and heard the sound.

Signification begins early. We've already seen the seven spirits of the Lord. Verse 12 says he saw seven golden lampstands, and among them one like the Son of Man, with a garment to his feet, a golden band around his chest, head and hair white like wool or snow, and eyes like flames. Both are thrown into the lake of fire burning with brimstone.

The rest are killed with the sword proceeding from his mouth, the judgment he speaks. Birds are filled with their flesh. Jesus appears there again, as in chapter 14, because it is the same judgment. The idea of several chronological second comings is, I think, a mistake. It is one end described from several angles.

This is judgment of governance and spiritual order, worship order. I'm not speaking of spiritual authority, the devil and his forces. Chapter 20 is for them.

## The Thousand Years as the Church Age

Revelation 20:1–15 is the seventh eschatological account: judgment of spiritual authority, including sin, death, the devil, and his forces. The account is now told from the devil's point of view. By the church's reign he is considered restrained, but at the tail end he has an overwhelming effect.

Like the account where the angels cast him down, this account presents his restraint through the church. Other accounts say his activity is worse in persecution. Then, when the thousand years expire, verse 7 says Satan is released from prison to deceive nations at earth's four corners, Gog and Magog, and gather them for battle.

We've explained repeatedly that the thousand years describe the church age. The final three and a half years are also described as the thousand years. Restraint for a thousand years doesn't mean Jesus has done everything, the beast and false prophet are gone, all of us have gone, others remain on earth, and then the devil returns after a thousand years to disturb them. There is nothing like that. It's the same account from another angle.

The thousand years are the period of the church's reign. Many things point to it. The period of restraint is where the reign of the church is described, not a later reign but the present reign today. Let me read so that it helps.

Revelation 20:4: “I saw thrones, and they sat on them, and judgment was committed to them.” This is within the thousand years after his restraint. “Then I saw the souls of those who had been beheaded for their witness to Jesus and for the word of God, who had not worshiped the beast or his image, and had not received his mark on their foreheads or their hands. And they lived and reigned with Christ for a thousand years.”

“The rest of the dead did not live again until the thousand years were finished. This is the first resurrection.” Those who died in this period as believers are still alive. “Blessed and holy is he who has part in the first resurrection. Over such the second death has no power.”

Revelation 2:11 speaks of the second death having no power over those believing the gospel. That means they have experienced the first resurrection, a spiritual resurrection. Those who aren't believers in this thousand years are considered dead. Believers are considered alive and reigning with Christ, part of the first resurrection, even though there is still a second resurrection. The second death has no power over them.

“They shall be priests of God and of Christ, and shall reign with him a thousand years.” Where? On earth. When they're called kings and priests, they are reigning on earth.

## The Final Rebellion and the Judgment of Death

When the thousand years expire, Satan is released and deceives the nations in earth's four corners, Gog and Magog, gathering them to battle, as numerous as the sand of the sea. They go across earth's breadth and surround the saints' camp and beloved city. They're against the church. There's intense persecution at the tail end, or intended intense persecution.

Fire comes down from God out of heaven and devours them. The devil who deceived them is cast into the lake of fire and brimstone where the beast and false prophet are, and they are tormented day and night forever. Then we see judgment, in which even death and Hades are cast into the lake. Everybody comes out, there is judgment, and they are cast into the lake of fire.

Revelation 20:1–15 shows the judgment of spiritual authority as a whole. Sin, death, the devil, and his forces are all judged there.

## The End of Mortality and the New Heaven and Earth

The last part is Revelation 21:1–22:21: the end of mortality and corruption and entrance into the new heaven and new earth, or the entrance of the new heaven and new earth.

Let me give the outline again. We have ten parts. Revelation 1:1–20 is the introduction. Revelation 2:1–3:22 is the seven churches. Revelation 4:1–8:1/5 is the seven seals. Revelation 8:1/5–11:19 is the seven trumpets. Revelation 12:1–14:20 is persecution of Jewish saints and the church, and the judgment following it.

Revelation 15:1–16:21 is the seven bowls or vials. Revelation 17:1–18:24 is judgment of economic, social, and moral order, the woman Babylon. Revelation 19:1–21 is judgment of governance and spiritual or worship order. Revelation 20:1–15 is judgment of spiritual authority, including sin, death, the devil, and his forces. Revelation 21:1–22:21 is the end of mortality and corruption and the entrance of new heavens and a new earth.

That's the ten-part outline. I believe with this you can understand the book. The part I didn't mention in Revelation 9, I believe, concerns demons released to make people walk in the flesh. ## Living in Light of the Book's Message

Our goal for understanding Revelation as a single teaching is complete, but your understanding of the whole book is not yet complete. You must hear the whole teaching where we went verse by verse, explaining it so there would be no confusion. I think someone can pick up Revelation with this teaching and begin to see it make sense.

There was an immediate application or intention in the author's mind, but it has a continuing effect today. Governance order, economic order, social and moral order still play out. The devil's activities behind the scenes still lead to what happens. We must be aware as this age continues.

It particularly gives us the disposition of remembering that the end is near. We will be accountable and judged. We must live for God's purpose and fulfill his mission. We must be inspired by that to live righteously.

Revelation is not a mysterious book saying weird things beyond the other Scriptures. It does it graphically. We can find the same thing in the Olivet Discourse and epistles. It's more graphic and highly figurative to express the same truth, because of the Lord Jesus' intention with that account.

On that note we bring this teaching to a close. Father, we thank you. We bless your name. We walk in the truth of your word revealed, in Jesus' name. Amen.
