# The Process of Bible Interpretation

## The Church, God's Purpose, and the Knowledge We Need

Our Father, I thank you that I am your child and a part of your family. In this meeting, gathered with my siblings, I receive of your word light, truth, revelation of Christ and his work in all its fullness, and of his fullness that is in me. In the name of the Lord Jesus, amen.

We are looking at the process of Bible interpretation. This is Level Three, and this is the second teaching in Level Three. I want us to start from Ephesians 4:11, to see what Paul says about the church. Speaking about the Lord Jesus, he said he himself gave the apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers. Paul was talking about Jesus giving the leaders of the church and why he gave them. He says he gave them for the perfecting of the saints.

This is the primary reason why we have the church: for us to be perfected or discipled as saints. If you ask why he wants us perfected as saints, the answer is so that we can fulfill his purpose. The reason God made us in the first place is the purpose he had from the onset. There is a purpose God has for our lives. From Level One you would have seen what God's purpose is. God's purpose is sonship. He wants us to walk as his children as well as his heirs. It means we are to fellowship with him, walk in his nature and character, and carry out the assignments and responsibilities he has for us: our civic responsibility, natural assignment, and spiritual assignment. If that is his purpose, he tells us the church is designed to help us fulfill it.

God has a purpose for my life, and he gave the church to help me fulfill that purpose. What we are looking for in Ephesians 4 is how the church would help us fulfill that purpose. It is simple to know God has a purpose. The Lord Jesus' death and resurrection is to redeem us back to that purpose, and that has been done. Your faith in Christ brings you into that purpose. You are already in God's purpose. But there is a question that must be answered: if the church is given to help us fulfill God's purpose, what exactly is the church meant to do?

It says the church is given for the perfecting of the saints. The next statement is “for the work of ministry,” but we have said a more accurate reading of that text would be “unto the work of ministry.” The first word “for” is *pros*: towards the perfecting of the saints. This is their direction, their work. The second is *eis*: unto the work of ministry. The last one says unto the edifying of the body of Christ. When the church perfects the saints, the saints will do their assignments—particularly here their spiritual assignment. They will do the work of ministry, and the body of Christ will be edified, built up. That building is both internally and externally. As a saint doing the work of ministry, you are not only bringing people to the church or adding people; you are also helping those people become stronger spiritually.

How will they perfect the saints? Verse 13 answers that question. That word “perfecting,” *katartismos*, means you are to bring something to an end. If he is telling us that the church is to perfect the saints, he needs to show us the endpoint to which the church will be brought. The way he shows that endpoint reveals what would be done to get us there. We exist for God's purpose. We have been redeemed by the Lord Jesus into God's purpose. The church was given to perfect us to fulfill that purpose. When we find out the end God intends us to be brought to, it also shows how we will be brought to that end.

Look at verse 13: “Till we all come to the unity of the faith.” The next statement says “the knowledge of the Son of God,” then “the perfect man,” and finally “the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.” He shows us four descriptions of where God wants to bring us.

“Unity of the faith” means wholesome doctrine, because “faith” there is what you have believed. Ordinarily the word faith is the act of believing. If you want to speak about believing, the verb, as a noun, you say faith: someone believes. But faith can also be used for what is believed. Here he is saying we are to come to the unity or wholesomeness of what is believed, the entirety of what we have already believed to be saved. We are to come to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God. In the first two instances, he is definitely talking about knowledge or information.

The next statement says “the perfect man,” and the last says “the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.” The last two are not talking about information. They are talking about a person, the state of growth a person has gotten to. The perfect man is an adult. You have grown into the perfect man, the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.

If the church is going to see us fulfill God's purpose, it must bring us to an endpoint in knowledge as well as an endpoint in growth. That growth is dependent on the knowledge of God's word. We are supposed to become stronger in our inner man and adorn our hidden man by our faith stand. The growth that is supposed to happen to us depends on the knowledge we have. When we receive that knowledge of God's word, we will be able by that knowledge to grow in what we have known. For the church to get us to fulfill God's purpose, what is primary is the knowledge of God's word.

Upon that knowledge—the unity of the faith and knowledge of the Son of God—we are going to take our faith stand. We are going to be found affirming, reckoning, presenting ourselves to walk the way God wants us to walk. The first thing we need is the knowledge of God's word.

That is where our study today is focused. We are looking at the process of Bible interpretation. What I am about to show us is that your fulfillment of God's purpose depends on how effectively, accurately, or precisely you engage Bible interpretation. Ordinarily we would say your fulfillment of God's purpose depends on the knowledge of God's word: a particular belief system, an entire framework of belief, the unity of the faith. But how do you even get that message? How do we arrive at the message we are going to grow by?

## The Apostolic Message and Paul's Responsibility to Present It

The first thing we might notice is that Paul tells us he has presented that message. In Ephesians 3, he even told them he had written about it in the earlier parts of the book. One way we can get this message is to look at what the apostles taught. You might say, “I don't know what you are talking about when you say we need Bible interpretation to know the message to fulfill God's purpose. We can simply go to what the apostles taught.” On one hand, you are right. The message we need to grow so that we can fulfill God's purpose is the message taught by the apostles.

Don't forget that I am making a heavy claim: your fulfillment of God's purpose depends on Bible interpretation. Another way to say it is that it depends on the truth. You need the truth to be saved, and you need the truth to grow. Once you have received the gospel, you are talking about learning the same message you have received. That message was taught to us by the apostles. We must receive that unity of the faith so that we grow to the stature that will cause us to fulfill God's purpose.

Ephesians 3:1: “For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles—if indeed you have heard of the dispensation of the grace of God which was given to me for you.” Paul is saying he had the privilege of having the grace to present the message. What he says here is also what he said in 1 Corinthians 3: “According to the grace of God which is given unto me, as a wise master builder, I have laid the foundation.” Paul is speaking about a grace particularly given to him to present the message.

He says, “How that by revelation he made known to me the mystery, as I have briefly written already, by which, when you read, you may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ.” Paul is saying, “I have already taught what God wants us to know.” He had already taught it in the earlier parts of this book. If you ask what message we need to grow so that we can fulfill God's purpose, you will say it is what the apostles taught. But let me add another detail. If you know better, you will say it is what the apostles taught as they taught the Holy Scriptures.

Romans 16:25: “Now to him who is able to establish you according to my gospel.” Does that sound familiar to Ephesians 3:4? There it says “my knowledge”; here it says “my gospel.” “According to my gospel and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery kept secret since the world began, but now made manifest, and by the scriptures of the prophets”—if you use the KJV. The NKJV says “by the prophetic Scriptures”—“made known to all nations.” On one hand he calls it “my gospel”; on the other hand he says it is by the Scriptures that we are making it known.

Where do we get this knowledge, this message that is going to cause us to grow and fulfill God's purpose? You can say from the apostles. You might even add particularly Paul, or centrally Paul, and you wouldn't be wrong because of the grace given to him. I gave an example of this, perhaps in Level Two: God doesn't just tell this message to Paul. Paul has the role of an architect. He actually tells it to several people. The apostles were already preaching that message in Acts 2:42: “They continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine.” Paul's role is to synthesize that message, to give it its most exact and precise presentation.

I used the illustration of having an uncle outside Nigeria, in a first-world country, an economy that is not similar to Nigeria's, where converting the money gives it considerable value here. He is earning well, but there is the weariness that he is being taxed a lot. If he does not plan ahead, anything could happen, and he might need a safe haven. He may have a little good money, but buying a house there could take a while to pay off, depending on what he does. He says, “I could probably build a house easily in Nigeria.”

He plans to build a house in Nigeria and calls the other family members. Let's assume he is the firstborn and has four siblings. He calls them and says, “I want to build a house in Nigeria. This is what I have in mind. I want it to be my house, but probably also to serve as a family house, so I want us to make some other things.” He tells them the whole plan: how many buildings he wants in the compound, the kind of buildings, whether a semi-detached building or a duplex. He tells them exactly what he wants.

As informed as they are about what he wants, they can communicate it to other people. They could tell their spouses or children. But as informed as they are, they cannot be as exact as an architect. They will still need to get an architect and say, “This is what our elder brother wants to build. We need you to draw the plan and the design.”

When Paul makes that heavy claim about himself, and you see him say “my gospel” and “my knowledge in the mystery of Christ,” he is making a claim to his presentation, not to a new content. His presentation includes a particular detail that was especially an emphasis of his: the inclusion of the Gentiles. Notwithstanding, we need his presentation. It is a heavy claim he makes, acknowledged by Peter in 2 Peter 3:15–16: “The longsuffering of our Lord is salvation, even as our beloved brother Paul, according to the wisdom given to him.” That wisdom is talking about the same grace Paul says makes him a wise master builder. The grace is a wisdom to be an architect.

He is saying, “I have this wisdom, this grace, that makes me able to present as an architect the message that is supposed to be taught.” We should be very comfortable with Pauline theology as the apostolic theology, as God's own theology: God's message, and God become man giving that message to his apostles. The Lord Jesus' message, the apostles' message, what they were teaching, is still the Pauline message, but now having God's intended presentation.

When people speak about Paul, you might start feeling it is one message different from the other. It is not. It is the prophets' message. The same house God wanted to build is what he was telling the prophets about—the same purpose and plan he had. Like that elder brother telling the others what he wants to build, it is the same thing God had been telling men even before the Lord Jesus was on the scene. It is the same thing the Lord Jesus was saying, the same thing the apostles were saying, and the same thing Paul is saying, but now accurately, exactly, and precisely presenting it. It is important that we recognize that.

## Having the Scriptures Does Not Guarantee Right Interpretation

If I say we need this message to grow, you may say, “Let's go to the apostles and get it,” or, “Let's go to the Scriptures and get it.” But people have gone to the apostles and the Scriptures and made a mess of it. We are looking at the process of Bible interpretation, and we are saying our fulfillment of God's purpose depends on Bible interpretation.

Bible interpretation might be the reason you do not know God's purpose for your life. Someone can tell you, “This is God's will for you,” and it is not entirely accurate. That too is a matter of Bible interpretation. The subject of God's purpose is an issue at the core, and interpretation can rob you of the truth of that matter. If you ask many people what God's purpose is for someone's life, they might tell you it is just to know God, or to worship him. In saying that, you might lose sight of the entire picture of what God will judge you on at the end, because he already revealed in his word what his purpose is.

In Luke 12:47–48, he said that he who did not know and did not do according to his will would be beaten with few stripes, while he who knew and did not prepare would be beaten with many stripes. Not knowing exactly what he has said his will and purpose are is still going to affect you. That issue itself is a matter of interpretation. It is not the case that the apostles did not say what God's purpose is, or that the prophets did not, or that Paul did not present it exactly. It will be the case that you have not seen it. The issue would not be that the message you need is not with the apostles or prophets. The issue is interpretation.

Second Peter highlights the seriousness of this issue and why we need to understand the process of interpretation. Second Peter 3:15: “Consider that the longsuffering of our Lord is salvation, even as our beloved brother Paul, according to the wisdom given to him, has written to you.” Verse 16: “As also in all his epistles.” What I like about this text is that Peter is recognizing the scriptural authority of Paul's writings, the divine authority given to the apostles to submit the message, and recognizing Paul's inclusion in that.

“As also in all his epistles, speaking in them of these things, in which are some things hard to understand.” In his epistles he is writing certain subject matters, some of which are hard to understand. There are some subject matters that are difficult by nature. Think about studying mathematics. Mathematics is sometimes the most straightforward of subjects. One of the less straightforward subjects is English, particularly pronunciation. You find there is a British pronunciation and an American pronunciation, and you wonder which you have been using. I found out about the British and American pronunciations of “wrath” and “new,” and I am still healing! It is not a straightforward issue because that is human decision, consensus, agreement.

Mathematics is very straightforward, but some subject matters in mathematics are almost by nature more difficult. I remember wanting to start learning further mathematics a little because of competitions we used to go for. Those who had been doing further mathematics from SS1 did not have a problem. As I started going further, I began to realize I wasn't sure I wanted all that stress. I was good in mathematics. I could compete with or even beat those who did further mathematics in regular mathematics. I wasn't bad at it. But permutation and combination—what was all this?

There are things you study which, just by the nature of the subject matter, are difficult. It is not the person writing who is difficult. Even if the person endeavors to be clear, you still need to pay attention. For example, some subjects on the podcast you receive pretty easily. With certain other matters you need to think a bit harder, exercise yourself a bit more.

There are those areas when it comes to doctrinal truths. Paul is the one giving us clarity because he has wisdom. His is the best explanation we could possibly have; he is the architect. It cannot be that Paul is the one making things hard. It is the simplest and clearest explanation we could possibly have, yet some subject matters by nature are difficult, and therefore they need a bit of patience.

He is speaking in his epistles of salvation and biblical truths, some of which are hard to understand. Don't say Paul himself is hard to understand. If Paul has wisdom and clarity and you say he is hard to understand, what about those who don't? It is some of the subject matters that are hard to understand.

Then he says, “Which untaught and unstable people twist to their own destruction, as they do also the rest of the Scriptures.” There are people who go to the apostles, and also to the Scriptures of the prophets, and twist them. They wrestle with them, struggle with them, trying to force out a meaning different from what it should be. That already shows you a kind of interpretation. They reference the writings of Paul and of the prophets, and yet they are twisting them.

How do we rightly know what the apostles and prophets are saying, in a way that we are certain we are not twisting their words or forcing out a meaning other than the one they intend? When it says untaught and unstable people twist them to their destruction, are they using the text? Yes. It is not something else they are twisting. They are twisting the very text. The apostles and the Scriptures make up the Bible. They are using the Bible, but are they arriving at the meaning the Bible authors intend? No. It is possible to have the text and not arrive at the meaning intended by the biblical authors.

## The Author's Intention, the Text's Rendition, and the Reader's Interpretation

What is going on here is wrong interpretation. Right interpretation is not to create a meaning. Right interpretation is to find the meaning. I have said it this way: the author's intention is to be taken as the text's rendition. We are not with the author asking, “Is that what you mean?” Once he has written—and he knew the weight upon him as an apostle or prophet speaking what God expects him to write—what he is writing or saying is what he intends. If you write what you do not intend, you are either lying or have made a mistake. By God's orchestration, we are not going to take this divinely orchestrated document that way, whether the Old Testament text or the New.

The author's intention is to be taken as the text's rendition. That author's intention, which is the text's rendition, must become the reader's interpretation. What he intended and rendered in text is what we must also see. If we have the source of the message that is supposed to give us growth to fulfill purpose, how do we interpret that text to arrive at that message?

Our growth, walk, and fulfillment of God's purpose depend on that message. The message is codified in a text. They have presented it. The author's message, intention, and meaning are in the text's rendition. How do we arrive at the message they are giving us?

The Scriptures are the source of the message. The real question is how we find the message from the text. How do we decode it? I don't mean they were trying to encode it secretly. Writing is a form of encoding in another sense: you communicate a meaning with the symbols of writing. How do we decode the meaning intended, written, and coded by the authors?

Someone can say, “It's simple: just read it.” Were the people twisting it not reading? We like to go to Ephesians 3:4, “By which, when you read, you may understand,” and say that once we read, we understand. I am not debating that when we read, we understand. We are about to see reading rightly done, because those people were also reading, yet twisting it into another message.

As I am exposed to the kinds of arguments people make online, on one hand I feel like going away, but on the other hand I am concerned. If these mindsets exist, I have to address them. It is part of “all nations,” “every creature.” There is a lot of pride and assumption, just concluding, without the humility people should have. It is good to feel, “I think I have the truth, and I am living by it.” On the other hand, someone who has the truth is always willing to examine. If I have the truth, I shouldn't fear examining what any other person is saying.

I don't spend all my time there, because it will affect your growth, your inner man, your hidden man. When you have to pause and spend time on something, it will affect you. Sometimes you can sacrifice for a while, but you cannot do that all the time and mess up your mind. Particularly within the faith, you should be able to listen to what someone else is saying. Listen through. Don't assume they are already wrong so that from the beginning you are looking for what to fault. When you are done, you can know what you know.

The process of Bible interpretation should make you patient. You shouldn't be dismissive, quick to dismiss everything. Of course, there are abuses and outright falsehoods, but you cannot approach them with an impatient attitude. The servant of the Lord must not strive; he must be patient both to teach and to listen, so he knows what he is responding to.

## The God-Orchestrated Documents and Their Message

I took us to 2 Timothy 3:16, although I am going to go earlier. “All scripture is given by inspiration of God.” We can say it is orchestrated by God. “God-breathed” is orchestrated by God. The entire document is God's orchestration. Sometimes people might want to argue that it is the content of the document that is God's orchestration, but there is more reason to argue that the document is what he is saying is God's orchestration. Of course, the document contains the content. It is God's intention that this very document be his authoritative document for truth, containing the message that still requires interpretation.

Let me explain that word “God-breathed.” There are parts to it. The *theo* part is pretty easy. There is the other part, the *pneu* part, where we have *pneuma*. Some people might want to take it from the standpoint of *psuchē*, like when we study spirit or soul, which you will see later in Level Three and which is on the podcast. The word *pneuma* means an invisible reality that has visible effect. That is *pneuma*, p-n-e-u-m-a, the word spirit or wind. The word *psuchē* has a similar implication: breath, an invisible reality with animating effects or animating power. But they did not use *psuchē* here, so we should stick with *pneuma*, *pneō*, and related words: something invisible is having a visible effect.

If we stick with that, when he says *theopneustos*, he is saying the documents are caused by God. They are a visible effect of an invisible source, and that invisible source is God, is divine. They are divinely effected. I don't want to speak of them as divinely inspired merely in the sense that the message they carry contains divinely inspired utterances. There is a place for that if you leave the document and speak of the content, saying God breathed or inspired the things within it. He referred to the content earlier in verse 15, no doubt. But in verse 16 he says *pas*, *graphē*—using the base words here. All the Scriptures, the documents, are God-breathed. The effect we are seeing is from an invisible source.

Interestingly, if someone asks you about the document, you could tell them its visible source: Isaiah wrote this one, this person wrote that one, Paul wrote this one. We know the New Testament text was not just in view here, but we are applying it the same way. He is saying the documents are God-orchestrated, effected, brought to be by God.

They are given by the orchestration of God, and they are profitable for doctrine. That means the minister is to teach them. For reproof: to give evidence. For correction: to straighten people up where they are crooked or missing it. For instruction in righteousness: that is child-training or growth in righteousness. If someone is going to grow, be straightened out, and have the persuasion to walk in all that God desires, they need the Scriptures, that document, the text. But they need the Scriptures to give them the message.

How do we move from the Scriptures, the document, to the message? From manuscripts to meaning? From the text to what it says and means? They are given for this purpose: “That the man of God may be complete, thoroughly furnished,” or equipped, “for every good work.” If you will do all God desires, you need the document, but you need the document to give you its message. Paul tells us how we move from document to message.

## Fully Following the Apostolic Doctrine

If we are going to speak of anybody as owning, as it were, the message because of his responsibility to present it, that person would be Paul, by God's election. God chose him to be his architect. Look at 2 Timothy 3:10. The KJV says, “You have fully known”; the NKJV says, “You have carefully followed.” I believe the best translation would be, “You have fully followed.” We can take the “fully” from the KJV and the “followed” from the NKJV.

The word there is *parakoloutheō*. It is from *para* and *akoloutheō*. The latter is explained from *alpha* and a word meaning road [Greek derivation unclear in the captions]. The alpha there is a union, not a negative in that case. It means you are together with me on that road. Then *para* means beside: you have been with me on that road, together by my side. We have been side by side on the same road.

When he says, “You have carefully followed my doctrine,” it is like describing the doctrine as a road map, a road, a journey. When you are racing, there are several laps; in some races it is the same exact road, but several laps. You know when to turn and what to do. Imagine the doctrine in that manner. That is why doctrine is sometimes called a way. In 1 Corinthians 4:17, Paul says he will send his son to them, who will remind them of his ways, as he teaches everywhere in every church. Ways are like a road, like a journey. Even in the Old Testament text, the word “way” or “ways” is used to describe the doctrine.

Imagine the doctrine like that. Timothy has been on the same road with Paul, side by side. He had gone one, two, three laps with Paul on that doctrinal journey, and he can now tell, “This is how the message is. This is the way to go. We are supposed to show this, then show that, then present it like this.”

“You have been on the same road with me, side by side.” In this sense, it is not that you have been careful in following my doctrine that is the issue—not that he has not been careful. It is that you have gone that road, the entire road, with me by my side. It is a matter of having fully followed. That is why you can understand the KJV using “fully known.” You can also understand why the NKJV uses “followed,” because it is a matter of a road, but it should be “fully followed.” It is not just the manner in which you followed; you were completely there.

Timothy has fully followed Paul's doctrine, and he mentions the things he learns in the doctrine: his manner of life, his purpose, his faith, longsuffering, love, perseverance, persecutions and afflictions that happened to him in Antioch—the persecutions Paul endured, and God delivered him out of them all.

Then in verse 12 he says those who would live godly in Christ Jesus must suffer persecution. You cannot have acceptance from everybody. The degree of persecution might differ, but if you are going to live godly in Christ, you will not have general acceptance. You cannot be liked by everybody. Then verse 13: “But evil men and impostors will grow worse and worse, deceiving and being deceived.” Remember, verse 13 follows his saying, “You have carefully followed my doctrine,” but there are evil men who will grow worse and worse, deceiving people.

## Paul's Doctrine and Those Who Resist the Truth

You will not understand what Paul means when he mentions evil men deceiving and being deceived until you read the earlier verses that led up to “You have carefully followed my doctrine.” We are still looking at the process of Bible interpretation. Second Timothy 3:7 says, “Always learning and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth.”

In this particular material, we know Paul's truth. When he says “the truth,” the intention is existential truth, the truth of existence, which he believes—and he has explained—that the gospel is the answer to that question. Paul's truth is the gospel. Paul had been concerned that people had been deviating from that truth in this material. You would have seen him use “truth” about three times earlier than this particular passage in 2 Timothy 3.

Second Timothy 2:15 says Timothy should rightly divide the word of truth. We will come back to that. In 2 Timothy 2:18, he was mentioning, from verse 17, the message of some people. He said their message will spread like cancer. Hymenaeus and Philetus are of this sort. Is it possible for people to be preaching something false and for it to grow like wildfire? When you say “like cancer,” it is growing like wildfire. Sometimes people even take something that is the truth, distort it here and there, and make something that is not the best—like cancer affecting healthy cells and making a mess of things.

He says their message spreads like cancer: Hymenaeus and Philetus, “who have strayed concerning the truth.” He is saying our own message is the truth; Timothy will be rightly dividing it. But some people have strayed concerning the truth, saying that the resurrection is past already, and they overthrow the faith of some. People can mess up people's persuasion and even their growth, which is their faith stand. They can mess up their conviction, their faith and their growth by what they preach.

Look at 2 Timothy 2:24 and 25. “A servant of the Lord must not strive,” or quarrel, “but must be gentle to all.” It is as if people do not even want to obey that, as if it does not exist in the Bible, as if it is not part of God's word. It is as if they are willing to be judged by God at the end: “We want to lose our reward at all costs. We want to suffer. We want to be flogged.” People are quarrelling shamelessly, with a quarrelsome spirit that is not expected of a bishop, as in 1 Timothy 3. They are unnecessarily troublesome and quarrelsome in how they approach and present God's word. I wonder why, on all sides.

It says the servant of the Lord must not strive but must be gentle to all, apt to teach, patient—effective at presenting the word—“in humility correcting those who are in opposition, if God perhaps will grant them repentance.” There are times people teach and do not even want people to know the truth; they just want to shine. Your goal is that you want to persuade people, so you are conscious: “If I say this now, if I leave it like that, they will be more focused on the offence than the truth. Let me say it calmly.”

It should take someone deliberately rebellious—someone who knows you have said it already and is trying to get you to step a little out of character, so they can say, “You are just being like this.” But it should not be your natural disposition. That is abnormal. It should not be your default. He says, “Correcting those who are in opposition, if God perhaps will grant them repentance, so that they may know the truth.”

The entire context of this material is that Timothy will have been teaching the truth, and some people are going off teaching other things. He goes back to those same people from 2 Timothy 3:1. He starts talking about perilous times coming in the last days. Men will be lovers of themselves, lovers of money. He shows the things that will happen because of the error of the teaching of certain people.

Read from verse 6: “For of this sort are those who creep into households and make captives of gullible women.” These people with this terrible character creep into households, making captives of gullible women. Of course, it is not because they are women. It is a social thing more than a natural thing: there was a tendency for women at that time to be gullible. It was a social condition that unfortunately existed. They are led away by various lusts, “always learning and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth.”

Now verse 8: “As Jannes and Jambres resisted Moses”—you could check the etymology of those names; they are Egyptians—“so do these also resist the truth.” Is it the same truth Moses was preaching? Yes. “Men of corrupt minds, disapproved concerning the faith; but they will progress no further, for their folly will be manifest to all, as theirs also was.” He is likely quoting from an oral tradition about something that happened then, perhaps the Talmud [unclear reference].

There are people presently opposing the truth, just as there were people in Moses' day opposing the truth. Paul now says, “But you have carefully followed my doctrine.” He is calling his doctrine the truth. By the time he gets to verse 13 and says evil men and impostors will grow worse and worse, deceiving and being deceived, he is talking about those people opposing the truth. They will not stop. They will grow worse and worse, deceiving and being deceived.

## The Authority of Interpretation Must Be a Definite Process

Now look at verse 14. Remember everything we have said to this point. We have said it takes the message of the apostles and the Scriptures to fulfil God's purpose. We already have what they said in written form: the Old and New Testament texts. The question is, how do we know the message in what they said? It is possible to have what they said, what they wrote, and to twist it, according to 2 Peter 3:15 and 16. How do we know exactly what they were saying?

If they do not give us a process of going from their written text to their message, to what they mean, then there is trouble. Anybody can handle the text any way they like and arrive at whatever meaning they think is right, and then make a mess of things. Even worse, when we lack a definite process of interpretation, people can make themselves the authority of interpretation. That is the problem. The authority of interpretation cannot be people. Humans are fallible.

You cannot just say, “I trust them. I have no fear.” That is not the way life works. You cannot just trust me. You can trust me based on precedent, but even then you should not enter a state of blind trust. Generally, you may have a level of trust because someone tries to stay faithful with this. But how can you call people to trust just out of the blue? Imagine that is how you go and get married: you do not build a relationship with someone to know the person enough before knowing whether to put trust in the person. Nobody does that. There will be a bit of track record, a bit of precedent, a bit of observation.

Do not locate the authority of interpretation in people; rather, it should be in a process. You can trust your science teacher, but there is a process. You know that if we do this, it produces this. Imagine he tells you to do something combustible that will probably blow up in your face, and you know that this process is going to blow up in your face. Even if you have always trusted the person, at that point you need to pause and tell him to do it first and see whether it affects him. You cannot say you will just trust. If you do that, you are not wise.

There is nothing about saying, “I am loyal to my teacher.” I understand, but that is not loyalty. If you know that, with everything we have learned and the process, this will blow up in your face, say, “Do it first. When it does not blow up, and you have that process, I will do it. That is why you are the teacher.” The only problem is when it concerns things whose negative effects take a long time to be seen. That is the unfortunate case here.

How do we move from the text—what the apostles and prophets said—to the meaning? That is the challenge we face today. We have said it has to be a process of interpretation. Let me add that we do not need methods of interpretation in the sense of, “This is my own approach. This is the way I go about interpreting.” We need the process. We do not necessarily even need concepts of interpretation. Within the process, people can come up with concepts reflecting that process. Those are secondary. They are extra layers on what is basic.

## Methods and Concepts Must Serve the Process

Let me give an example. If you are playing football, your goal is to get past the opposing players to their goal. When the game starts, everybody is on either side and the ball is in the middle. Your goal is to get the ball into the net: to get it past the players, including the goalkeeper, into the net. That is the simple goal of the game.

There are different ways of getting the ball past the players and into the net. Someone can send it from defence to someone who runs. They can decide to play through and get the ball through the players. As an individual player, you want to get the ball past the players into the net. You can start doing a lot of skills. You do a leg-over, the person goes, then you move forward again. You can simply run past and ensure you get them out of your way. But you can also decide to do certain skills: you cut it, someone goes, then you move forward to the other person, and you keep going like that.

There is no problem with those extra things you are doing. It is nice as long as you get the ball in the net. But you could have stayed with the most stripped-down approach: “I will go past all of them”—it is still dribbling, no doubt, but without some extra skills—and get the ball in the net. That is called efficiency. Certain players have dropped some things they used to do, or gone into certain roles, to be more efficient for the purpose of putting the ball in.

That is almost the way I see the use of methods and concepts when it comes to interpretation. Some things are extras. They are not the issue. They must even be aligned with the process, and sometimes you might question whether they align with the process. You cannot come up with a concept and make the concept the arbiter of truth in interpreting the text. It must be the process of interpretation that leads us to the meaning or the message.

I have said there is nothing necessarily wrong with methods and concepts. For example, people say things like “the law of first mention.” Nothing is wrong with looking, as you think through God's word, at the first time a concept came up. It might even be that you intend to use first principles when you say “the law of first mention.” But there is a way someone takes that concept or method and abuses it, because it is not the process of interpretation. You have created a concept or method, and you can get into all sorts of problems with it. Someone can go to anything and say, “The first time it was mentioned, it was this, so here it means this.” What kind of rubbish is that? What is the process of Bible interpretation?

Methods can be okay and concepts can be fine. But what we need, as you will shortly see—instructed by the apostles, instructed by the Scriptures, and necessitated by the truth—is the process of Bible interpretation. The truth itself calls for its own process of Bible interpretation.

## The Text, the Process and the Message

Second Timothy 3:14 and 15 are the texts we will start with. It says, “But continue in the things which you have learned and have been assured of, knowing from whom you have learned them.” When it says the things you have learned, what is that? The message: the different subject matters, the details of what you have been taught. He is saying, “Continue in a message that you got from a particular source.” I am using “message” as singular, but with many subject matters, because he says “things.” Continue in the message you got from a particular source, knowing from whom you learned it.

Who did he learn it from? Paul. Second Timothy 2:2: the things you have learned from me among many witnesses, commit the same to faithful men who will be able to teach others also. He learned it from Paul. Paul is representative of the apostles, and because of that central role we can speak today of the New Testament texts, because of the role of the New Testament texts.

“Continue in the things which you have learned and have been assured of, knowing from whom you have learned them, and that from a child you have known…” He reminds him of whom he learned from, and that from a child he has also known the Holy Scriptures. What is the source of the message? Paul and the Old Testament text. For us today, we say the New Testament text and the Old Testament text. In one word, we say the Bible. You can say the Christian Bible, or the Protestant Bible: the Bible.

Look at the next statement: “Which are able to make you wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ.” Did you see that process? “Timothy, the things I have taught you, the message you have learned, continue in it, knowing that you got it from a source that would make you wise to its message.” You do not just arrive at its message. There is a process between the source and the message from that source.

You have learned from me and the Holy Scriptures, “which are able to make you wise unto salvation.” There is the source. There is the process. The arrival at the message is by a process called “making you wise.” The text makes you wise, and you arrive at the message. There is a source, the text; there is the process, which in this statement says it makes you wise; and there is the message.

At this juncture, we can say that the process by which we arrive at the meaning or message from the text, from the source, is what he terms here “make you wise.” The Greek word is *sophizō*. It is a verb: it makes you *sophos*. *Sophizō* is from *sophos*. If you check Strong's, it says to make or render *sophos*. *Sophizō* is to make someone *sophos*, which means we need to know what it means to be *sophos*. We are using the Greek words this way for the purpose of a simple explanation.

The Scriptures will *sophizō* you to the message from the text. Is the Scriptures' making you wise to the message automatic? No. Otherwise, 2 Peter 3 would not have been said. It means there is a precise way by which, from the Scriptures, you are made wise to the message. *Sophizō* is to make you *sophos*. *Sophos* is from the word *saphēs*, which means clear. *Sophos*, the adjective “wise,” means someone who has clarity. Clarity itself is the word *sophia*: wisdom. Someone has wisdom, *sophia*.

## Wisdom and Understanding: Putting the Parts Together

We need to find out what it means to be *sophos*, wise, and how one arrives at being wise. Matthew 11:25 says, “At that time Jesus answered and said, I thank You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent and have revealed them to babes.” This use of wise and prudent is negative. There is a positive use, but this one is negative. I want us to track something here.

“Wise” is *sophos*. Do you notice that they put wise and prudent together? They are parallel or complementary words. They explain each other. The word “wise” is *sophos*, and “prudent” is *synetos*. You see that same thought again in Luke 10:21. We are trying to understand what it means for someone to be wise, so we can see how someone is made wise. How do you arrive at having *sophia*, which makes you a *sophos* person, a wise person? How you arrive at having *sophia* is how you are made wise. To *sophizō* is to give you wisdom so that you are wise. How do you arrive at having that?

Luke 10:21: “In that hour Jesus rejoiced in the Spirit and said, I thank You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent and revealed them to babes.” Again, wise and prudent are parallel or complementary words; they explain each other.

First Corinthians 1:19 will show you the parallel nature of wise and prudent and help us appreciate how we are made wise. Interestingly, this also concerns negatively wise or prudent people: people who ignore revelatory truth because they do not accept the truth of God even from natural truth. If you do not accept that, then you cannot accept revelatory truth, which becomes a problem. But that is not our focus here.

“For it is written, I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and will bring to nothing the understanding of the prudent.” “Wisdom” is *sophia*, “wise” is *sophos*, “understanding” is *synesis*, and “prudent” is *synetos*. Paul told us he is a wise master builder. Could Paul also be called prudent? If he is a wise master builder, or as Peter says, there is wisdom, *sophia*, given to him, can we say he has *synesis* if wisdom and understanding are used as parallel words?

If Paul has wisdom, he must also have understanding. Does Paul have *synesis*? Yes. Ephesians 3:4 says, “By which, when you read, you may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ.” That word “knowledge” in Ephesians 3:4 is *synesis*: “You may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ, which in other ages was not made known unto the sons of men.”

If we know how we arrive at wisdom, *sophia*, or how we arrive at understanding, then we know how we can be made wise. The word that will help us here is *synesis*, because it explains itself. The words “understanding” or “prudent” are from the verb *syniēmi*—S-U-N-I-E-M-I—from two words: *syn*, together, and *hiēmi*, H-I-E-M-I, to send or bring. It means to bring together or put together. You can check different dictionaries and confirm that.

It is used in Luke 24:45: “He opened their understanding, that they might comprehend the Scriptures.” When he says he opened their understanding, their *nous*, that they might comprehend—put together—the Scriptures, it means he has shown them different parts, or broken it down into parts and put it together.

From putting together, you are said to have understanding, and therefore you are called wise. It is by this putting together that you have wisdom. *Sophia*, wisdom, is the state you are in because understanding has been achieved. Understanding is the putting together; the state you are now in is called clarity. That is why there is no process word for wisdom: wisdom is the resultant effect of the process of understanding.

When you have gone through the process of understanding, the state you have arrived at, when you have totally put it together—*synesis*—is called wisdom. It is clear to you. You have synthesised this; therefore, it is clear to you. You have wisdom, therefore you are wise, *sophos*. You have been made wise, *sophizō*, because there has been a *syniēmi*, a putting together.

Why is this important? When Paul says the Scriptures are going to make you wise, it means the Scriptures are to be put together to the message. How *sophizō* is done is the putting together that gives you *synesis*, which also gives you *sophia*, wisdom, and makes you *sophos* or *synetos*. You have been made wise by the putting together.

The process of arriving at the message from the text is a process of putting together parts or details. We are looking at the process of Bible interpretation, and for now we have arrived at this conclusion: the process of arriving at the meaning of the text, or the message of the Scriptures entirely, is the process of putting together the Scriptures.

## The Jigsaw Puzzle: Exact Parts, Not Parts We Invent

Whenever you say you put something together, it is because that thing has definite parts, and those exact parts can be broken down or fitted back. One of the best ways to explain *syniēmi* is with a jigsaw puzzle. When you buy a jigsaw puzzle, you will probably see the image you are trying to fit together at the back, or on the carton. I saw one recently with a paper showing different numbers, so if you do not get it, you can follow the numbers for the different pieces and fit them in. You find number one and know it belongs on this side. Perhaps it is a new concept, or I missed it when I was younger.

With a jigsaw puzzle, you look at it and take the different pieces: “This one is supposed to be here.” I find the numbering interesting because it is like the work of a teacher. Normally there is strain and stress. Sometimes a jigsaw puzzle is not a one-day project. It depends on how simple it is and how much time you give to it. It might not even be a two-day project, and it can become annoying. People sometimes get frustrated and scatter it out of anger. Perhaps they assumed something belonged somewhere and realised later that they were wrong. You start questioning whether the puzzle is even correct, whether it actually fits.

It will be far more stressful doing it without instruction than doing it with instruction. If someone has told you, “This goes there; this is why this goes there,” it becomes easier. Racking your brain through it can be far more stressful.

The way we arrive at the meaning of the text, or the message of Scripture entirely, is to fit the parts together. You do not fit in what you like. There are exact parts to be fitted together. If I am speaking to you now, there are exact parts of what I am saying that you fit together to know what I mean. The way we arrive at the meaning or message of the text is by fitting together the parts of the text as they actually are—the exact parts and pieces of the text.

## Rightly Dividing: Disassembling and Reassembling Precisely

Now look at 2 Timothy 2. If something has exact parts that can be fitted together, it means there is a way those exact parts can be broken out. Let me use the example of a car. If someone wants to show you how a car is made, there are exact parts where things are joined together. Some things may be welded, so you might have to melt through them, but there are exact points where things meet, exact joints. Even if it is welded, you can still take it apart and afterwards weld it back again. I am not an expert; I am winging the example here.

If you are going to break down a car, or even a toy car, you do not just carry it and start smashing it, as you might when you are younger. If you had enough sense and the tools, you would remove it patiently and see, “This is what they put there. There is the motor. There is this part. There is the battery.” When you loosen it precisely, you can know, “There is this part that makes this thing work like this. There is another part that makes that work like that.” You then get why it works together, and you can bolt it back exactly, fit it back exactly.

If you want to explain how that car came about, you can break it down: you disassemble it and reassemble it. If someone sees you disassemble and reassemble it, the person can tell, “So that is how it came about.” You explain each part: the tyres are for this, this part is for that, and then they put it all together. That is how we have this car; that is why it works like this.

In fact, you might not know some functions of the car until they break it down, explain it to you and put it together. Perhaps it is a flying car and you do not know it has that function. But they say, “This does this; that does that.” Once they put it back together, your eyes are open: “I can do all these things with this car.”

That is what we see in 2 Timothy 2:15. “Be diligent to present yourself approved to God,” or approved of God, “a worker who does not need to be ashamed.” You are showing yourself as fit, qualified, a worker who does not need to be ashamed, “rightly dividing the word of truth.”

The word “rightly dividing” is *orthotomeō*—O-R-T-H-O-T-O-M-E-O—and it is from two words. *Orthos* means straight, or not crooked. The idea there is not that you are cutting everything in a straight line. It is the idea of being exact. You are loosening something exactly; you are disassembling it just the right way. If you are disassembling the car, you are not smashing it just to see what is inside. You are disassembling it exactly. The other word is *temnō*, to cut. You are cutting it exactly or precisely: “This is where you are supposed to loosen this place; this is where you are supposed to loosen that one.”

We can now see why Paul tells Timothy that the Scriptures are able to make him wise. We could say they are supposed to bring things together for you to the message, bring you to a state where you are wise, where you have *sophia*, where you have *synesis*. They are going to bring things together.

With the Scriptures, there must be attention to a breaking down and a putting together. There must be a disassembling and a reassembling. In our car example, if someone merely disassembles and reassembles it, you may not have understanding if the person does not say, “This is what this part means; that is what that part means; this is why it now works like this.” Use a simple example like a laptop. You see them disassemble and reassemble it, and they explain it, and you say, “So this is why it works this way.” You might know more things to do from there, but you know how to reproduce exactly that.

It is the same way with the Scriptures. You are to *orthotomeō*, and upon doing that you help people see how each part is; then you put it together. What happened to Timothy is what you are supposed to do to people. “Continue in what you have learned and been assured of, knowing from whom you learned it, and that from a child you have known the Holy Scriptures, which are able to make you wise.” Who did the rightly dividing? Paul. He was breaking it down, but did he stop at that? He also brought it together. That is why Timothy became wise. Paul put it together so that there was understanding, *synesis*, *sophia*.

The process of Bible teaching—let us start with that—is *orthotomeō* and *syniēmi*. I am using the verbs here: you break it down and put it together. If I say I want us to look at this text, the process of explaining it is that you must break it down and put it together. That is when people say, “That is the meaning.” By the time you have done that across several texts, you are now taking the texts themselves and putting them together.

Then that is the message.

## Interpretation Is a Process We Already Use

There is a process of Bible interpretation. It is not to go to the text and say what you feel like saying. Interestingly, this is not only applicable to the Scriptures. We are taking time to emphasize it, as Paul does, because of the technical or complex nature of what we are dealing with. We are dealing with matters of eternal verity, and therefore it is important that we are careful in how we approach it. But it is actually what we do every day.

Even as you are listening to me, you are piecing out the details of what I am saying and putting them together at the same time. It is happening very fast, in a way that you are taking for granted. As I say, “You are taking for granted,” you already know “you” as a part, “are” as a part, “taking” as a part. It is happening almost intuitively. “Taking” ordinarily would be a different thing, but when I say “taking for granted,” that is a phrase, almost like an idiom. If you pieced it as “taking,” “for,” “granted,” someone would be a bit lost. “I am taking that thing; I am not taking it seriously.” Automatically, your mind treats “taking for granted” almost as one word, because it is a phrase on its own.

It is happening as you listen to me, but it is happening very fast. Even with the words I used to explain that statement, it is also happening. It means you are always engaging in the process of interpretation.

If someone hears me saying, “And you are taking it for granted,” because the person does not have the context of everything we have been discussing, he will not get what I am saying. There is a context you also have as you are interpreting what I was saying about taking for granted, because you knew what we were discussing. That itself is a part of what we are talking about.

## Breaking the Bible into Books and Textual Contexts

What are the exact parts of the text that it should be broken down into, so that we can put those parts together to get the meaning of the text and the message of the Scriptures altogether? If we know that by God's orchestration we have been given the Bible, let us break the Bible down in an exact manner.

Starting with the entire book of the Bible, what would be the first breaking down? Let us forget Old and New for now. There is that division people developed; it is not bad. But the first exact break would be the books of the Bible, because they were individual books, and so they will have their own implications. From the one book called the Bible, you break it down into the books.

Since we have broken it down into books, let us focus on this: when I approach the books, what exactly am I breaking down? The first thing, when we are rightly cutting, cutting precisely, rightly disassembling the books, will be textual context. Some books are so short that you might almost argue that there is just one textual context. But even those very short books might have a salutation, and that in itself can be considered a textual context.

Many times, on the mind of the author, the author can have different textual contexts he is bringing out. First Corinthians as a book makes that clear. I think the people who did the chapter divisions helped in this regard; sometimes they got the textual context right in their chapters. Notice we are not saying that after the books we have chapters or verses. No, we have textual contexts—the contexts of texts.

You are trying to know the author's textual context. Sometimes you also have the liberty in teaching to have your own textual context that you are piecing together, without hurting the overall context of the book or a given author-intended textual context. You see the apostles and the Lord take a textual context within the overall textual context of a book to bring out an explanation or meaning that they might now piece with another one corroboratively to arrive at a submission. That can be done in Bible interpretation.

In First Corinthians, chapter 7 is definitely a textual context clearly different from chapter 8. He says, “Concerning the things you wrote to me, it is good that a man should not touch a woman.” He starts talking about marital matters. He finishes, and we know he has finished because he goes to another matter in chapter 8. He is now talking about knowledge: knowledge puffs up; love edifies. Concerning this issue of idols, do it like this.

We can argue that that textual context runs into chapter 9. We can even argue that it runs into chapter 10, because at the end of chapter 10 that same matter is being brought up as though he is concluding it. By chapter 11, there is another textual context: “I commend you that you are keeping the traditions.” He starts talking about the covering of the head. That is why he says the hair is for covering the head. That is not our issue today; that textual context has several textual contexts that we must explain to put together what the text means.

In that same chapter, he talks about another tradition he is concerned about—the Lord's Supper and everything therein, up to the end of chapter 11. By chapter 12, we clearly have another author-intended textual context: “Now concerning spirituals, I would not have you ignorant.” That continues into chapter 13 and into chapter 14, and by the end of chapter 14 you can tell he has concluded.

Chapter 15 is another textual context: “Now I declare to you the gospel which I preached to you.” Clearly, he wants to discuss another issue, not the issue of spirituals. He is talking about how this gospel includes resurrection. “What is wrong with you? After addressing all the issues people have brought up, I want to address an issue I am hearing about. How can people be saying resurrection has passed? I want to tell you about that same message I have been preaching.”

When breaking down texts, you must be aware that there are author-intended textual contexts. There can be all kinds of debate around that. But it is also the right of the reader to have a textual context he wants to examine, noting the author-intended textual context and the overall textual context of the book. If you are ever going to properly interpret Scripture, you must deal with textual context. You must do that yourself—your own breaking down of textual context, so that you can piece the meaning together.

## Historical Context Without Overstatement

Number two, when dealing with a given textual context, is to know the historical context. You do this all the time, not just with the Bible. It is a truth process of interpretation. Because Paul emphatically said it about the Scriptures, we are saying it is the process of Bible interpretation, but it is the way we function as a matter of truth in interpreting.

You know we have been talking about Bible interpretation, so when I say certain things, you know I am saying them within the context of our discussion. There is a historical context going on. Someone who just comes in may not be able to make sense of it. If you are reading the text of Scripture and see “however,” you have to take a pause. If you see “therefore,” what are the premises leading to that “therefore” conclusion? What brings up that discussion?

Be careful, because people sometimes abuse these things. To a certain degree, a material can help you with its own historical context. You do not need to overflog historical context. As touching the Bible, we have a reasonable amount of historical context that we can work with. Imagine you are reading the Epistles: you have the book of Acts.

There is some extra historical context that will help you know what happened in the Second Temple period or the intertestamental period. It will really help you to a good degree. But do not overflog it. Sometimes people take something they have just read in history and try to draw it exactly to why certain statements were being made, in a way that might stretch the meaning. There is a place for historical context, and there is work of scholarship in that regard that can be helpful. You do not draw conclusions of meaning from it alone; it can help you arrive at meaning.

## Literary Genre, Elements, and Techniques

Number three is literary genre. As you break the Bible into parts, dealing now with textual context, you identify the historical context and the literary genre. If you are reading poetry like prose, there is a problem; reading prose like poetry is also a problem. There must be that recognition going on in your mind: this is the literary genre.

The next thing, when we say we are breaking the text down into parts, is literary elements. By literary elements, I mean the literary constituents or makeup—what makes up this particular piece of literature. We are talking about themes, subject matters, characterization, and plot. Plot is a major part of this: “This is what is going on.”

This is different from the next thing, literary technique, where you are aware of certain technical literary applications going on. For example, figures of speech: you notice a technique here. Sometimes in poetry, and even in narratives like the book of Revelation, they make the same conclusion several times, which is a technique of writing. You have the literary elements—the plot and these other things—and the person has employed a particular literary technique you must be aware of.

If someone is using a metaphor, a simile, apostrophe, or personification, that is a literary technique. “Sin shall not have…” Who is sin? It is a sort of personification, even though there is a way you can read it without that and understand it. You should be aware of literary technique.

## Syntactical Structure and the Bible Student's Tools

Number six is syntactical structure. When breaking down the text, there should be a breaking down of syntactical structure. The interlinear helps us a lot here—the Greek-to-English interlinear, not the other way around. They show you the way the structure is in the Greek, relating it to the English, and you can see this syntactical structure as originally presented by the authors.

There is nothing wrong with a Bible student, a believer, engaging with Greek words, engaging with syntactical structure, consulting a concordance, a lexicon, and these other things that help. There are those who give the idea that these are the preserve of Bible scholars—those who decide to go and do a first, second, or further degree in biblical studies or related matters, perhaps historical or critical studies of the Bible. That is not true.

A lot of the work of scholarship, things like the lexicons and concordances, is intended for Bible students generally to use in their hermeneutics. They write it there and tell you not to stretch things in some cases, to be aware of certain things, but they expect you to use it. It is strange today when people try to dunk on people who are using Greek words. You can say some people are abusing it, and that is right. I will not even excuse myself; I have been guilty. There is a need to learn carefulness and your own personal limitations. But it is still very necessary and helpful for your hermeneutics.

There are things that, because of my use of Greek words in my own hermeneutics, I have been able to understand better in what Paul was saying, without necessarily having recourse to a particular scholar having to say it. I would not appreciate what Paul says about “makes you wise” if I did not have the opportunity to reference the words and see that “makes you wise,” *sophizō*, is saying you will become *sophos* and have *sophia*.

There is a parallel use. By the time you have not just the dictionary but also a concordance of the words, you see that they are related. If you did not have the concordance across the board, you would not have seen that when they say Paul also has wisdom, there is also a place where they use [related term unclear] for him. That would be a very limiting study, because you are not studying in the way the author wrote.

Of course, you do not know the language directly, but you can still bridge the extent and scope of your study by having access to that. Thinking that it is the exclusive preserve of biblical scholarship is not wise; it is not correct. I know there have been abuses where people make outlandish claims and say things off the cuff. That is not a reason to throw away the baby with the bathwater.

Going back to that attitude is like the attitude people talk about in the Dark Ages, where a few people are the ones involved in the study of Scripture. It is like you are setting up for that. All of God's Word should be available to all of God's people. What we need is faithfulness while doing it.

We have broken the Bible down into the books, and now we are looking at a book of the Bible. We deal with textual context, historical context, then literary context. There are three things in literary context: literary genre or form; literary elements, what constitutes the literature; and literary technique. Then there is syntactical structure: syntax is structure, the structure of the sentence.

## Phrases, Idioms, and Individual Words

Number seven is phrases and idioms. “He kicked the bucket.” You cannot break that into its individual parts when they mean something idiomatic. Phrases and idioms can come up, like when I said “taking for granted.” You would not think there was a physical thing being taken. You have to understand that term.

There are times when, beyond understanding words and checking words, you need to understand a phrase. Some people argue that “weeping and gnashing of teeth” can be taken as a phrase on its own to be explained—not just the simple idea of weeping and gnashing teeth, but reflecting regret. I do not want to teach assertively on that; I am just using it as an example. In that case, you can say the thought is to express regret.

There are other times when you are reading something that is a phrase of its own and you must be aware of that. What you should be aware of is not just the different words. “Second death” is one thing: destruction, damnation. If you say it is dying the second time, you are missing a phrase. In breaking down the text, you must be aware that what you are supposed to break down is a phrase, not its individual words.

There is also a place for words. That is number eight: dealing with words and knowing what those words mean. Those are the things we break down, up to number eight. Number nine in Bible interpretation is analytical conclusion. When you have broken down all the parts and pieces and explained them, there is now the need to put them together.

## Measured Judgments and the Degree of Certainty

I should have mentioned under syntactical structure that this can be very important. There are all kinds of debates today about whether the syntax expressed in a translation is exactly the syntax intended by the author. These things are debatable; you find translations that have different opinions.

First John 5:14–15 says, “If we ask anything according to his will, he hears us.” There is a way to understand the sentence as meaning, “If you ask anything that is according to his will, he hears us.” Others argue that it should be understood, “If you ask anything, according to his will he hears us”—that it is his will to hear us. You can see how syntax can change meaning.

Whatever you think the syntax is, this is where you need to bring in other things. In analytical conclusion, there is a place for deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning. What can it not be, based on everything else he has been saying? What are the options for what he is saying? Which one is most probably right? There are times when, in a text, you cannot have an absolute answer. Do not overstate texts.

This is a general principle in reasoning: in drawing conclusions, you must be aware of five measures of judgment you can give. Not every judgment is certain. It is certain in Scripture that there was Jesus. Some other things you read in Scripture are debatable: they are probable or plausible.

The measures of judgment move from impossible to possible. Something can be possible but not plausible. It could have happened, but is it really likely that this is what happened? Let me give an example, without making a judgment or creating a basis for controversy at this level. When they spoke about Herod being eaten up by worms, there are other historical documents that affirm that he became sick. Someone can say that at that moment [unclear] started eating him up immediately and he died that day. It is possible. Someone might argue from a natural standpoint that it is not possible, but a supernatural thing can happen.

With historical context, you might argue that another reading is most probable: that there is a connection, perhaps known by revelation, between the general way he had been walking in the flesh and his becoming subject to that negative situation. We would need the truth on morality and those other things to start saying that it is God's judgment but can still be a consequence. Let us leave controversial matters; some of these things are debatable. From your standpoint, looking at many things inductively and deductively, you might say it is more probable that it is demonic, but you cannot say it with all certainty. Let us leave it there.

Judgment goes through impossible, possible, plausible, probable, and certain. When you say something is probable, you are not just saying it is plausible. Among the plausible options, this is what is most probable. Lastly, you have certain: there is no question that this is the case.

Always make measured judgments, even when you are saying that the meaning of words is this, or the historical context is this, to the degree of what is available. For example, you go to First Timothy 2 about women and men and say it is because of Diana and this and that. You can say it is most probable. You cannot say it is certainly that; all kinds of arguments are made on that text. You cannot impose a given historical context when you do not have all the arguments for it.

There is also First Corinthians 14, in Corinth, about women not speaking. Some people try to impose a historical context when there is no certainty of the connection. You can say it is plausible or probable that this is the case. Looking at the text, we can still talk about abductive reasoning concerning what can and cannot be, based on everything else we know from the theology of the apostles.

Let your judgment be measured. You should be able to know the literary elements to a very good degree—the characters, the plot, and the literary techniques. Do not overstate literary techniques either. Do not assume what is not there, so that everything becomes allegory. Say things in a measured manner.

## Analytical Conclusion: Putting the Parts Together Logically

Number nine is analytical conclusion: the logical putting together of each part to arrive at the meaning of the text. Seeing that the words mean this and a particular phrase means this, you are aware that words and phrases have their implications, and you put them together. In the syntax, this happens to be the subject and this the object; you are not confusing those things.

Do not make certain claims that might get you into trouble, such as saying that the original writings of the apostles lack clear pronouns or the singularity or plurality of pronouns. That is not true. In the original rendition in their language, whether Greek or Hebrew, you can tell the pronouns and their singularity or plurality. Those things inflect the words and verbs, and sometimes the nouns.

Looking at a base noun in a concordance or in something like MySword does not show you the inflected form. You do not want to make a claim, based on what you saw in a concordance, about how it is as they actually wrote it. The inflected form can tell you whether something is past, present, or future. It can tell you the plurality or singularity of the subject, sometimes the object; you can know whether a noun is plural. It is there. That is not the translator's decision. Let us be aware of those details.

Analytical conclusion is the logical putting together of each part to arrive at the meaning of the text. There are two things here. There is synthesis—you are putting it together—and there is conclusion: by deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning you arrive at what the meaning would be in this case.

With some texts, by the time you synthesize the parts you have broken down, the meaning is immediately clear. What does it mean to “jump the gun”? In a race, someone has gone off before the gun; he has jumped the gun. Suppose someone shoots at a person and the person jumps, and someone says, “That guy jumped the gun.” They can look close, but they are not the same.

There are statements that, when you put their parts together, are enough to give you the meaning. You synthesize all the parts and can say, “Definitely, this is what it means.” But there are times when, in putting them together, you have options. You can induce several options and then abduct from all the induced options.

In analytical conclusion there is synthesis. You put it together, and sometimes, almost immediately, like a deductive issue, it is clear. Sometimes you have options inductively: it can be this, this, or this in the given context. Then abductively you say it is most probably this. That is okay. Many things will be certain immediately. In difficult cases, having synthesized the text, you may have to look at all the inductive possibilities and then abductively find the most probable option.

That is your job as an interpreter functioning with good reasoning. You cannot do more than that. It is like someone judging a case: it is what they bring to you that you have to decide on. Some things become deductively clear by everything they have put before you. With other things, you induce all the possibilities and abduct the most probable. You know which one you are resting on as certain and which as most probable. Sometimes you may just say all the options are equally plausible.

This is why I talked about logical reasoning, the three kinds of reasoning, and the weight of judgments to give on those reasonings. If something is not deductive, it might not have the level of certainty you need. You might have probability or plausibility. In some far-off issues in apologetics, where people are trying to [unclear], the case at best is just possible. Sometimes these are also nonissues and not worth the fuss.

## Corroboration, Contradictions, and Subject-Matter Truth

Number ten is addressing corroboration and contradictions. On a given text, after you have established its meaning, consider, for example, a text about the glorification of our body. People sometimes say that quickening our mortal body in Romans 8:11 is about the Holy Ghost healing us today. It is not that the Holy Ghost does not heal us today. The Holy Ghost is in us, and by faith we can put that power to work in our body, therefore quickening our body in the sense of healing. But Romans 8:11 was about the glorification of our body. The context is abundantly clear about what it is addressing at that moment.

After you have explained that, you can say, “To show you that this is the case, see the role of the Holy Ghost explained there also as that.” You are examining corroborations. Someone might want to make a contradiction: “Is quickening not used in another way in this place that pertains to what I am arguing for?” You have to deal with that seeming contradiction. I do not think there is any strong text against that in the Bible; quickening is mostly about making alive.

You use words there as a part of the process: “The word is this; the syntax is this; the idiom or phrase is this and means this.” You have broken down those parts and now put them together, synthesizing them in your analytical conclusion and determining the meaning. Then you address corroboration and contradiction. You show other texts talking about the glorification of the body and deal with seeming contradictions about why that text means nothing else but the glorification of the body.

When you do that, what typically happens is that you arrive at subject-matter truth. From examining a text and then addressing the corroborations and contradictions pertaining to that text, you can say, “There is such a thing as the glorification of the body.” That also leads you, as you examine other texts and their subject matters, to what we might call wholesome biblical truth.

I have mentioned ten points for the process of interpretation. You break down the parts: textual context, historical context, literary genre, literary elements, literary techniques, syntactical structure, phrases and idioms, and words. Then there is analytical conclusion, followed by corroboration and contradiction. With that you are good.

But note these two things: when you do that with a text, from one to ten, you usually arrive at subject-matter truth. When you do that across different subject matters, different texts and their corroborating texts, you typically arrive at wholesome biblical truth.

## From Faithful Interpretation to Practical Theology

If we faithfully follow the process of Bible interpretation, what we will have is biblical theology, together with philosophical theology and historical theology, that produces thorough systematic theology, which guides our practical theology.

We are not going to ignore the role of philosophical theology or historical theology. Historical theology would also look at things other people have said throughout the history of the church, not just find out the history of the text itself and its context. We have biblical theology with philosophical theology and historical theology, producing thorough systematic theology that guides our practical theology.

## Primarily Intuitive, Secondarily Intentional

The process of Bible interpretation is primarily intuitive and secondarily intentional. This is important, because nobody is asking you to pause at every juncture in your Bible and say, “What is the textual context? What is the historical context? What is the literary genre?” You do not even do that in your conversations, yet you are following the same process of interpretation.

When he says, “When you read, you understand,” it is that you are aware of that process ordinarily. There are times, because of the nature of a text, when you have to be intentional. Beyond what you are doing intuitively, you have to slow down and be intentional. In the intuitive process, you are being attentive as you read your Bible; by intentional, I mean that you now have to slow down and break things down.

There are texts where you pause and break down the text. You look at the syntactical structure and the words. You want to know whether there are certain phrases. Sometimes you might almost use syllogism or symbolic logic. You draw X here and say, “This is the subject, X; this is Y.” You might take an entire phrase and make it one symbol.

For example, Hebrews 6 says it is impossible for those who were once enlightened, and so on. You might draw a line around some of those things and make them one thing. Then there is “and they fall away”; you can make that another thing. You now argue whether that is supposed to be part of the first thing or not. You break it out like that so that by the time you are done, you can be confident in the conclusion you have arrived at.

You will meet texts where you cannot simply be intuitive with the process of interpretation. You have to slow down and be very deliberate and intentional about Bible interpretation in those cases.

There is a definite process of Bible interpretation. It is truth-determined and Scripture-instructed. Everything we have said is what happens normally as a matter of truth, yet it is Scripture-instructed: Second Timothy 2:15 and Second Timothy 3:15. Today it is necessary for us, so that we can faithfully arrive at the meaning of texts and the message of Scripture that makes for our growth and walk in God's purpose.
