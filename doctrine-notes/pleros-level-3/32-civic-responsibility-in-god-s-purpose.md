# Civic Responsibility in God's Purpose

## Citizenship and Responsibility in an Organized Community

Our Father, I am so glad that I am your child and a part of your family. In this meeting, gathered with my siblings, I receive of your word light, truth, revelation and growth by the knowledge of Christ and his work in all its fullness, and by the knowledge of him and his fullness that is in me, in the name of the Lord Jesus. Amen.

I want us to look at civic responsibility in God's purpose. This is an important part of our understanding of God's purpose and our walk with God as a whole on earth. We need to define or explain what we mean by civic responsibility, so that people do not get lost. Sometimes, when you say civic responsibility, people think you are talking about politics and governance. No, civic responsibility has to do with the responsibility you have as a citizen.

You are a citizen. You belong to a city. That is another way of saying it. Let me show you that citizen is not a strange word to Scripture. Ephesians 2 uses it to describe our participation in God's own city, God's own nation, God's own country. When they borrowed that word, they borrowed it from ordinary secular usage and applied it to the spiritual community called the church.

Ephesians 2:19 says, “Now therefore, you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens.” That is sympolitēs. I hope I am saying it correctly. You are a fellow citizen. Politēs is citizen; you are part of the polis. Polis is the word for city. Politics itself takes its root from that language of citizen, citizenship or the city.

God's own community, the church, is described as the city of God. Hebrews 12:22 says we have come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem and an innumerable company of angels. In this city there are angels. In this city there is the church. In this city there are the spirits of just men made perfect. God has his own city.

But today, with the way we speak about a city, if you saw God's city you probably would not call it a city. You might call it a nation. You might even call it a community of nations. Yet we see the word city used to describe it. So when we say city and citizen, you should be aware that this can mean a global citizen or a local citizen.

The possibility in the word is membership in a community organized together, an organized community. If they use city for a spiritual community, they are borrowing that usage from the ordinary secular sense. That is why we also see city used in an ordinary sense throughout Scripture.

For example, you see the word Decapolis. After Jesus healed the man of Gadara, he went and testified in Decapolis, the ten cities. So before we even discuss everything entailed in civic responsibility, the concepts of city, citizenship and civic responsibility are not strange to Scripture.

In this teaching, however, we are not speaking about responsibility as a citizen in God's spiritual community. We are speaking about responsibility as a citizen in the natural community which God also created. There is civic responsibility in God's purpose.

When we went to school, we had government in senior secondary school and social studies in junior secondary school. Now there is civic education, teaching people about their roles as citizens. But God's word itself teaches civic responsibility. That means there is civic education in God's word.

## Civic Responsibility and Walking in Righteousness

The most popular, direct passage is Romans 13, where Paul addresses our participation. The interesting thing is that he focuses on one of what I will argue are five parts of our civic responsibility. He focuses on complying with the governing code. We might say the code we have agreed on, although in their case it may not have been agreed upon. It was the existing code, imposed and in place, and they were to comply.

Give honour to whom it is due. Give each person what is due. Ensure you owe no one anything except to love one another. That is the concluding part, and it is interesting. After saying we should owe no one anything except love, he explains that love is the fulfilment of the law. The earlier things he said about civic responsibility play into fulfilling the law as we walk in love and righteousness.

You cannot walk with God in righteousness and refuse civic responsibility. You cannot walk in love, fulfilling the law which teaches God's truth, without participating in and fulfilling your civic responsibility. There is such a thing as civic responsibility addressed in God's word. That is the first thing established.

What we are going to do is give civic responsibility its rightful context. Was Paul simply saying, “We already exist in organized communities, so let me tell them something about complying with those communities”? Was that his standpoint, or is there a fuller picture of civic responsibility in God's word and purpose?

## God's Revealed Purpose Is Sonship

To establish that civic responsibility belongs to God's purpose, we have to start with what his purpose is altogether. The most direct passage explaining God's purpose is Ephesians 1. Verse 9 says, “Having made known to us the mystery of his will.” God's will, what he wants, has been made known to us.

You can never find out someone's will except that person reveals it. Someone's will is on the person's heart. It is not discernible by observation and reason; you can only know it by revelation. God has made known the mystery of his will, according to his good pleasure which he purposed in himself.

His will, what he wants, accords with his good pleasure, what he likes. That is what he purposed in himself. If you ask, “What is God's purpose?” the first answer is that God's purpose is also his will and good pleasure. To find out what that purpose is, we need to find out what his will and good pleasure are.

Ephesians 1 is telling us that what he purposed in himself is what he wants and likes. What is that will and good pleasure which he purposed to have? Verse 5 is where Paul explains it: “Having predestined us to adoption as sons by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will.”

He is speaking of those who have faith in Christ, because that is the us in the context. He has predestined those who have faith in Christ to adoption as sons. We believe in Christ and in the redemption he has done, and it is by that redemption that we come to be who we are: people of faith in redemption.

In one sense, what God predestined is redemption. Those of us who have now believed are part of that. We can bring the thought down to this: he predestined redemption to sonship, because the us refers to those who are redeemed. He predestined the redeemed to adoption as sons. In the Greek, that is one word, huiothesia. He predestined us to sonship.

“Having predestined us to sonship by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will.” One way to read verse 5 is to start at the end. According to the good pleasure of his will, he predestined us to sonship. That shows what Paul is saying: because this is the good pleasure of his will, he predestined us to it.

Based on the fact that this is the good pleasure of his will, he predestined you to it. God's purpose, therefore, is sonship. We asked what that thing is which God likes and wants, which he purposed to have. Ephesians 1:5 shows that it is sonship. His will and good pleasure are what he purposed in himself. Why did God make you? Sonship.

## The Spirit's Witness and Our Sonship

What does sonship mean? If I exist because God wanted sonship, which he also reflected with the Israelites in Romans 9:4, what exactly does it mean? Romans 8 gives the clearest explanation.

Verse 15 says, “You did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, ‘Abba, Father.’” Adoption there is huiothesia, the exact same word rendered adoption as sons in Ephesians 1:5. We could simply say that we received the Spirit of sonship, by whom we cry, “Abba, Father.”

We did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but the Spirit of sonship. In this passage Paul explains what this purpose of God means. The explanation comes in the next verse: “The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God.”

Which Spirit? The Spirit of sonship we received. The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit, symmartyreō. The two give the same testimony. Bearing witness is very similar to what happens in court. You call someone as a witness, and the person gives a witness: “I was there when it happened. I was hiding. I was in this place, and I saw it happen with my eyes.”

Or, “I work in that place. The day it happened, I was there. I saw the person walk through the door.” You are saying the person is an eyewitness, and the person gives that witness.

Sometimes the witness brought to court is not a human being. You bring forensic evidence. Suppose there was blood at the crime scene, or strands of hair, or saliva. You bring it, and they examine it. There are different things they can use. The evidence is said to have come from the scene; if the police attest that they took it from that scene, the court is bound to believe that is the case.

Then they conduct a forensic examination and find that it belongs to a particular person. There is almost no way that person can excuse himself from the evidence that he was there. The blood, hair or saliva gives evidence of his presence. The blood is not speaking literally, but it is giving evidence.

When Paul says the Spirit gives evidence with our spirit, he means that if the two are put to the test, they give evidence that we are children of God. If your DNA is tested, it gives evidence that you are your parents' child. For some of us, it is already obvious. I do not even need a DNA test to show that both my parents gave birth to me. The resemblance to both is clear, and the same is true of many of us.

If the relevant evidence from two people is tested, the common source can be established. God's Spirit has done something to our spirit, making us one with himself, so that when his Spirit and our spirit are put to the test, both give the same evidence. They have the same source.

The Spirit bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God. That is the first testimony or evidence. Think of a blood test again. Different realities can emerge from that test. They may identify your genotype, which is one reality about you. They may identify your blood group, another reality. They may find that you are low in iron or something else.

When we put the Spirit to the test, what realities are seen? First, that we are children of God. But it does not stop there. Read from verse 16 into verse 17. We are looking at civic responsibility in God's purpose; his purpose is sonship, and we are now seeing its realities. The Spirit bears witness that we are children of God, “and if children, then heirs: heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ.”

## Joint Inheritance and Individual Assignments

In this case, the inheritance is not individual at all, because he says we are joint heirs. We are heirs of God jointly. In property law, they teach about joint inheritance and inheritance which is given separately. Someone can give an inheritance jointly to a number of people, so that no one individual owns it. They own it collectively.

They may collectively choose to sell it and divide the proceeds, but no one person can simply act upon the whole property however he wishes. It is a joint inheritance. That is different from giving different things to each individual.

It is also true that God gives us individual portions. We have seen that in our natural and spiritual assignments, where each person has a portion or a lot. But there is also a joint inheritance in God's purpose. We are heirs of God, not only in a sense that can be spoken of individually, but also as joint heirs with Christ.

What do we jointly own with Christ? What are we joint heirs of? What does God own which he gives us collectively to own? Paul says we are joint heirs with Christ, if indeed we suffer together so that we may be glorified together. Then verse 18 speaks of the sufferings of the present time not being worthy of comparison with the glory to be revealed in us. The next statement is about the earnest expectation of creation.

What is Paul doing? God created this world, and there is a hope for what he created on account of those who now own it. If God is the owner of creation and we are his joint heirs, then what we inherit jointly is what he owns: creation.

The earnest expectation of creation waits for the revealing or manifestation of the sons of God. When we say we are joint heirs, we are joint heirs of the world, the cosmos, creation. You can use any of those descriptions.

Romans 4:13 gives another text. Speaking of Abraham in the light of God's promise of redemption, it says the promise that he would be heir of the world was not to Abraham and his seed through the law, but through the righteousness of faith. World there is kosmos. We are heirs of the world.

Matthew 5:5 says, “Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.” We are joint heirs of creation, what God owns and has given to humanity, to those who are his own. What is the inheritance of those who are of faith? The earth.

The meek here describes people of faith. Remember that the Beatitudes describe those of saving faith and their works. The poor in spirit realize that they need salvation, that something is wrong with them spiritually. Those who hunger and thirst realize that something is lacking, so they seek it. The meek realize that they have nothing to boast in themselves and need help.

The Beatitudes describe people of saving faith and their works. “Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.” What God has called us to own is creation, the cosmos, the earth. When you say, “I am an heir of God,” what do you own? The world. An heir of God is an heir of the earth, creation, the cosmos.

## Creation's Deliverance on Account of the Heirs

Go back to Romans 8. One of the things Paul is doing there is pointing to our responsibility as joint heirs with Christ. Our eventual responsibility as heirs of God in this age is to change this world into a righteous one. I said eventual. Our eventual responsibility concerns its change from this state of corruption into an incorruptible state.

Immediately after saying we are heirs of God, Paul speaks about change in creation. Verse 19 says the earnest expectation of creation eagerly waits for the revealing of the sons of God. Creation reflects a gap in itself: “I could be more than this. This is not my best state. This is not the state I am supposed to be in.”

Verse 20 says creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it in hope. Creation itself will be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of God's children. We can argue from Romans that its subjection to futility and corruption resulted from man's rejection of God's purpose. It was left in that state, but there is hope of deliverance.

Who, in the sense Paul is discussing, is responsible for the change which is to happen to the cosmos? The sons of God. But it is not that we are the ones who will it into happening. It is on our account that it happens. It happens at the end when the Lord comes. The Lord himself wills it; it is at his initiative that creation changes from corruption into incorruption.

Yet it is on account of those who own it. That we are of righteousness means creation is to change according to righteousness. What is righteousness? Primarily, righteousness is life. The cosmos is to come out of corruption, which is a state of death, into incorruption. It is supposed to be in a state of life, and that happens on our account.

If we own the cosmos and are now of life, creation will not continue along the way of death to which it was initially subjected. Who was responsible for its corruption? Man. Man was given responsibility over it and put it in a state of death.

By redemption, the right over the cosmos now belongs to the man in life, the man in Christ. It belongs to Christ and those who are joint heirs with him. On their account it will come out of its present state into a righteous state.

## The Present Kingdom and Future Incorruption

What have we identified? In God's purpose, we are children of God and heirs of God. As heirs, we have individual inheritances and a collective inheritance. Individually, God has allotted you a natural assignment and a spiritual assignment. There is work of ministry for you to do and a natural assignment to fulfil. Aside from those portions, we all have the joint inheritance of the cosmos.

Our clearest, most complete responsibility towards the cosmos is what is fulfilled at the end: its change from corruption into incorruption. But in this age, relative to the world, we also have civic responsibility as we participate in ordering it after righteousness.

Second Peter 3:10 says the day of the Lord will come as a thief in the night, the heavens will pass away with a great noise, the elements will melt with fervent heat, and the earth and its works will be burned up. Since these things will be dissolved, what manner of people ought we to be in holy conduct and godliness, looking for the coming of the day of God?

Verse 13 says, “Nevertheless we, according to his promise, look for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells.” The present heavens and earth are a world in which unrighteousness or death dwells. Through man, sin entered the world, and death through sin. Death entered the cosmos. We now seek a world in which righteousness dwells, in which life dwells, a world of incorruption.

First Corinthians 15:50 says, “Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God; nor does corruption inherit incorruption.” By kingdom of God, he is speaking about the kingdom in glory. There is a sense in which we can speak of the kingdom as present. Colossians 1:13 says he has delivered us from the authority of darkness and translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love. We have already entered in that sense.

But there is another sense in which the kingdom is future. When Paul says flesh and blood cannot inherit it, he is evidently speaking about that future reality. You cannot be part of an incorruptible world while you have corruption. Flesh and blood there refers to mortality or corruption. What is the state of the kingdom in glory, fulfilment or consummation? Incorruption.

“Behold, I tell you a mystery: We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed,” in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. The trumpet will sound, the dead will be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. This corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality.

It happens on account of the sons of God. We have a responsibility towards the entire cosmos. Our eventual, joint responsibility concerns its movement from corruption into incorruption, from death into life. Does that responsibility exist only at the end, or do we have responsibility towards the cosmos in the present? If we do, then we have civic responsibility now.

## The Present Work of Salt and Light

Matthew 5:5 says, “Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.” The earth will belong to them; it will be their right. At the end it will be transformed into incorruption. They inherit the earth, the cosmos, the world, creation.

Do they have a responsibility towards that world in the present? Yes. The earth they will inherit is the same earth of which they are called the salt. In the same conversation, Matthew 5:13 says, “You are the salt of the earth.” He speaks about its flavour and its preservative role. Presently, you are the salt of the earth.

Our work towards the earth is not ultimately just preservation. It concerns the complete transformation of the earth into an incorruptible state. But he says that presently we have a preservative role. “If the salt loses its flavour, how shall it be seasoned? It is then good for nothing but to be thrown out and trampled underfoot by men.”

The next statement is, “You are the light of the world. A city that is set on a hill cannot be hidden.” People do not light a lamp and put it under a basket, but on a lampstand, and it gives light to everyone in the house.

You might make the mistake of thinking he is talking only about preaching the gospel. He is not. Someone says, “Being the light of the world only means sharing the gospel.” That is not true, because the statement immediately following is, “Let your light so shine before men.” Does it say, “That they may hear the gospel”? No. It says, “That they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven.”

On one hand, that concerns moral responsibility. But it is not only moral responsibility. It also concerns civic responsibility. People see your conduct and recognize that there is something good about the way you live. They commend it, and God is glorified. That includes civic responsibility, not merely an individual moral life.

## Good Conduct, Suffering and Civic Responsibility

First Peter 2 addresses servants. The idea can be applied to employees in their workplaces. “Be submissive to your masters with all fear, not only to the good and gentle, but also to the harsh.” If you can change your job, change your job.

“For this is commendable, if because of conscience towards God one endures grief, suffering wrongfully.” Verse 20 asks, “What credit is it if, when you are beaten for your faults, you take it patiently? But when you do good and suffer, if you take it patiently, this is commendable before God.” To this we were called, because Christ suffered for us, leaving an example that we should follow his steps.

Now look at 1 Peter 3. These passages are connected. Begin at verse 8: “All of you be of one mind, having compassion for one another; love as brothers, be tenderhearted, be courteous; not returning evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary blessing, knowing that you were called to this, that you may inherit a blessing.” Then he quotes Scripture from verses 10 through 12.

Verse 13 asks, “Who is he who will harm you if you become followers of what is good?” Even if you suffer for righteousness' sake, you are blessed. Do not be afraid of their threats or troubled. “Sanctify the Lord God in your hearts, and always be ready to give a defence to everyone who asks you a reason for the hope that is in you, with meekness and fear.”

Have a good conscience, so that when they defame you as evildoers, those who revile your good conduct in Christ may be ashamed. They know that you are actually living rightly. “For it is better, if it is the will of God, to suffer for doing good than for doing evil.”

Understand the kind of community they were in. Today you have certain rights which you should exercise. Do not let people make you suffer in vain. But in both passages, we are called to be examples. Even those who will not admit it are ashamed in their hearts, because they know the truth.

You are supposed to be an example of living rightly, even when it means suffering. People should see that example. Part of what leads up to this is in 1 Peter 2:13, and it concerns what we do as citizens: “Submit yourselves to every ordinance of man for the Lord's sake.”

You have a godly reason, a biblical reason, for submitting to human ordinances and authorities. We will see that reason when we consider God's design at the beginning. “Whether to the king as supreme, or to governors, as to those who are sent by him for the punishment of evildoers and for the praise of those who do good.”

In this instance he teaches compliance with governing authority, one part of civic responsibility. It may seem to be the most emphasized part, but across Scripture we find that it is not the only part. They were addressing what was available to them at the time.

“For this is the will of God, that by doing good you may put to silence the ignorance of foolish men.” Is that part of being the light of the world? When you do good in civic responsibility, you silence the ignorance of foolish people.

One concern while they were suffering persecution was that people should not be able to say they were revolutionaries. There should be no ground for accusing them of doing something wrong. Nevertheless, the principle holds true beyond that situation. When we are faithful in civic responsibility, in the sense of compliance here, we are examples to the world.

Being the light of the world and salt of the earth is not just moral responsibility. We have moral responsibility, certainly, but aside from that there is civic responsibility in God's purpose. It is part of our example in this world.

We are free, yet must not use liberty as a cloak for vice. We are bondservants of God. Verse 17 continues, “Honour all people. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honour the king.” Is civic responsibility part of being salt and light? Yes. Is it part of the good works people see and through which they glorify our Father? Yes. They recognize something about what we believe through that conduct.

## Responsibility at Work and the Light of the World

Your attitude in your natural assignment is part of this too. That is why Peter moves to the conduct of servants. Moral responsibility, civic responsibility and responsibility at work all belong to being salt and light. Civic responsibility means your participation as a citizen in any organized community, and work responsibility includes your conduct as either master or servant.

There is a way to conduct yourself morally that brings shame or reproach to God, and a way that brings him glory. The same applies to your work and your conduct as a citizen. When Jesus speaks of salt and light, it is not only moral conduct; civic responsibility and natural assignment are involved.

I personally do not think spiritual assignment is in that particular salt-and-light statement at all. I do not think he is speaking about ministry there. Salt is preservative: things remain bad, but you preserve. The world is dark, but you are a light in that dark space. It is like a bulb at night. You have not removed night itself, but the light is on in that place.

There is ministry, and he has spoken earlier about persecution for his name's sake. But here it seems he is speaking about moral responsibility, your morality preserving something in a decaying, corrupt world, together with civic responsibility and what you do in natural assignment.

First Timothy 6:1–2 says those under the yoke of service should count their masters worthy of all honour, so that God's name and doctrine are not blasphemed. Glorifying your Father includes your natural assignment. Those with believing masters should not despise them because they are brethren. Do not treat them as common, thinking, “We are both believers; there is nothing to it.” Rather, serve them because those receiving the benefit are believers and beloved. Teach and exhort these things.

Being light in the world includes civic responsibility and work, so that Christ's name is not blamed. Civic responsibility belongs to God's will. First Peter 2:15 says it is God's will that by doing good we silence the ignorance of foolish people.

## A Preservative Role Rather Than Coercive Takeover

I believe civic responsibility belongs to God's will and purpose from the beginning. Today we are discussing a preservative role. This is civic responsibility in redemption, because something happened to civic responsibility under sin. We must first consider the original expectation and then understand what redemption means for our present participation.

We need to be comfortable with the fact that this is civic responsibility in redemption. You cannot try to take over. That itself becomes a problem; you can be functioning in sin by doing that. But it is also wrong to become lethargic and do nothing. We must have a preservative attitude.

If you try to take over, you provoke the flesh in yourself and other people: “We must take over the spaces; now we must make the policies.” You need to understand your actual role in policymaking. Even when you hold office, if you do not understand that you must make negotiated policies rather than simply impose your own kind of policies, you do not understand your role.

Suppose you govern Nigeria and decide to make a law banning homosexuality. You can preach people out of homosexuality, but you cannot use governing authority to control people's private morality. Stealing, murder and other matters affecting public order are a different issue. People's private morality cannot simply be controlled by government authority. Even if you tried, would it work? Would they not still do it, with sin increasing? That is something people are to be preached out of.

I am not saying your public position must now be that you support it. That is not the issue. You can make clear that it is not something you believe in. But the redemptive role we have in civic responsibility involves negotiation.

The word I will use is compromise. You must make a compromise in the code. I am not saying that you personally should compromise. I am saying the code with which you function will be compromised. If you refuse a compromised code, you will have a seemingly righteous code enforced unrighteously. If you refuse negotiation, you will go all the way into unrighteousness.

Do not cut this out of context and make it say that we should encourage wrongdoing. That is not what I am saying.

## Public Order, Private Morality and Difficult Judgments

Some matters are worth debating as matters beyond private morality. For example, I think taking life, including life in the womb, belongs in that discussion. We have to find what is acceptable, but the general rule cannot be that people freely end life.

There can be ethical dilemmas in particular situations. People create examples: suppose you are driving a bus full of schoolchildren, a trailer is approaching head-on on the wrong side, and the only alternative is to hit one or two people on the roadside. You either deliberately hit those people, or continue into the collision with the children on board. Sometimes the question is first asked about driving alone, then about driving with others. The answer may not be the same.

You find out what is acceptable to the Lord, as Ephesians 5:10 says. You use moral truth and moral intention to make a moral judgment: how can I further life here? Different people may reach different judgments. You may still be judged for poor judgment, but what first matters is that your intention was to preserve life, whichever way you were thinking at that moment.

Let us leave that one. These are very tricky places; they are edge cases.

## Responsibility Begins in the Local Community

Is civic responsibility something that arose later merely so that we could be exemplary? No. It belongs to the beginning. When we say that God's purpose makes us joint heirs of the world, civic responsibility is already embedded in that.

Remember what we mean: participation as a citizen of any organized community. I do not want to limit it to a city, because it extends beyond that. Your organized community may be your compound, your area, your estate or your local government. You are a citizen of that community.

Suppose an estate meeting is arranged on a day when you have a church meeting. You cannot simply say, “To hell with the estate meeting.” God's purpose does not expect that from you. You belong to that community. If you need to be absent, explain and find how to participate: “Can I receive the briefing afterwards and find out my share of the responsibility?”

We used to have church meetings on Saturdays, and the community knew that. There were contributions and responsibilities we still had to meet. You do not just ignore what is happening. That is not proper. You remain a participant.

Imagine your community is arranging security, and you are not involved. You say, “This world just needs to be saved.” You are not being serious. Civic responsibility belongs to God's purpose too.

## Reading Dominion Wholesomely

Genesis 1:26 is read in different ways when it says God gave man dominion over the earth and everything in it. I think the best way to read it is wholesomely. One of the hardest things about being human, and particularly being a Christian, is being wholesome. There seems to be a tendency to move to one extreme or the other.

If people speak about discipline and drive, it can be to the detriment of love. If they speak about love, it can be to the detriment of discipline. But you cannot walk in God's purpose without discipline, and you must still walk in love.

You need to be driven, and you may need to drive people in spiritual or natural assignment. Yet you must not become mean, abusive or insensitive. You must not stop caring about people's health or other realities. You have to be disciplined and driven while remaining loving. That applies personally and in leadership. Being wholesome is difficult, but it is necessary.

My primary leaning in Genesis 1:26–28 is towards spiritual assignment. When it says, “Be fruitful and multiply; fill the earth and subdue it,” I always like to read the text from a spiritual-assignment standpoint, like the Great Commission given on the sixth day. Yet I am aware that it can also be read in relation to natural assignment: God gives dominion, and man tills the ground. You can see natural assignment and spiritual assignment, the garden and God's purpose, and the role of priesthood.

When it says God gave man the earth, it can also be read in relation to civic responsibility. That is a major part of dominion over the earth. You are responsible for organizing the earth.

“Let us make man in our image, according to our likeness; let them have dominion.” Notice the intention. The man made in God's image and likeness is the man to have dominion. The way he orders the world, the dominion he exercises, is to be according to God's nature and image.

This depends on your understanding of image and likeness. We can accept a reading of image which concerns all humanity with the intention of being in God's image, where image also concerns nature. Man was made from the beginning to order the world, not merely in the natural-assignment sense of subduing its elements, but also in ordering communities after God's life.

When God called man to receive his life, as we see in Genesis 2:15, that dominion involved the way humanity would order and arrange the world. Civic responsibility is part of what was given to humanity.

## The Earth Given to Humanity

Psalm 115:16 gives us another way to see this: “The heaven, even the heavens, are the Lord's; but the earth he has given to the children of men.” In that sense, the heavens are the spiritual space, while the earth is given to humanity.

There are other passages which say the earth belongs to God: “The earth is the Lord's, and all its fullness, the world and those who dwell therein.” It belongs to him, but he gives it to the children of men. At the beginning, God gave humanity the world to manage.

Someone can hear that and think of natural assignment; another can think of civic responsibility. Both are right. That gives context to Jesus' central statement about civic responsibility: give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and to God what belongs to God.

Man is responsible for civic matters. Whoever is responsible in the system human beings have established, give what is due under that arrangement. That is consistent with what God has already done in giving humanity the responsibility.

You can also argue for a deeper meaning from the question of image. Jesus asks whose image is on the coin. It is Caesar's, so give Caesar his image. Whose image are we, humanity or the new creation? God's image. Give your whole self to God.

There are things you give in civic responsibility, but in doing so your whole being is given to God. It was a perfect answer to those trying to trap him. You give yourself wholly to God, and under him you still give what belongs to civic matters to the people in charge. You fulfil your responsibility in the community under God.

## The Rights Restored Through Redemption

We have established that God's purpose includes civic responsibility. At the beginning, God gave humanity responsibility over the earth, the cosmos. Why are believers now called heirs of the world? Humanity rendered it subject to death. But the Lord Jesus has paid the full consequence of death, so the right over creation now belongs to him and legitimately to every other person of faith.

Death has been allowed to run its course and lose its hold. Rightfully, the cosmos now belongs to life, to God, to the redeeming God. Notice, however, that this belonging to God is also a belonging to man, God become man, because the cosmos was given to man. It belongs to Christ, and we are joint heirs with him.

People in life, people in Christ, now own the cosmos. It was originally God's property given to man. Man rendered it subject to death. Redemption allows death to take its course and lose its authority, and the cosmos now belongs to Christ, the one who redeemed it and earned its rights, and to all who are in him. That is why we are heirs of the world.

What was man supposed to do when given the cosmos to manage? Specifically, what did that entail in civic responsibility? Here I am reasoning from what has been established. It may not be a matter of finding a separate text for every detail, although I will give passages which point towards these things.

## Determining the System and Its Governing Code

The first responsibility is to determine the kind of order, system or government around which the community will be organized. If humanity is responsible for ordering its communities, that must be part of the responsibility. What system or kind of governance will the community have?

Because man rejected God's purpose, the way we see communities organized is not necessarily the best. At first, communities developed naturally out of families. But by Genesis 6, we see tyranny, force and autocratic government. People amassing things begin to oppress others.

When God gave man responsibility to organize his communities, the intention was that he do it according to God's image and nature of life. If man received life, there should have been a shared code of morality, righteousness, civic responsibility, consequences and judgments. Imagine everyone of faith in Christ: there would be a shared code for organizing communities based on truth and on being in God's image.

But with humanity's rejection of God's purpose, there are differing codes. One person says this is acceptable; another says something else is acceptable. Keep that detail in mind, because it becomes important later. The original intention is a shared code governing the community according to truth.

The first responsibility is to determine the system of governance. Different communities use different systems. Nigeria has a democracy. Other places have military rule or monarchy. Some claim a theocracy, but the people we see governing are still human beings. Do not remove their responsibility by putting God's name on it. The responsibility was given to man. You may seek to do it according to him, but human beings are the ones there.

The second responsibility is to determine or negotiate the code and laws defining the community. The system is one thing; its ongoing laws and policies are another. Those are also determined by human beings. God gave us the responsibility. We determine the system and the code, laws and policies by which it operates.

## Participation in Governance and Representation

The third responsibility is to participate in the system or governance of the community. That presupposes politics as well. This participation may be a career or may not be a career. Many of us are not career politicians or government officials, yet we participate.

In our community, perhaps we vote. In another community, there may be different things people are called upon to do. They may volunteer for a responsibility without being politicians. The community may require people to contribute their share in some way. Participation in governance and the politics surrounding it may form your natural assignment, or it may not.

Again, I am presenting this as a matter of reason, a deduction from God's giving civic responsibility to man. We first determine the system. In Genesis 6, some men determined it and suppressed others under it. Then the laws are determined. Sometimes a few people do that and everyone else is subjected to them; we can argue that this is not how it ought to be.

Elsewhere, people are represented by others, who express their views in determining the code: a republic, which was practised in Greece, I think, then in Rome. Was it practised in Greece before Rome? I think Greece has this history of democracy as well. A republic allows everyone to participate through representatives because there are logistical difficulties in having everyone do everything directly. The right to organize in this way was given to humanity.

But many people, including believers, are not conscious that what the representatives do is their work. Some nations have a greater political consciousness. People write to their representative and say what they want. A group signs to show that this is the majority view: “If you do not represent us, we may recall you or refuse to elect you again.”

Do we have that consciousness in Nigeria? I am not saying the system is absent. The system is in place. The problem is that people are not sufficiently conscious of it. It is not merely a matter of saying a few places have it; the representative system is there. We need to understand our responsibility within it.

## Conscious Participation and the Limits of Religious Rule

Whose responsibility is it to help us become conscious? The church has a responsibility to teach civic responsibility, because it belongs to God's purpose. It is part of our duty to order the world after righteousness, through negotiation and by accepting the best compromise available in a given situation.

That does not mean I believe in Christian rulership in the sense of creating a Christian governmental system. Any governmental system remains the beast spoken of in Revelation. You cannot avoid that by giving it a Christian label. Look at church history. When religion has been used to rule in that way, it has been oppressive. Look at other attempts to rule through religion as well. It will always be oppressive.

You have to be clear about your role and accept a level of immorality. If you try to enforce righteousness in that way, you will be acting unrighteously. Understand how you are supposed to approach this.

The apostles did not unfold every part of this to its fullest philosophical conclusion, but they gave us the starting premises. Their system affected what could be done, and they commented sufficiently for us to understand further implications as circumstances unfold.

Personally, I believe a democratic system has more potential for righteousness. There are many variables and challenges, but these things lie with humanity and unfold over time. I do not believe democracy should be forced by one country going into another and imposing it.

If the conscience of the people develops, they can demand it in a way that becomes normal for them. If they are not there in their thinking and prefer a monarchy or another arrangement, that is fine. As with other matters, if we seek righteousness in this area, it has to be preached.

Participation in governance includes recommending policies even if you are not a career politician. It also includes politics leading to governance. Even in a nondemocratic community, there is lobbying and politics involved in having power to affect policy. In a democracy, that politics is clearer; elsewhere it may be less visible, but it is still there.

Humanity is meant to participate. What about Christians? In a world where everyone believed, these responsibilities would still exist. There would still be governance and politics. Humanity would still need to determine the system most aligned with truth, determine the laws, and participate in governance and the politics associated with it, whether as a career or otherwise.

## Compliance and Righteous Participation in Change

The fourth responsibility is to comply with the code or laws governing the community. This is what Scripture speaks about most directly. Jesus' statement about giving what belongs to Caesar is in that context. We will look at Matthew 22, Romans 13 and Titus 3.

Remember that we are joint heirs with Christ of this world, the cosmos. Our most obvious responsibility is what happens at the end, but that includes responsibility now. The salt-and-light passage is not merely moral responsibility, and I do not think spiritual assignment is what it discusses there.

I am not denying that spiritual assignment has value now. Of course it does. The more people are saved, the more opportunity there is to teach them what is right and for them to walk in that light. But in the salt-and-light statement, moral responsibility is a major part, civic responsibility is another, and natural assignment is also important.

Did Jesus teach people to ignore civic responsibility? No. He taught compliance. Taking the premises we have already seen, participation is also included. Can a believer participate in determining the kind of system which should exist? That may look like revolution. A believer can participate righteously, but there are many qualifications.

Think of the democratic coalition in the Abacha period, the movement seeking a return to democracy after the June 12 election. There may have been underhanded tactics in some things, but some of what they did appears to have been done righteously. They were oppressed, went underground and put out information through radio while pressing their case.

Could a believer have been involved? Let us put that one aside. I will leave it and refine some things I have said, put the right words. How could he have been involved is also another question, because it is not just important to do what is right or seek what is right. It is important to do so righteously.

This is broader than a Christian's involvement in party politics. Politics is a subset of civic responsibility. You may never be a politician. How do you see yourself as a citizen? It even involves your compound. What kind of heir of the world allows his own compound to remain dirty? Your house, compound and estate are part of your own small cosmos, your microcosm.

Sometimes you need to raise awareness: “We cannot continue like this. What is our plan for waste? What is our plan for this problem?” It can be frustrating when people do not know or care about their responsibility as heirs. They leave the work to everyone else. But believers cannot be indifferent. We are heirs of God and heirs of the world.

## Initiatives Beyond the Governing Code

Let me state the five responsibilities together. We determine the system around which we organize our communities. We determine the operating code and laws. I have used negotiate because that is the better way to approach it, but in some countries someone simply determines the laws oppressively. We participate in governance and the politics leading to it, as a career or otherwise. We comply with the governing code. And fifth, we take initiatives to further life and righteousness, even beyond that code.

Some things have not yet been legislated. A community may not have serious laws about littering or may not enforce driving rules as strictly as another place. But you are a believer, an heir of the world. Do not throw things on the ground.

People throw waste out of car windows. Sometimes they are educated and well dressed, and you are astonished to see them do it. They tidy themselves up after throwing their rubbish into the environment, as if that makes them neat. What about the world you just dirtied?

That is bad conduct for an heir of the world. You have been told that this waste blocks drains and contributes to flooding. How can an heir of the world treat it that way? Civic responsibility does not begin only when a law threatens you with punishment.

## Not Destroying the Earth

Revelation 11:18 speaks of the time of judgment and reward for God's servants, prophets and saints, those who fear his name, small and great, and of destroying those who destroy the earth.

The word is diaphtheirō, connected with corruption or decay, with the sense of thoroughly corrupting or destroying. There is a moral dimension to the destruction spoken of, but it need not be limited to moral destruction. It concerns how people treat the cosmos.

There are people who pursue themselves, gathering what they want at the expense of other human beings and the world. Believers should not be like that. We are heirs of the world and intend to order it after righteousness and life.

Do not throw things on the floor. You might think education would have reduced that behaviour, but people can continue unless they are taught. Someone says it is not the church's work to teach this. That is not true. It is part of telling you that your conduct is not righteous. You have civic responsibility under God.

A school can teach civic education, but why should you obey it? The only reason why you are compelled to obey genuinely, existentially, is if it is the purpose of the Creator. Without that root, something is missing. That is why the church has responsibility to teach it.

Reducing the waste we throw around can improve our communities. We injure one another's lives by what we do. We pray for peace and order, at least in matters of security, so that the gospel can be preached, but then destroy lives in other ways in the very world where we seek to further God's worship and purpose. That makes no sense.

We need a conscience about civic responsibility, intentionally doing what is right and correcting what is wrong. “This is not right; let us not do it.” People dump waste in a central place, and you wonder whether that is really the best humanity can do. Is there no way to arrange a proper place and system for it? It is not normal, and it is a biblical concern.

My work is teaching the gospel and God's purpose. Civic responsibility is a major part of that purpose. It is not the only thing we teach, but it is important. We should order the world after righteousness and life.

## Caesar, God and a Question Intended to Trap

Return to Matthew 22 and the fourth aspect, compliance. Verse 15 says the Pharisees plotted how to entangle Jesus in his talk. There is a wisdom which is not of God, a wisdom which plots evil. They sent their disciples with the Herodians, so that government-leaning people would be present as witnesses.

“Teacher, we know that you are true, and teach the way of God in truth; nor do you care about anyone, for you do not regard the person of men.” They were trying to put him in a corner: you speak only God's truth, so surely you will not say we should submit to a man, especially in a tyrannical system.

They thought they had caught him. But Jesus' answer was not false. Giving what belongs to Caesar is biblical truth. Humanity has responsibility for creating its systems, and we are to comply with them.

They built their premises: “You only care about God. You only say what is true. You do not fear any man, even Caesar. Tell us, therefore, what do you think? Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?” Jesus perceived their wickedness: “Why do you test me, you hypocrites?” A person can ask a natural, sincere question, but this was not one.

Not every hypocritical question must be ignored. Some can be answered. If an answer brings the matter to a conclusion, give it. There are other questions whose purpose is endless antagonism. The person does not care whether you are right; he just wants to win.

Think of arguments about Messi and Ronaldo. Some of those debates do not end because people do not want a conclusion. Both are accomplished players. Personally, I do not think it is fair when people argue that Ronaldo is not even the second greatest. If someone has achieved that much, whom are you putting ahead of him, and on what basis? You may appeal to how another player looked when playing, but what about longevity and what was achieved over that time?

I do not think Ronaldo is the first, but I also cannot accept casually placing him fifth, sixth or seventh. There is no shame in being second. Even being seventh among all players would be an extraordinary achievement. Much of the argument comes from trying to shame someone rather than recognize what he has done.

It makes me wish they had played in different periods. People who are committed to a preference can resist what they know is true. The illustration helps me understand how someone can hear the gospel, know what is being said, and still insist on the thing he prefers. Human beings can be ready to go to hell because of something they prefer: “Leave me alone. If I am going to hell, let me go to hell like that.” I am not saying every fan is like that; some are reasonable. But some people have simply made up their minds, and the argument goes nowhere.

Jesus asked for the tax money. They brought a denarius, and he asked whose image and inscription it bore. They answered, “Caesar's.” Then, “Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's.”

It is Caesar's inscription, the human system under which they live. Comply with what humanity has established. Does that mean human beings can never determine to change the system? No. But changes can be made unrighteously too, with people killing one another.

Consider Julius Caesar. He was killed by people who had planned the assassination, and the title Caesar continued to be used afterwards. They believed he was getting out of hand. Whether someone tries to justify such an act in the furtherance of life is a question of how the situation is judged, but is that the best way for things to be done? Probably not. Perhaps someone can be restrained by imprisonment. Yet in those historical settings, people also feared the influence a ruler retained and what loyal supporters might do while he remained alive. Understand their setting.

The responsibility was given to humanity. What belongs to God? We belong to God, and ultimately the cosmos does too. Jesus' answer is brilliant even at the intellectual level: he does not abandon the truth about God in acknowledging human civic responsibility.

Compliance is biblical, including under systems which are not the best, insofar as they serve order. Order serves the furtherance of life. What belongs to God's nature is truth, order and life.

## Human Authority as an Ordinance of God

Romans 13:1 says, “Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and the authorities that exist are appointed by God.” Does that mean God's direct involvement in every appointment? Not really. It means God has ordained this arrangement. He ordained that humanity should determine how it organizes itself.

Jeremiah 27:5–6 says God made the earth, man and the beasts by his great power, and gave it to whom it seemed proper to him. He claims ownership of the cosmos and the right to choose to whom it is given. He says he has given those lands into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, his servant.

It looks individual there, but I would still argue further from the ordinance God made. There is also an argument that Nebuchadnezzar was of redemptive faith, particularly after the incident described in his life. It is not impossible that he had some such faith earlier but had become full of himself. The account presents the clearest case after that period of mental illness.

Isaiah 45 also speaks of Cyrus as God's anointed, whose right hand he held. Cyrus was not necessarily a believing king. His government was more permissive towards different beliefs, and the Jews were allowed to return and rebuild the temple. This is understood as God's orchestration, using him in that role.

Return to Romans 13. Humanly determined authority is what God designed from the beginning. God ordained that humanity should determine its systems of governance and how to organize itself. That is the context in which these authorities are from God.

“Whoever resists the authority resists the ordinance of God.” When God ordained civic responsibility, the thing he was going for was order, not anarchy. Unfortunately, humans can establish an autocratic system. I want to distinguish that from saying every oppressive act is orderly, because wickedness may itself destroy order. But there are autocratic places which are highly orderly.

God desires order. Do not simply disrupt it. If you want to change a system, seek righteous ways to do so. The goal of human governance is order because it furthers life.

## Order, Life and Righteous Reform

Those who resist the ordinance bring judgment on themselves by acting unrighteously. There may be judgment now, and there is also final judgment if people do not act righteously.

Romans 13 says rulers are not a terror to good works but to evil. If you want to be unafraid of the authority, do what is good. The point is that authority exists for order and life, so that there is not anarchy. Many constitutions describe government in terms of protecting people's lives and livelihood. There is the security of physical life and the furtherance of livelihood, including economic responsibility. Do not let everything collapse.

Does humanity have the right to determine the system it wants? Yes. Can believers participate? Yes, but righteously. Imagine a country in total breakdown, where the government is doing nothing. Is it wrong for people to seek a way back to order and life? They have the right to do that.

It does not mean they must burn down their nation. They can protest: “We do not want this person again. You have to go.” They may seek support, perhaps even military support, for a change. But be careful with such matters; you can be dining with the devil. Having the right does not mean every way of exercising it is righteous.

Believers cannot participate unrighteously, but they can participate righteously. Can they be involved in determining the laws and code? Absolutely. Imagine Nigeria moving towards democracy in 1999 and people from different walks of life being called to a national conference. I cannot remember the exact name of the gathering, but believers could be there, and there would be nothing wrong with that.

Can they participate in governance and politics, as a career or otherwise? They should. Should they comply with the code? They should. Should they take initiatives for life and righteousness? They should. These are parts of civic responsibility, and civic responsibility is part of God's purpose.

## Conscience, Taxes and Honour

Romans 13:4 calls the authority God's minister to you for good. God ordained that we should have human authority, and so he calls those occupying that role his ministers. That is like the language used of Cyrus.

Can your governor or president be called God's minister in that sense, whether you like the person or not? Can the person chosen to organize your estate be described that way? Yes: for furthering life and good and dealing with evil. That person may coordinate security and other matters in the community.

If you do evil, be afraid, because the authority does not bear the sword in vain. It is God's minister to execute judgment against wrongdoing. Therefore, be subject not only because of wrath but for conscience' sake. There is a deterrent, but you should comply because you know it is right, from conviction and conscience.

“For because of this you also pay taxes, for they are God's ministers attending continually to this very thing.” Render to everyone what is due: taxes to whom taxes are due, customs to whom customs, fear to whom fear and honour to whom honour. That is compliance.

There are examples of people of faith participating in governance: Erastus, Daniel and Joseph. There is nothing wrong with participation. The matters which may be harder to work through are determining or changing a system and determining its code, because changes are not always pursued righteously. But humanity has those responsibilities too.

Beyond compliance, should we take initiatives which further life? In the same discussion, Paul says, “Owe no one anything except to love one another.” There are things to do in love and righteousness which further life. Matthew 5 also speaks of good works which people see and through which they glorify the Father. That calls for initiative.

Do not throw waste from the car window. Put it in your bag or pocket until you reach a proper bin. Even if our wider waste system is broken, that is better than throwing it where it goes into the drain or river. These small things are part of our responsibility.

## Initiative, Truth, Order and Life

Ephesians 5:8 says, “You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Walk as children of light.” Does that involve moral responsibility? Yes. Does it involve civic responsibility? Yes. The fruit of light is in goodness, righteousness and truth. Find out what is acceptable to the Lord.

We have studied righteousness: what is of God involves life, truth and order. Do not isolate one of those from the others. Suppose someone asks you to help him cheat in an examination and calls it walking in love: “Do you want me to fail? Help me further my life.” No, cheating is not walking in love. It is untruthful.

There is nonrelational righteousness. You cannot use relational love to contradict it. What is of God is life, truth and order. Likewise, someone cannot appeal to love and the furtherance of life to demand that you help fulfil a wrongful sexual desire. That is disordered conduct.

Relational righteousness does not contravene nonrelational righteousness. Love does not mean participating in someone's decision to walk in the flesh. Someone may try to quote the language of compassion and ask how God's love can dwell in you if you refuse. But what about God's life and righteousness dwelling in me? You cannot use that appeal to require unrighteous conduct.

“Finding out what is acceptable to the Lord” is important because some situations are not clear-cut. In an edge case, you may not find a verse stating the precise answer. You must make a moral judgment for a moral expression in that situation.

## Counting the Cost of Changing a System

A believer seeking a system which tends towards anarchy rather than the furtherance of life has probably not thought the matter through. Sometimes I hear what people demand and wonder whether they have counted the cost. Have you considered, from truth, whether this is righteous?

The system itself may not be the problem. The problem may be your participation: whom you put in office, how you engage in politics and governance, which policies you seek and what you speak up about. It may not be the system that needs changing. Do not use the language of change as an excuse for unrighteousness.

At other times, people may rightly conclude that a different system would serve them better. There is nothing unrighteous about that if they pursue it righteously.

Titus 3:1–2 says to remind people to be subject to rulers and authorities, to obey and to be ready for every good work. That is compliance, and readiness for good works may also include taking initiatives for life. Speak evil of no one, be peaceable and gentle, and show humility to all people.

## Origin, Sin and the Best Negotiated Code

We began by establishing civic responsibility in God's purpose. We saw its final outcome and our present responsibility as salt and light. At the beginning, humanity was given responsibility to determine systems of governance, determine their operating code and laws, participate in governance and politics, comply with the code and take initiatives for righteousness and life.

Under sin, those responsibilities became distorted. After the family structures and communities arising in Genesis 4 and 5, Genesis 6 presents oppressive government. The language of mighty men, gibbōr, points us towards people ruling by force of arms. Systems were imposed, codes could be determined autocratically, and people were forced to participate and comply.

That was not the best of civic responsibility. Under sin, it changes in different places and degrees, but we find people destroying the earth, as Revelation 11:18 describes.

Redemption should steer us towards life and righteousness in governance and civic responsibility. Even those of redemptive faith before Christ came can be seen interacting with these responsibilities. Redemption directs us towards what is best within the world as it exists.

We do not presently have a shared code of truth in governance. Different moral codes exist. We must negotiate for a compromised code which is the best available. If you refuse the possibility of such a code, your participation will be unrighteous.

We must be willing to allow people's liberties, even where they use them sinfully, while still seeking what is most righteous for the community. These are God-given liberties. Our participation in this age, until the end, involves that tension.

Should we participate in policy to that extent? Yes. What is most righteous in this situation? What should we seek? That should affect our thinking, writing and even the offices in which we serve. There is nothing wrong with believers entering positions where they can affect these things.

We must know both our responsibility and our limits. You cannot force the outcome. There will be negotiated outcomes which are compromised compared with what we know to be wholly righteous. We seek what is presently best, the most righteous outcome available in the actual situation.

In an age where people share the same nature and beliefs, the situation will be different. The field of participation itself will be different.

## A Speculation About the Coming Age

People sometimes ask whether there will be sin in the new heavens and new earth. I think there would be, depending on what is meant by sin. I do not think there will be sins of commission, doing things of death and darkness, such as adultery, fornication or murder. I do think there will be sins of omission: weakness in doing the righteous things one ought to do, perhaps in relation to assignment.

I do not think everyone will automatically be serious about everything. I think there will still be a need for growth and a strong hidden man, and differing degrees of faithfulness will still involve consequence and judgment in that age.

This is theological speculation. There are degrees of doctrine. There is primary truth which is dogmatic. There is secondary truth with strong doctrinal authority. There is practical truth whose application can be examined in a particular context. Then there is theological speculation and opinion.

Many questions about the new age fall into that last category. Sometimes you can try to make the best judgment from what you know of God's nature, character, human design and the dynamics of the human person. You reason that something is likely to be the case. That is my opinion here.

Someone asked me about this after listening to the teaching on our righteous nature in Christ. I really still think that is the case, because otherwise something about humanity, purpose and meaningful action would be removed. I do not simply mean that it would remove the fun. I mean that if everything were automatic, we would be like robots rather than human beings.

We have a nature, but part of the beauty of the mind is that the nature is expressed through a mind which reasons and makes decisions. Certain desires will no longer be available to us, but the strength that comes with your consciousness is still a thing. Some things have been removed from the equation, but that does not mean all remaining aspects become automatic.

Fellowship with God would still be real, and differing strength in that fellowship would matter in our work. Again, this is theological speculation. I am not presenting it as authoritative doctrine. It is an opinion which may be worth thinking about, not something I am asking you to treat as settled teaching.

## Personal Judgment and Faithful Civic Participation

Do you have civic responsibility in God's purpose? How faithfully are you participating? Think from your home, compound and estate to your area, local government and state. How are you carrying out that responsibility?

Think seriously about policies and people. Who do I genuinely believe can further righteousness and life? This is between me and God. It is a responsibility for which he will judge me.

Do not merely follow the pressure of people who say you must support someone. What looks like banter can become harassment intended to control your vote. Different sides use those methods. But you are a believer: keep a clear head and think it through. Who do you genuinely believe will give more righteousness and life?

We may not make the same judgment. Do your best, acting fairly according to what you believe. Luke 12:47–48 speaks to accountability according to knowledge. Someone in this church may think APC is best; another may think NDC, another ADC. The names have changed, but each is acting on what he thinks is best, and that is okay. I do not think anybody is acting on what he thinks is evil.

You may look at another person's judgment and think it is evil, while that person is seeking what he thinks is best. Think it through: what do you believe will further righteousness and life? Sometimes the difference is relatively small. Think of a U.S. election involving Obama and another strong candidate. At that point, the decision may largely concern policies and differences of emphasis.

Even in the last election, I believed the people there were fantastic people, and someone could simply be deciding which one he thought would be better. Sometimes candidates' manifestos promise similar things. Whom do you think is better able to give more righteousness and life? You must decide. We have different parameters and reasons and may reach different conclusions. That is part of civic responsibility.

## Churches, Local Government and Collective Citizen Demands

We need to do better in local government. In Nigeria, many of us do not know what is going on locally. What can I do? What is my part in making the community better? In some respects, churches function more like local government than the local government does.

If churches take civic responsibility seriously and help raise people's consciousness of their duties, I believe it can change the nation. There is power in the church, but not by dictating, “Vote for this person,” or pronouncing curses against that person. Those things can make the problem worse. They are part of the bad behaviour we are discussing.

Let people see their responsibilities and understand that the goal is life and righteousness. Let them know they ought to participate. Perhaps then people will become more serious about the outcomes they want.

Even from an entrepreneurial standpoint, something could be done. Imagine communities gathering to identify the political outcomes they want from the next government. Citizens state their goals, and those goals are compared with what each candidate promises to do.

At present we often do not have an aggregated communal demand. Someone could gather what people say matters most to them, compare it with what candidates propose, consider how the goals might be achieved and decide whom they trust most to do it. I have thought of an idea along the lines of a citizens' platform, perhaps a name like citizens.ng, though someone may already have that domain.

These are my ideas for helping our participation work better. Civic responsibility is one part of God's purpose, but it is an important part. I hope we do better as a people.

Let us pray as we close. As you are seated, commit your heart further and better to participating in this responsibility you have.
