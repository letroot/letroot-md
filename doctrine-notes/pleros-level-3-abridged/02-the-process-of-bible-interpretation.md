# The Process of Bible Interpretation

## The Church, God's Purpose, and the Knowledge We Need

Our Father, I thank you that I am your child and part of your family. Gathered with my siblings, I receive of your word light, truth, revelation of Christ and his work in all its fullness, and of his fullness in me. In the name of the Lord Jesus, amen.

We are looking at the process of Bible interpretation, the second teaching in Level Three. I want us to start from Ephesians 4:11. Speaking about the Lord Jesus, Paul says he gave the apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers for the perfecting of the saints. The primary reason we have the church is for us to be perfected or discipled as saints. Why does he want us perfected? So we can fulfill his purpose, the reason he made us in the first place.

God's purpose is sonship. He wants us to walk as his children and heirs: to fellowship with him, walk in his nature and character, and carry out our civic responsibility, natural assignment, and spiritual assignment. The church is designed to help us fulfill that purpose. The Lord Jesus' death and resurrection redeemed us back to it, and your faith in Christ brings you into it. You are already in God's purpose through faith in Christ, but being brought into that purpose does not remove the question of how the church will help you walk in it. What exactly is the church meant to do to help us fulfill it?

The church is given “for the perfecting of the saints,” then “for the work of ministry.” A more accurate reading of the second phrase is “unto the work of ministry.” The first “for” is *pros*: towards the perfecting of the saints. The second is *eis*: unto the work of ministry. The last says unto the edifying of the body of Christ. When the church perfects the saints, the saints do their assignments—particularly here their spiritual assignment—and the body is built up both internally and externally. You are not only adding people to the church; you are helping them become stronger spiritually.

How will they perfect the saints? “Perfecting,” *katartismos*, means bringing something to an end. Verse 13 shows the endpoint and, in doing so, reveals what would be done to get us there: “Till we all come to the unity of the faith,” “the knowledge of the Son of God,” “the perfect man,” and “the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.”

“Unity of the faith” means wholesome doctrine. Ordinarily faith is the act of believing, but it can also mean what is believed. Here we are to come to the wholesomeness, the entirety, of what we have believed to be saved. The first two descriptions concern knowledge: the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God. The last two concern a person's state of growth: the perfect man, an adult, and the measure of the stature of Christ's fullness.

The church must bring us to an endpoint in knowledge and an endpoint in growth. That growth depends on the knowledge of God's word. We are to become stronger in our inner man and adorn our hidden man by our faith stand. When we receive the knowledge, we can grow in what we have known. Upon it we affirm, reckon, and present ourselves to walk as God desires. The knowledge of God's word is primary. This does not mean that information and growth are identical. The first two descriptions in Ephesians concern what we know; the last two concern what state of maturity we attain. We receive the knowledge first, and by that knowledge grow in what we have known.

Our study is focused on how we obtain that knowledge. Your fulfillment of God's purpose depends on how effectively, accurately, or precisely you engage Bible interpretation. We could say fulfillment depends on the knowledge of God's word, that entire framework of belief called the unity of the faith. But how do we arrive at that message we are going to grow by?

## The Apostolic Message and Paul's Responsibility to Present It

Paul tells us in Ephesians 3 that he has presented the message. He had even written about it in the earlier parts of the book. You might therefore say, “Why do we need Bible interpretation? We can simply go to what the apostles taught.” On one hand you are right. The message we need to grow and fulfill God's purpose is the message taught by the apostles. You need the truth to be saved and to grow. Once you have received the gospel, you are learning that same message, receiving the unity of the faith to grow to the required stature.

Ephesians 3:1 speaks of “the dispensation of the grace of God which was given to me for you.” Paul had the grace to present the message. This is also what he says in 1 Corinthians 3: “According to the grace of God which is given unto me, as a wise master builder, I have laid the foundation.” It was a grace particularly given to him to present the message.

He says that by revelation the mystery was made known to him, as he had briefly written already, “by which, when you read, you may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ.” He had already taught what God wants us to know. But we must add a detail: the message we need is what the apostles taught as they taught the Holy Scriptures.

Romans 16:25 says, “Now to him who is able to establish you according to my gospel and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery kept secret since the world began, but now made manifest.” It continues, “by the scriptures of the prophets,” or “by the prophetic Scriptures,” “made known to all nations.” As Ephesians says “my knowledge,” Romans says “my gospel.” Yet Paul says it is by the Scriptures that this gospel is made known.

We obtain the message from the apostles, and you might say particularly or centrally Paul because of the grace given to him. God did not tell the message only to Paul. The apostles were already preaching it in Acts 2:42, where the believers continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine. Paul's role is that of an architect: to synthesize the message and give it its most exact and precise presentation.

Imagine an uncle living in a first-world country who wants to build a house in Nigeria. Buying a house where he lives might take a long time to pay off, but he can build one in Nigeria. He calls his siblings and explains what he wants: his own house, perhaps also serving as a family house, the number of buildings in the compound, and the kinds of buildings. He tells them not merely that he wants a house but how many buildings he has in mind and whether the buildings should be semi-detached or duplexes. The siblings are informed enough to tell their spouses and children exactly what he wants. But they cannot be as exact as an architect. They still need an architect to draw the plan and design.

When Paul says “my gospel” and “my knowledge in the mystery of Christ,” his claim concerns his presentation, not a new content. His presentation does include the inclusion of the Gentiles as a particular emphasis. But we need his presentation. Peter acknowledges his claim in 2 Peter 3:15–16, speaking of Paul writing according to the wisdom given to him. This is the same grace that makes Paul a wise master builder, the wisdom to be an architect.

We should be comfortable with Pauline theology as apostolic theology, God's own message, the Lord Jesus' message given to his apostles. It is not one message different from another. It is the prophets' message too. The same house God wanted to build, the same purpose and plan, was what he told the prophets before the Lord Jesus was on the scene. It is what Jesus and the apostles were saying, and what Paul is now presenting accurately, exactly, and precisely.

## Having the Scriptures Does Not Guarantee Right Interpretation

You may say, “Let's go to the apostles or the Scriptures and get the message.” But people have gone to them and made a mess of it. Bible interpretation might be why you don't know God's purpose for your life. Someone can tell you what God's will is without being entirely accurate. If you ask people about God's purpose, they might say merely to know God or worship him. You can thereby lose sight of the entire picture of what he will judge you on, although he has revealed his purpose in his word.

In Luke 12:47–48, the one who did not know and did not do according to his will would be beaten with few stripes; the one who knew but did not prepare would be beaten with many. Not knowing exactly what he said his purpose is still affects you. The issue is not that the apostles and prophets failed to say it, or that Paul failed to present it exactly. It is that you have not seen it. The issue is interpretation.

Second Peter 3:15–16 says Paul wrote according to the wisdom given him, “as also in all his epistles.” Peter is recognizing the scriptural authority of Paul's writings, his inclusion in the divine authority given to the apostles to submit the message. Rather than saying Peter gives Paul scriptural authority, it is better to say he recognizes it. He acknowledges the authority already belonging to those writings and the wisdom given to Paul for their presentation. He says Paul speaks of things “in which are some things hard to understand.” Some subject matters are difficult by nature.

Mathematics can be the most straightforward subject, unlike English pronunciation, where British and American pronunciations can differ. Yet even mathematics has subjects that are inherently more difficult. I was good in mathematics and could compete with people studying further mathematics, but when I started further mathematics myself, permutation and combination made me wonder whether I wanted all that stress. The difficulty was in the subject matter, not necessarily the person explaining it. Even with a clear teacher you must pay attention. Some subjects on the podcast are received easily; others require you to think harder and exercise yourself more.

Paul has wisdom and is giving us clarity. As the architect, he gives the simplest and clearest explanation we could possibly have, yet certain doctrinal subjects require patience because they are difficult by nature. Don't make Paul himself the problem. If someone with his wisdom and clarity is difficult, what of those who lack it? The text speaks of difficult subject matters.

Peter continues, “Which untaught and unstable people twist to their own destruction, as they do also the rest of the Scriptures.” People go to the apostles and prophets and struggle with their writings, forcing out a meaning different from what it should be. They reference the writings, but twist them. How do we know what the apostles and prophets are saying without forcing out a meaning other than the one they intend?

These people are using the very text. They are using the Bible, yet not arriving at the meaning intended by its authors. Having the text does not guarantee that you have its meaning. They are not twisting something outside the Bible; they are working with the apostles and the Scriptures of the prophets themselves. Their use of biblical references is therefore not enough to establish that the message they have reached is the intended one.

## The Author's Intention, the Text's Rendition, and the Reader's Interpretation

Right interpretation does not create a meaning; it finds the meaning. The author's intention is to be taken as the text's rendition. We are not with the author asking whether he means this or that. He knew his responsibility as an apostle or prophet to write what God expected, and what he wrote is what he intended. Writing what you do not intend would mean lying or making a mistake. We do not take God's divinely orchestrated document that way, whether Old or New Testament.

The author's intention, rendered in the text, must become the reader's interpretation. What he intended and wrote is what we must see. Our growth, walk, and fulfillment of God's purpose depend on that message, which is codified in the text. How do we interpret the text to arrive at it?

By “decode,” I do not mean the authors were trying to hide their message secretly. Writing communicates meaning through symbols; it is encoding in that sense. How do we recover the meaning intended and written by the authors? Someone may say, “Just read.” But the people twisting it were also reading. Ephesians 3:4 says that when we read, we may understand. We are not disputing that; we are examining reading rightly done rather than reading and twisting the text into another message.

The arguments I see online sometimes make me want to go away, but if such mindsets exist, I must address them as part of reaching all nations and every creature. There is much pride and assumption, without humility. It is good to believe you have the truth and live by it, but someone with the truth is willing to examine. I should not fear examining what another person says.

That does not mean spending all your time there. Pausing to spend time on such things affects your growth, your inner man and hidden man. You may sacrifice for a while, but cannot do it continually and mess up your mind. Particularly within the faith, listen through to what someone is saying instead of assuming from the start they are wrong and merely looking for fault. Afterward you can know what you know.

The process of Bible interpretation should make you patient, not quick to dismiss everything. There are abuses and outright falsehoods, but an impatient approach is not right. The servant of the Lord must not strive. He must be patient to teach and also patient to listen, so he knows what he is responding to.

## The God-Orchestrated Documents and Their Message

Second Timothy 3:16 says, “All scripture is given by inspiration of God.” God-breathed: orchestrated by God. The entire document is God's orchestration. Some may argue this refers only to the content, but there is more reason to see the document itself as what is being described. It contains the content, of course. God's intention is for this very document to be his authoritative document for truth, containing the message that still requires interpretation.

In “God-breathed,” the *theo* part is straightforward. For the other part, we should stay with *pneuma*, *pneō*, and related words rather than *psuchē*. *Pneuma*, spirit or wind, is an invisible reality with visible effect. *Psuchē*, breath, has the similar implication of an invisible reality with animating effect, but that is not the word used here. *Theopneustos* says the documents are caused by God: the visible effect of an invisible, divine source. They are divinely effected. The distinction I am making is between merely saying there are divinely inspired utterances within the text and saying that this document itself has been brought to be by God. The document and its message must not be set against one another, because the document contains the message.

There is a place for saying the content contains inspired utterances, but I question whether that is the best expression here. He referred to the content in verse 15; in verse 16 he speaks of all the Scriptures, the documents, as God-breathed. You could name their visible sources—Isaiah wrote this, another person that, Paul another—but their invisible source is God. Although the New Testament text was not just in view here, we apply the same principle. These documents are God-orchestrated, brought to be by God.

They are profitable for doctrine, so the minister teaches them; for reproof, to give evidence; for correction, to straighten people where they are missing it; and for instruction, child-training or growth in righteousness. Someone who will grow and have the persuasion to walk in all God desires needs the Scriptures. But they need the document to give them its message.

How do we move from manuscripts to meaning, from the text to what it says? Scripture is given “that the man of God may be complete, thoroughly furnished”—equipped—“for every good work.” To do what God desires, you need the document's message. Paul tells us how to move from document to message.

## Fully Following the Apostolic Doctrine

Paul is the one chosen by God as the architect responsible to present the message. In 2 Timothy 3:10, the KJV says, “You have fully known,” and the NKJV, “You have carefully followed.” I believe “You have fully followed” is the best rendering: “fully” from the KJV and “followed” from the NKJV.

The word is *parakoloutheō*. The explanation is of being together on a road, with *para* meaning beside: you have been with me on that road, side by side. Following the doctrine is like following a road map or journey. In a race with several laps on the same road, you know where to turn and what to do. Imagine doctrine that way.

Doctrine is sometimes called a way. In 1 Corinthians 4:17, Paul sends his son to remind them of his ways, as he teaches everywhere in every church. Even in the Old Testament, “way” or “ways” is used to describe doctrine: like a road, like a journey.

Timothy has been on the same road with Paul, side by side. He had gone one, two, three laps with Paul on that doctrinal journey and can now tell, “This is how the message is. This is the way to go. We are supposed to show this, then that, then present it like this.”

The issue is not simply that he has been careful—not that he has not been careful—but that he has gone the entire road with Paul. It is a matter of having fully followed. That is why you can understand the KJV using “fully known,” and the NKJV using “followed.” It is a road, but it should be “fully followed”: not just the manner of following, but that you were completely there.

Timothy has fully followed Paul's doctrine, and Paul mentions his manner of life, purpose, faith, longsuffering, love, perseverance, persecutions and afflictions—the persecutions he endured, out of which God delivered him. Verse 12 says those who live godly in Christ Jesus must suffer persecution. The degree may differ, but you cannot have everybody's acceptance. Then verse 13 says evil men and impostors will grow worse and worse, deceiving and being deceived.

## Paul's Doctrine and Those Who Resist the Truth

To understand those evil men, read the earlier verses leading up to “You have carefully followed my doctrine.” Second Timothy 3:7 says, “Always learning and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth.” Paul's intention when he says “the truth” is existential truth, the truth of existence. He believes and has explained that the gospel answers that question. Paul's truth is the gospel, and he is concerned here that people have been deviating from it.

Second Timothy 2:15 says Timothy should rightly divide the word of truth. In verses 17 and 18, Paul says the message of some people spreads like cancer. Hymenaeus and Philetus are of this sort. False preaching can grow like wildfire. People can take truth and distort it, making a mess of it like cancer affecting healthy cells. These men have strayed concerning the truth, saying the resurrection is past already, and they overthrow the faith of some. What people preach can damage people's persuasion, conviction and growth—their faith stand.

Second Timothy 2:24 and 25 says a servant of the Lord must not quarrel but must be gentle to all, apt to teach, patient, in humility correcting those in opposition, if God perhaps will grant them repentance. Yet people quarrel shamelessly, as though this is not in the Bible or they are willing to lose their reward. A quarrelsome spirit is not expected of a bishop, as in 1 Timothy 3. Why be unnecessarily troublesome in presenting God's word?

Sometimes people teach not because they want others to know the truth but because they want to shine. Your goal is to persuade people. Be conscious that if you say something in a particular way, they may focus more on the offence than the truth. Say it calmly. It should take someone deliberately rebellious, trying to make you step out of character, rather than quarrelling being your natural disposition. It should not be your default. The goal is repentance so that they may know the truth.

The context is that Timothy teaches the truth while some people go off teaching other things. From 2 Timothy 3:1, Paul describes perilous times, people loving themselves and money, and the things that happen because of the error of certain teachers. Verse 6 says they creep into households and make captives of gullible women. It is not because they are women: it is a social rather than natural thing, a social condition at that time that unfortunately made women susceptible. They are led away by various lusts, always learning but never coming to the knowledge of the truth.

Verse 8 says that as Jannes and Jambres resisted Moses, these people resist the truth. You could check the etymology of those names; they are Egyptians. Is it the same truth Moses was preaching? Yes. These are men of corrupt minds, disapproved concerning the faith, whose folly will become manifest as theirs did. Paul is likely referring to an oral tradition about what happened, perhaps the Talmud [unclear reference].

People oppose the truth now just as people did in Moses' day. Paul then says, “But you have carefully followed my doctrine.” He calls his doctrine the truth. The evil men in verse 13 are those opposing that truth, growing worse and worse, deceiving and being deceived.

## The Authority of Interpretation Must Be a Definite Process

We have said it takes the message of the apostles and the Scriptures to fulfil God's purpose. We have what they said in written form, the Old and New Testament texts. But how do we know the message in what they said? Second Peter 3:15 and 16 shows that people can have what they wrote and twist it.

If there is no process from their written text to their message, anybody can handle it however they like and arrive at whatever meaning they think is right. Worse, without a definite process, people can make themselves the authority of interpretation. That authority cannot be people. Humans are fallible.

You cannot simply say, “I trust them; I have no fear.” You may trust me based on precedent, but should not enter blind trust. There may be a track record of faithfulness, observation and precedent. You would not marry someone without building a relationship and knowing enough to decide whether to trust them. Do not locate the authority of interpretation in people; locate it in a process.

You may trust your science teacher, but you know a process produces a certain result. If he tells you to do something that, from everything you have learned, will blow up in your face, you need to pause, even if you have always trusted him. Tell him to do it first and show you that it does not blow up. Saying “I am loyal to my teacher” does not make that wise. The unfortunate case is when the negative effects of something take a long time to become visible.

How do we move from what the apostles and prophets said to the meaning? We need a process of interpretation, not merely methods in the sense of “This is my own approach.” People can develop concepts that reflect the process, but those are secondary, extra layers on what is basic.

## Methods and Concepts Must Serve the Process

In football, the goal is to get the ball past the opposing players, including the goalkeeper, into the net. There are different ways: a pass from defence to a runner, playing through the players, or an individual running past them. You can use extra skills, leg-overs and cuts, getting one person out of the way and moving to the next. Those are fine as long as you get the ball into the net.

But you could use the most stripped-down approach, going past them without extra skills and scoring. That is efficiency. Certain players have dropped things they used to do or changed roles to become more efficient at putting the ball in the net.

I see methods and concepts in interpretation similarly. Some are extras, not the issue. They must align with the process. You cannot invent a concept and make it the arbiter of truth in interpreting the text. It is the process of interpretation that leads to the meaning or message.

There is nothing necessarily wrong with something like “the law of first mention.” You can think through God's word and consider the first time a concept appeared. You may even intend to use first principles by that expression. But someone can take the concept and abuse it because it is not the process of interpretation. They can say, “The first time this was mentioned it was this, so here it means this,” and get into all kinds of problems. What is the process of Bible interpretation?

Methods can be okay and concepts can be fine. But we need the process instructed by the apostles and the Scriptures, and necessitated by the truth. The truth itself calls for its own process of Bible interpretation.

## The Text, the Process and the Message

Second Timothy 3:14 and 15 says, “Continue in the things which you have learned and have been assured of, knowing from whom you have learned them.” The things learned are the message: its different subject matters and details. He is saying, “Continue in the message you got from a particular source.” I use “message” in the singular, but it contains many subject matters.

Who taught Timothy? Paul. Second Timothy 2:2 says to commit what he learned from Paul among many witnesses to faithful men able to teach others also. Paul is representative of the apostles. Because of that central role, for us today we can speak of the New Testament texts.

Paul also reminds Timothy that from childhood he has known the Holy Scriptures. The sources of the message are Paul and the Old Testament text. Today we say the New Testament and the Old Testament—the Bible, the Christian or Protestant Bible.

Then comes the statement, “Which are able to make you wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ.” The message Timothy learned came from a source that would make him wise to its message. You do not simply arrive at the message: there is a process between the source and its message. There is the source, the text; the process, making you wise; and the message at which you arrive.

“Make you wise” is the Greek *sophizō*, a verb meaning to make you *sophos*. Strong's gives “to make or render *sophos*.” We need to know what it means to be *sophos*. We are using these Greek words for a simple explanation. The Scriptures make you wise to the message from the text. Is that automatic? No, otherwise 2 Peter 3 would not have been said. There is a precise way this happens.

*Sophos* is from *saphēs*, clear. The adjective “wise” means someone who has clarity. Clarity itself is *sophia*, wisdom. We need to understand what being wise means and how one arrives there.

## Wisdom and Understanding: Putting the Parts Together

Matthew 11:25 says, “I thank You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent and have revealed them to babes.” Wise and prudent are used negatively here; there is also a positive use. Notice that “wise,” *sophos*, and “prudent,” *synetos*, are parallel or complementary words explaining one another.

Luke 10:21 repeats that thought as Jesus rejoices in the Spirit and thanks the Father for hiding these things from the wise and prudent and revealing them to babes. We are seeking how one obtains *sophia* and becomes a *sophos* person. To *sophizō* is to give wisdom so that you are wise.

First Corinthians 1:19 shows the parallel again: “I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and will bring to nothing the understanding of the prudent.” This is another negative use, concerning people who ignore revelatory truth because they do not accept God's truth even from natural truth. But focus here on the words: *sophia*, wisdom; *sophos*, wise; *synesis*, understanding; *synetos*, prudent.

Paul calls himself a wise master builder, and Peter speaks of wisdom given to him. If wisdom and understanding are parallel, can we say Paul also has understanding? Yes. Ephesians 3:4 says, “By which, when you read, you may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ.” “Knowledge” there is *synesis*. The mystery was not made known in other ages to the sons of men.

If we know how we arrive at wisdom or understanding, we know how we are made wise. *Synesis* helps because it explains itself. “Understanding” and “prudent” come from the verb *syniēmi*, from *syn*, together, and *hiēmi*, to send or bring: to bring together or put together. You can check dictionaries to confirm it.

Luke 24:45 says he opened their understanding, their *nous*, so that they might comprehend the Scriptures. To comprehend is to put together. He has shown them the different parts, broken it down and put it together.

Putting together gives understanding, and you are therefore called wise. *Sophia*, wisdom, is the state you are in because understanding has been achieved. Understanding is the putting together; the resulting state is clarity. Wisdom is the resultant effect of that process. You have synthesised it, it is clear to you, you have wisdom, and therefore you are wise. You have been made wise because the putting together has occurred.

When Paul says the Scriptures make you wise, the Scriptures are to be put together to the message. That putting together gives *synesis* and *sophia*, making you *sophos* or *synetos*. The process of arriving at the text's meaning or Scripture's message is putting together its parts and details.

## The Jigsaw Puzzle: Exact Parts, Not Parts We Invent

When something is put together, it has definite parts that can be broken down or fitted back. A jigsaw puzzle illustrates *syniēmi*. You see the intended image on the back or carton, then take the pieces and decide where they belong. I recently saw one with numbered instructions showing exactly where each piece went. That is like a teacher's work.

Without instruction, a puzzle can take days and become frustrating. Someone assumes a piece belongs somewhere, later discovers the mistake and starts questioning whether the puzzle even fits. With someone showing where a piece belongs and why, it becomes easier than racking your brain without instruction.

Similarly, we arrive at the text's meaning or Scripture's message by fitting its parts together. You do not fit in whatever you like. There are exact parts. When I speak to you, you fit together the actual parts of what I say to know what I mean. We fit the exact pieces of the text together as they actually are.

## Rightly Dividing: Disassembling and Reassembling Precisely

If exact parts can be fitted together, there is a way they can be broken out. Consider a car. It has exact joints where parts meet. Even welded parts can be taken apart and joined back. I am not an expert, but consider a toy car: rather than smashing it to see inside, with the right tools you can patiently loosen it and see the motor, battery and other parts.

You see which part makes something work, why the parts work together, and how to bolt them back exactly. You can disassemble and reassemble it. Someone watching, while you explain each part, understands how the car came about and why it works that way. They might discover functions they never knew it had. Perhaps it is a flying car, and only when its parts and workings are explained do they see what they can do with it.

Second Timothy 2:15 says, “Be diligent to present yourself approved to God,” or approved of God, “a worker who does not need to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth.” You show yourself fit and qualified.

“Rightly dividing” is *orthotomeō*, from two words. *Orthos* means straight or not crooked. The point is not cutting everything in a straight line, but exactness: disassembling something just the right way. You do not smash the car to see inside; you disassemble it exactly. *Temnō* means to cut. You cut precisely, recognising where each part should be loosened.

Now we see why Paul says the Scriptures can make Timothy wise: they bring things together to the message, to a state of wisdom and understanding. With Scripture there must be a breaking down and putting together, a disassembling and reassembling.

Simply disassembling and reassembling without explanation may not give understanding. You must show what each part means and why it works that way. If someone disassembles a laptop, explains its parts and reassembles it, you see why it functions and perhaps discover more things it can do. You also know how to reproduce that process.

It is the same with Scripture. You rightly divide, show how each part is, and then put it together. What happened to Timothy is what you are to do for others. Paul broke it down, but did not stop there: he brought it together. That is why Timothy became wise, having understanding and wisdom.

The process of Bible teaching is *orthotomeō* and *syniēmi*: break it down and put it together. In explaining a text, you do this until people see its meaning. After doing that across several texts, you take the texts themselves and put them together.

Then that is the message.

## The Process We Use in Understanding Speech

There is a process of Bible interpretation. It is not going to the text and saying what you feel like saying. This is not applicable only to Scripture. We emphasize it, as Paul does, because we are dealing with matters of eternal verity and must be careful in our approach. But it is what we do every day.

As you listen to me, you are piecing out the details of what I say and putting them together. It happens very fast, almost intuitively. When I say “taking for granted,” you do not treat “taking” simply as taking a physical thing. Your mind treats “taking for granted” almost as one word, because it is a phrase on its own. Even with the words I use to explain that statement, the same thing is happening. You are always engaging in interpretation.

Someone who hears me say, “You are taking it for granted,” without the context of our discussion will not get what I mean. You have a context as you interpret, because you know what we have been discussing. That itself is part of this process.

## Books and Textual Contexts

What exact parts should we break the text into, so that we can put those parts together to get the meaning of the text and the message of Scripture? Starting with the whole Bible, the first exact division is the books of the Bible. They were individual books, and that has implications.

When we approach a book, the first thing we rightly disassemble is textual context. Some books are so short that you might argue there is only one textual context. Yet even a short book can have a salutation that can itself be considered a textual context. Notice that we are not saying that after books come chapters and verses. We are saying textual contexts. An author can have several things he intends to bring out, and the reader must recognize the transitions rather than assume the printed chapter division settles the matter. Sometimes the chapter divisions got the textual context right, but we must identify the context of the text.

The author can have different textual contexts he is bringing out. Sometimes, in teaching, you also have the liberty to examine a textual context of your own, without hurting the overall context of the book or the author's intended textual context. The Lord and the apostles take a textual context and bring out a meaning that they corroboratively piece with another to arrive at a submission.

First Corinthians makes this clear. Chapter 7, “Concerning the things you wrote to me,” deals with marital matters. Chapter 8 moves to another matter: knowledge puffs up, love edifies, and the issue of idols. We can argue that this context runs through chapters 9 and 10, because at the end of chapter 10 Paul brings up that same matter as though concluding it.

Chapter 11 introduces another context concerning keeping the traditions and the covering of the head. He says the hair is for covering the head. That is not our issue today; that passage has contexts we would need to explain to put its meaning together. In the same chapter, he addresses another tradition, the Lord's Supper. Chapter 12 introduces “concerning spirituals,” which continues through chapters 13 and 14. By the end of chapter 14, he has concluded.

Chapter 15 brings another context: “I declare to you the gospel which I preached to you.” He is not discussing spirituals but how the gospel includes resurrection. Having addressed the issues people brought up, he addresses another issue he is hearing: “How can people be saying resurrection has passed? I want to tell you about that same message I have been preaching.”

Be aware of author-intended textual contexts. As the reader, you can also identify a context you want to examine, noting the author's intended context and the overall book. To interpret properly, you must do this breaking down yourself, so that you can piece the meaning together.

## Historical and Literary Context

Number two is historical context. This is a truth process of interpretation that we use beyond the Bible. Someone entering our discussion midway may not understand what we mean, because he lacks the context of what we have been discussing. Likewise, when you read “however,” pause; when you see “therefore,” ask about the premises leading to the conclusion. What brought up the discussion?

Be careful not to abuse historical context. To a degree, a material can help you with its own context. The Bible gives us a reasonable amount to work with: when reading the Epistles, for example, we have Acts. Extra historical material about the Second Temple or intertestamental period can also help. But do not take something you have read in history and draw it directly to why a statement was made in a way that stretches the meaning. Scholarship can help us arrive at meaning; historical material is not, by itself, the conclusion of meaning.

Number three is literary genre. Reading poetry like prose, or prose like poetry, causes problems. You must recognize the genre. Number four is literary elements: the constituents of the literature, such as theme, subject matter, characterization, and plot. What is going on in this piece?

Number five is literary technique. This is distinct from the elements. Figures of speech are techniques, as are particular ways of writing. In Revelation, for example, making the same conclusion several times is a technique. Metaphor, simile, apostrophe, and personification are literary techniques. When we read, “Sin shall not have…” who is sin? There is a sort of personification. Be aware of what the writer is doing, rather than confusing the technique with the plot or other elements.

## Syntax and the Bible Student's Use of Language Tools

Number six is syntactical structure. A Greek-to-English interlinear helps us see the structure in Greek and relate it to English, so that we can see the structure as presented by the authors.

There is nothing wrong with a Bible student engaging with Greek words and syntax or consulting a concordance or lexicon. The Greek-to-English direction matters: it shows the original structure in relation to English rather than beginning with an English structure and treating that as the original. These are not exclusively for people taking degrees in biblical studies. Much of the work of scholarship is intended for Bible students generally to use in their hermeneutics. The writers give cautions about stretching it, but they expect you to use it.

Some people abuse these tools. I will not excuse myself; I have been guilty. We must learn carefulness and recognize our limitations. But abuse does not mean that we should throw away the baby with the bathwater. These tools are necessary and helpful. Learning to use them carefully is different from excluding ordinary believers from them. Recognizing that you have personal limitations does not require abandoning the opportunity to investigate what the author wrote.

For example, I would not appreciate Paul's “makes you wise” in the same way without referencing the words: *sophizō* means you become *sophos* and have *sophia*. A concordance, not just a dictionary, helps us see the related uses across texts, including what is said about Paul's wisdom. Without that, our study would be limited because we would not be studying the way the author wrote. Even without knowing the language directly, these tools can extend the scope of our study.

To make this the exclusive preserve of scholars is not wise. It tends toward the attitude in which only a few people are involved in studying Scripture. All of God's Word should be available to all of God's people. What we need is faithfulness in studying it.

## Phrases, Idioms, and Words

Number seven is phrases and idioms. If “he kicked the bucket” is being used idiomatically, you cannot interpret it by breaking it into independent words. Likewise, “taking for granted” is a term you must understand as a whole, not a reference to physically taking something.

Sometimes, beyond checking the words, you must recognize a phrase. Some argue that “weeping and gnashing of teeth” can be explained as a phrase expressing regret. I am not teaching that assertively here; it is an example. “Second death” is also one thing—destruction, damnation. Saying it is dying a second time misses a phrase. In breaking down the text, recognize when the unit is a phrase rather than separate words.

Number eight is words: dealing with what the words mean. Those are the parts we break down. Number nine is analytical conclusion: after explaining the parts and pieces, we put them together.

## Judgments Must Match the Evidence

Syntax matters because there are debates about whether a translation expresses the author's intended syntax. In First John 5:14–15, “If we ask anything according to his will, he hears us,” one reading is, “If you ask anything that is according to his will, he hears us.” Others argue for “If you ask anything, according to his will he hears us”—that it is his will to hear us. Syntax can change meaning.

This is where other things enter analytical conclusion. Deductively, what can it not mean based on everything else he has said? Inductively, what are the options? Abductively, which is most probably right? There are times when you cannot have an absolute answer. Do not overstate the text.

There are five measures of judgment: impossible, possible, plausible, probable, and certain. Not every judgment is certain. It is certain in Scripture that there was Jesus; other things are debatable. Something can be possible without being plausible. It could have happened, but is it likely? To say something is probable is to say that among the plausible options, this is the most probable. Certain means there is no question that this is the case.

Consider Herod being eaten by worms, without making a judgment or controversy here. Other historical documents affirm that he became sick. Someone can argue that he was eaten immediately and died that day. That is possible; a supernatural thing can happen. Historical context might support another reading as more probable: a connection between his walking in the flesh and becoming subject to that negative situation. We would need the teaching on morality to discuss how something can be God's judgment and still be a consequence. These matters are debatable. One might reason that a demonic cause is more probable, but that is not the same as certainty. Let us leave the controversy aside.

Make measured judgments when discussing word meanings or historical context, according to the available evidence. In First Timothy 2, concerning women and men, some explain the context by reference to Diana. You can say that is most probable; you cannot say it is certainly the explanation when there are different arguments. Do not impose a historical context for which you do not have all the arguments.

Similarly, some impose a historical context on the instruction about women not speaking in First Corinthians 14, without certainty of the connection. Call it plausible or probable. Then reason abductively about what the text can and cannot mean in light of everything else we know from the apostles' theology.

You should be able to identify the literary elements—the characters and plot—to a good degree. But do not overstate literary techniques or assume something that is not there until everything becomes allegory. Let your judgments be measured.

## Synthesis and Analytical Conclusion

Analytical conclusion is the logical putting together of each part to arrive at the meaning of the text. These words mean this; this phrase has this implication; this is the subject and that the object. Do not confuse those things.

Also, do not claim that the apostles' original writings lack clear pronouns or singularity and plurality. Greek and Hebrew have inflected forms that communicate such things. A base form in a concordance or a tool like MySword is not the inflected form as it was written. The inflected form may tell you past, present, or future, singularity or plurality of the subject, and sometimes the object. A noun's plurality is not simply the translator's decision. Be aware of those details before making claims.

Analytical conclusion involves synthesis—putting the parts together—and conclusion through deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning. In some texts, synthesizing the parts makes the meaning immediately clear. With other texts, the work of putting together the parts is not yet the whole conclusion: you must reason from the possibilities, comparing what they imply with what else is known. With other texts, you have options. Think of “jump the gun” in a race compared with someone jumping when a gun is fired at him. The words can look close without meaning the same thing.

Sometimes you can say definitely what a text means. Sometimes you induce several possibilities and abduct the most probable one. That is your job as an interpreter using good reasoning. You cannot do more than the evidence permits. Like someone judging a case, you decide on what has been brought before you. Some things become deductively clear; other things require you to consider the possibilities and select the most probable. You must know which conclusion you hold as certain, which as probable, and when alternatives are equally plausible.

The kind of reasoning affects the weight of judgment. Do not express a conclusion reached as merely the most likely option with the confidence you would give to something established as certain. Both kinds of judgment have their place, and the interpreter must distinguish them. If something is not deductive, it may not carry certainty; it may have probability or plausibility. In some far-off apologetic issues, a case is at best possible, and some of these are nonissues not worth the fuss.

## Corroboration and Contradictions

Number ten is addressing corroboration and contradictions. After establishing what a text means, examine other texts that corroborate it and address seeming contradictions.

Take Romans 8:11. Some say quickening our mortal body there means the Holy Ghost healing us today. It is not that the Holy Ghost does not heal us today. He is in us, and by faith we can put that power to work in our body, quickening it in the sense of healing. But Romans 8:11 is about the glorification of our body; its context is clear.

Having explained the text, you can show the Spirit's role described elsewhere in a way that corroborates that meaning. Someone may argue that “quickening” is used elsewhere in another sense. You must address the seeming contradiction. You have first examined the words, syntax, and phrases, synthesized the parts, and determined the meaning. Now you show other texts concerning the glorification of the body and deal with arguments that the text means something else.

This typically brings you to subject-matter truth: there is such a thing as the glorification of the body. As you examine other texts and their subject matters, you arrive at wholesome biblical truth.

The ten points are textual context, historical context, literary genre, literary elements, literary techniques, syntactical structure, phrases and idioms, words, analytical conclusion, and corroboration and contradiction. Following these with one text usually brings you to subject-matter truth. Following them across texts, their corroborations, and different subject matters brings you to wholesome biblical truth.

## Theology and Deliberate Interpretation

Faithfully following the process of Bible interpretation gives us biblical theology, together with philosophical theology and historical theology, producing thorough systematic theology that guides our practical theology. We do not ignore philosophical or historical theology. Historical theology looks at what others have said throughout church history, not only the history and context of the biblical text itself.

The process of Bible interpretation is primarily intuitive and secondarily intentional. Nobody is asking you to pause at every juncture and recite, “What is the textual context, historical context, literary genre?” You do not do that in conversation, yet you follow the same interpretive process. “When you read, you understand” assumes this ordinary awareness.

But the nature of some texts requires you to slow down deliberately. Being intuitive does not mean being inattentive; you are attentive as you read. Being intentional here means pausing to break down the syntax, words, and phrases. Sometimes you may use syllogism or symbolic logic, marking a subject X or treating a whole phrase as one symbol.

In Hebrews 6, for example, “It is impossible for those who were once enlightened…” introduces things you might group together. Then you examine “and they fall away,” asking whether it belongs to the first group. Breaking the text out in this way helps you be confident in your conclusion. Some texts require that deliberate work rather than simply proceeding intuitively.

There is a definite process of Bible interpretation. It is truth-determined and Scripture-instructed. It is what happens normally as a matter of truth, yet it is instructed in Second Timothy 2:15 and 3:15. We need it to arrive faithfully at the meaning of texts and the message of Scripture, for our growth and walk in God's purpose.

God bless you. Fulfill God's purpose, and see you on the next one.
