## The newness of life and natural life

[0:00]

We are looking at faith for natural life. By natural life, we mean things that may not immediately look spiritual: health, career and relationships. Is there a place for faith and the supernatural in those areas?

Ephesians 4 shows that the emphasis of the church is the new creation or the newness of life. Jesus gave the leaders to perfect the saints until they come to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. Verses 20–24 explain this as learning Christ, putting off the old man concerning former conduct, being renewed in the spirit of the mind, and putting on the new man created after God in righteousness and holiness of truth.

The emphatic realities are spiritual. Something has changed in your spirit, in who you are. When we speak of faith for natural life, we are speaking of the effect of that new life on natural things, not saying that the new life itself is merely natural.

Second Corinthians 5:17 says that the one in Christ is a new creation. The old has gone and the new has come. He who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with him. Romans 6:1–6 asks how we who died to sin can continue to live in it, and speaks of being baptized into Christ’s death, buried with him, and walking in newness of life as Christ was raised from the dead.

The old man was crucified with him. Our bodies did not change at salvation, so this refers to our spirit. Yet there is a danger of limiting the newness of life to the inward change and overlooking its implications for natural life. We rightly speak of love, righteousness and discipline, but there are also realities that touch health, preservation, deliverance and relationships—including supernatural orchestrations, not merely loving conduct.

## What is ours and how we walk in it

[8:06]

I saw a post saying that the realities of who we are in Christ are already accomplished and do not become ours through prayer or confession. That part is true. We do not pray to make Christ’s finished work happen. But the post did not point out that it takes faith to walk in what is ours. Its framing made prayer, faith and confession sound like attempts to acquire what we already possess, rather than means of walking in it.

Those of us who teach reckoning, standing, believing and praying are not saying that the believer does not already possess these realities. We are saying that you walk in them by faith. Knowing them matters: saying they are yours does not mean you will walk in them if you do not know them. Even someone who knows that love is in him may need prayer and the renewal of the mind to walk in it.

After establishing who we are, Romans 6:11 tells us to reckon ourselves dead to sin and alive to God. Verse 13 tells us not to present our members as instruments of unrighteousness, but to present ourselves to God in righteousness. There is a responsibility to reckon and present so that we walk. We have called that a faith stand.

Faith for natural life is still faith in the newness of life. Our faith in Christ is exercised in what that new life means, to the end that we walk in it. The question is how this relates to natural life.

## Life in us and the life we enter

[12:53]

The life we are speaking of is the life God offered man at inception, pictured in Genesis as the tree of life. It was offered, rejected, and will finally be consummated. It is not only something in us; it is also a reality we enter.

Matthew 7:14 speaks of the gate and way that lead to life. Matthew 19:16–17 records the question about having eternal life, and Jesus answers in terms of entering life. The life God gives is something in you and something you are in. We call it the newness of life because death became the default through man’s initial response, whereas life was what God originally offered.

John 5:24 says the one who hears and believes has everlasting life and has passed from death to life. The first expression looks inward; the second describes a movement into a different reality. First John 3:14–15 similarly says we have passed from death to life because we love the brethren, and then speaks of eternal life abiding in a person. Both descriptions are present.

There are internal and external realities in this life. The external realities can touch our natural life on the premise of the life in us.

## Correcting abuses without abandoning what is true

[19:04]

There have been periods in church history when the general expectation of supernatural intervention was low. People might say, “God will be with you,” or hope his will would be done, without much particular expectation of his power in natural affairs. That does not mean no miracles happened or no one expected them.

Catholics have had room in their doctrine for supernatural activity, including exorcism. Some of the Protestant reaction against supernatural claims came from real abuses and exploitation, including promises associated with indulgences. Cessationism became stronger in some settings, although not every reformer or Protestant tradition was the same. [19:58–21:18 — Some historical wording and names in the captions are unclear.]

We must acknowledge legitimate reasons for concern about abuse. Yet we can never first rid the whole world of abuse and excess before walking in what is true. If that is our condition, we will never walk in it. We must contend for what is right as accurately as possible.

Faith for natural life has been abused, exaggerated and exploited. That does not remove our responsibility to say accurately what God’s word teaches. What does the new life make available now, and what belongs to the end?

## Accomplished redemption, future immortality and present healing

[23:00]

Second Timothy 1:9–10 says God saved and called us according to his purpose and grace, given in Christ before time began and now revealed through the appearing of our Savior. Christ abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel.

If death has been abolished, why do we still see it? The mention of immortality shows that this cannot be reduced only to spiritual death. Some things Christ has accomplished are not yet to be effected in this age.

First Corinthians 15:20 onward says Christ is the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. As in Adam all die, in Christ all shall be made alive—but each in his own order: Christ first, then those who are his at his coming. Immortality has been won, but it is effected at his coming. It is an effect of redemption on natural life, although not one to be completed now.

The last enemy to be destroyed is death. The victory has been won in redemption; in its effecting, physical death is the last enemy removed. Our mortal bodies will become immortal. What can the Spirit do before then? Healing. We saw this in Jesus’s ministry. The same Spirit who will effect immortality effects healing now, removing sickness and disease rather than removing mortality in this age.

[29:02–29:31 — The captions contain reversed or broken mortality/immortality wording. The surrounding explanation consistently distinguishes present healing from future immortality. The quoted passage appears to be Acts 10:38, although the caption says “10:28”; part of that quotation is incomplete.]

God anointed Jesus with the Holy Ghost and power, and he went about doing good and healing. Healing is prominent, but doing good is broader. God works good, as we will also see in favor.

Even though healing is available, it needs to be effected. As with walking in love or righteousness, there is a place for faith. We reckon and present ourselves so we can walk in what belongs to us. Immortality, however, is effected by the Lord at the end.

## Preservation and deliverance in a corrupt world

[31:02]

Some people accept healing but question any other application of faith to natural affairs because of the excesses they have seen, particularly concerning abundance. Greed is a persistent problem: approaching natural things simply for self rather than as a steward. Those errors need correction. But we cannot honestly exclude other areas when Scripture shows God’s power working in needs, basic wants, relationships and other situations.

The second reality is preservation, including deliverance, which corresponds to the final incorruption of creation. First Corinthians 15 speaks of corruption and immortality, and Romans 8 makes the application to creation clear. [33:02 — The caption says “1 Corinthians 15 and verse 15”; the quotation that follows appears to be verse 50.]

Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom in its consummation; corruption does not inherit incorruption. There is a present sense of the kingdom, as Colossians 1:13 says we have been delivered from darkness and translated into the kingdom of the Son. But there is also its fullness, when the dead are raised incorruptible and the mortal puts on immortality.

Romans 8:19–21 says creation waits for the revealing of the sons of God. It was subjected to futility, yet in hope, because it will be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of God’s children. As immortality is effected in our bodies, incorruption is effected in creation.

What about the corrupt cosmos now, when it becomes, as we might say, sick? There is faith for preservation and deliverance in those circumstances. Matthew 8:23 onward records the tempest while Jesus slept in the boat. The disciples cried that they were perishing. He asked why they were fearful and of little faith, then rebuked the wind and sea. There was a great calm. He exercised faith and expected that they could have done so too. The final corruption of the world was not removed at that moment, but that threatening situation was dealt with.

Deliverance also concerns wicked and unreasonable people. The prayer that the word of the Lord have free course includes being delivered from unreasonable and wicked men, for not all have faith. [38:00 — The reference is abbreviated or garbled; it appears to be 2 Thessalonians 3:1–2.] There are terrible things people do, including capturing young people. There is faith for deliverance in such a world.

Second Corinthians 1:8–11 describes trouble in Asia beyond strength, even despairing of life. They had the sentence of death in themselves, so they would trust not in themselves but in God who raises the dead. God delivered them, does deliver them, and they trusted he would still deliver them. Others helped through prayer. This is real trust in the redeeming God for preservation and deliverance.

The new life changes our spirit and affects character and ministry, but it also has external realities touching natural life. In this world, our walk in God’s purpose needs healing, preservation, deliverance and favor. Even Jesus walked in these things.

## Favor and the distinct humanity of Christ

[42:24]

The third reality is favor. It is an encompassing word that includes provision, orchestration, attractiveness and distinctiveness. It is fundamental because, unlike remedies for sickness and danger, it would have existed even apart from the reign of sin and death.

Luke 2:52 says Jesus increased in wisdom and stature and in favor with God and men. He was truly human and grew in knowledge. He laid aside his mighty power and glory to function as man. Yet he had an advantage none of us had: he never began in the flesh, in the sense of the fallen state we have been describing.

My primary reason for believing that Jesus is God and that redemption had to be God’s work is philosophical necessity. Creation under sin, death and darkness cannot redeem itself; only the Creator can. Biblical and historical evidence matters, but this necessity should not be replaced by arguments that can be misused. For example, calming the storm alone is not a sufficient argument that he was acting uniquely as God, when in that same account he expected the disciples to act in faith.

[45:19–48:54 — Some phrasing in the extended philosophical argument and subsequent comparison is unclear.]

God did not stop being God. He chose to become man and function in his humanity. That includes growth. We must identify his distinct advantages without losing the truth of who we are in Christ, and without treating identification with him as pantheism.

The verb “increased” also applies to favor. The question is not whether God has become better disposed toward you, but whether you are walking in what is available. Your growth and walk can affect it. Favor “with men” clearly touches natural life, not only inward character.

## Favor, provision and persecution

[50:42]

Acts 2:46–47 describes the disciples continuing together, breaking bread, praising God and having favor with all the people. This was an outcome of discipleship, not merely generosity among themselves. They were attractive to others. Yet favor does not exclude persecution; God can work through persecution for good.

Exodus 11:3 says the Lord gave Israel favor in the Egyptians’ sight. Exodus 12:35–36 says they asked for silver, gold and clothing, and the Lord had given them favor so that their requests were granted. Sometimes you need to ask. That is not an instruction to beg and borrow everywhere, but to recognize that favor may already be present while you are afraid to ask—for help or even capital.

Does favor grant provision? Yes. Does it exclude persecution? No. The Egyptians still pursued them. Favor does not mean nothing will ever go wrong. When things go wrong, you can still trust God’s working for good. And as with faith concerning healing or love, one can be stronger or weaker in walking in this reality.

Acts 7:9–10 says God was with Joseph, delivered him from troubles, and gave him favor and wisdom before Pharaoh. Favor affects provision, orchestration and attractiveness. Do not habitually see yourself as the worst or expect nothing good. Be in faith.

We see favor in Jesus, in the church, and in Israel as a people typifying God’s purpose. In Acts 2, the Lord adding people to the church also shows its bearing on spiritual assignment. Matthew 28 promises his presence, and Mark 16 speaks of the Lord working with them and confirming the word with signs. There is backing for the mission, including the ministry of angels.

## Favor by the Spirit and through angels

[58:17]

Favor operates by the Spirit and through angels. Healing operates by the Spirit; preservation also involves the Spirit and angels. If we teach favor only as something mysterious done by angels, we may neglect the spiritual walk with which that ministry works.

Hebrews 13:2 says some have entertained angels without knowing it. Unfriendliness can frustrate an opportunity. I spoke with someone while preaching who treated friendliness as something he could simply choose not to practice. But we must learn to walk in it. Imagine an opportunity has been orchestrated and then bad behavior spoils it.

That is not a call to abandon discernment about strangers or dangerous situations. The humorous example about someone approaching at night makes that qualification. [1:00:41–1:01:25 — Parts of the exchange are unclear.] Generally, be friendly and have a good spirit. Walking in love and diligence is important to walking in favor.

Luke 2:40 says the child grew, became strong in spirit and was filled with wisdom, and God’s grace was upon him. Something attractive and distinctive accompanied that growth. Proverbs 3:1–4 likewise connects keeping the word, mercy and truth, binding them to the heart, with finding favor and good esteem in the sight of God and men.

A minister can affect the reach of his ministry by constantly attacking people. The fact that Paul corrected Peter does not justify treating everybody as Peter every day. This is not about playing to the gallery or seeking popularity. Care about doing what is right. Do not let a failure to walk in wisdom, love, righteousness and diligence obstruct what God wants to do through you.

Jesus’s friendliness made it possible for sinners and tax collectors to approach him, and for Nicodemus to come to him. Priscilla and Aquila took Apollos and explained the way more accurately. There is something about that spirit. Growing and walking in the Spirit enables rather than hinders the work of favor.

Proverbs 11:27 says the one who diligently seeks good finds favor, while trouble comes to the one seeking evil. Proverbs 22:29 speaks of a person who excels in his work standing before kings. In Proverbs, diligence belongs to wisdom and righteousness; laziness is condemned as foolishness. Grow, walk in love, be kind, diligent and disciplined. Refusing diligence while praying for favor is contradictory.

## God’s goodness, distinctiveness and leading

[1:08:47]

God makes his sun shine and rain fall on the just and unjust. Psalm 148 uses sun, moon, stars and waters above the heavens in its description of the heavenly host. We have argued that this imagery speaks of angels, and that the goodness Jesus describes can include supernatural orchestration, not only the natural ordering of the world. People who do not believe can also benefit as they act in line with what God has laid down.

Growing and walking in the Spirit affects attractiveness, distinctiveness and effectiveness. There can be natural distinctiveness from diligence, and there is also the particular grace God has given for your part. As you grow, it shows.

The leading of the Spirit also works favor. Jacob is mentioned as an example. [1:11:57–1:12:19 — The captions are broken here and say “Isaac stay in Egypt”; the intended location or example cannot be established from the text.] God may lead someone to stay or to relocate. The point is that his leading can work for a person’s good.

## Favor as God’s gracious working

[1:12:41]

Favor is the gracious and benevolent working of God’s goodness, life and light for someone’s pleasure or in fulfillment of his purpose. It is not earned. Compassion and mercy may be included, but it is not limited to pity. It can touch relationship as well as provision: God gave Isaac a wife for his pleasure.

We can describe this working as proactive, active and reactive. Exodus 23:20 says God sends an angel before the people to keep them in the way and bring them to the place he prepared. Going before them is proactive. Keeping and bringing them is active alongside them. Responding to a threatening situation is reactive.

The Spirit can also lead through a direction or an idea, even about a change of venue. In fulfilling God’s purpose, be confident of his provision for your own portion and measure. I walk in favor. The new life affects natural life through healing, preservation and favor.

## Natural welfare, productivity and generosity

[1:16:10]

There are cautions when speaking of favor and provision. God’s baseline is natural welfare or well-being. He is not saying everyone must have excess wealth. In some contexts, abundance concerns needs and basic wants being met as opposed to poverty, rather than great surplus.

If we pull everybody toward excess wealth, we can enthrone greed. Let people choose greater wealth as a matter of productivity rather than making a promise God did not make. Otherwise they become disappointed in God for not fulfilling something he never promised.

The primary way natural well-being is met is productivity, with God’s favor despite external conditions. Sometimes it is met through other people’s generosity; that too is biblical. But generosity is to be withheld from those who refuse to be productive. [1:17:32 — The trailing reference is unclear.]

Faith affects natural life through healing, preservation and favor. These touch natural assignments and spiritual assignments: reception of the gospel, people coming into church, relationships, the people we meet and the course of our days. There is such a thing as the newness of life affecting our natural life. Amen. On that note, we bring this teaching to a close.
