# Leadership in the Body of Christ

## The Body of Christ and God's Purpose

We're looking at leadership in the body of Christ. What we call the body of Christ is one of the descriptions of what God wants in his purpose. If we say God's purpose is sonship, we mean being his child and his heir. God's purpose is a family. One way that family is described is as the body of Christ.

Ephesians 1:9 says God has made known “the mystery of his will, according to his good pleasure which he purposed in himself.” His will is what he wants, his good pleasure is what he likes, and he has purposed it in himself. Verse 10 shows the end towards which this was directed: in the fullness of times, to gather together in one all things in Christ, both in heaven and on earth.

Read from verse 7 and you see the place of redemption: “In whom we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of his grace.” God knew there would be sin, and redemption would be necessary. He has done the work of redemption according to what was on his mind, so that he can gather us together in Christ.

The word behind “gather together in one” is anakephalaioō. I hope I'm pronouncing it correctly. The idea I want you to see is things being summed up under one head. A body has a head, and the other parts are summed up in that head. Paul refers back to this in Ephesians 1:22–23: God gave Christ “to be head over all things to the church, which is his body, the fullness of him that filleth all in all.” What is added up in Christ? His body.

God was seeking the point where he would bring about his family, the church, the body of Christ, with everything added up in Christ. Colossians 1:18 calls Jesus “the head of the body, the church,” the beginning and firstborn from the dead, who has the preeminence. When we say body of Christ, we are talking about the church. God wanted a family that is his.

## A Community Sharing the Same Reality

Ephesians 2:19 describes the church in family terms: we are no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Jesus Christ as the chief cornerstone. He is the head of the corner, the one to whom the building is joined.

In him the whole building is fitted together and grows into a holy temple. We are built together as a dwelling place of God in the Spirit. We can call this the body of Christ or God's family. It is what God was heading towards: a community sharing his life and nature, summed up in Christ the head.

There are two ways the image of a body can be used. It may emphasize different parts with different responsibilities, or it may emphasize the same reality shared by all. Both pictures apply to the church. A body of lawyers or a body of doctors is a community sharing an identity. In that sense, the body of Christ is a community sharing the same life and nature.

Think of the Nigerian Medical Association as a body of doctors, or the Nigerian Bar Association as a body of lawyers. In calling it a body, you are speaking of the identity its members share as doctors or lawyers. The same word body can describe a community sharing one reality, as well as a community with different functions and responsibilities.

## The Spiritual Family in Heaven and on Earth

Most of what we have said so far describes a spiritual reality. We are members of God's household and his dwelling place. Ephesians speaks of those in heaven and on earth, which can include people who have passed from the body and whose engagement is now heavenly rather than earthly or physical. Ephesians 3:14–15 speaks of the whole family in heaven and on earth. There is still one community, although some are no longer engaged on earth.

Hebrews 12:22–23 shows this as well. We have come to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, an innumerable company of angels, “the general assembly and church of the firstborn who are registered in heaven,” to God the Judge of all and “the spirits of just men made perfect.” We are part of this church of the firstborn, these called-out ones, ekklēsia, headed up in Christ.

Hebrews 11 had spoken of people who were just by faith but could not be made perfect without us. They were perfected with us at the resurrection. The Lord Jesus, through his resurrection, brought about God's family, a community sharing the life and nature of God. He became the kind after which all of us are. We share his life and nature because of the redemptive work he did, the redeeming God made incarnate for redemption.

Notice that Hebrews 12 includes both the church of the firstborn and the spirits of just men made perfect in the place to which we have come. This is not a different family for people who have passed from the body. It is the same community, sharing what Christ has accomplished, although their engagement is now heavenly rather than earthly.

The body of Christ, the church, the family in heaven and on earth, God's dwelling place: these are ways of describing what God wanted in his purpose.

## The Church Christ Built Through Redemption

Matthew 16:18 presents this spiritual community: “On this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it.” Jesus speaks of building his church through redemption, bringing about the gathering which is God's family.

Death cannot stop it. The gates of Hades speak of death and its authority, death in all its ramifications, including the first and second death. We have been brought out of death. Hades has nothing on us, and the second death has nothing on us. Christ has built his church; death did not prevail against it and cannot prevail against it.

People also use this as an ongoing assurance that persecution and the evils of this world cannot prevail against the church. There is nothing wrong with believing that. We are people who share his life. But the primary truth in the passage is the direct work Christ did. Death and its power did not and cannot prevail against the community he brought about through redemption.

## The Natural Community Instituted by the Lord

The body of Christ is not only a spiritual community. It is also a natural community, an organization and an institution. Matthew 16 emphasizes the spiritual community. Matthew 18 speaks of the church in a way which requires a natural community.

Someone may object to calling the church an institution or say, “I don't like organized religion; I just do spirituality.” But what do you want, disorganized religion? Sometimes people are trying to identify a problem without identifying it precisely. They see something wrong in one place and use one brush to paint everything.

“The church is full of hypocrites.” There are all kinds of human beings in the church, just as you are a human being who needs to grow. Everybody needs to be perfected and trained. One more imperfect person will not ruin it. We must not use the fact that people need growth to dismiss the community in which the Lord intends them to receive it.

Jesus did not only found a spiritual community which people enter by faith. He also laid down the order of a natural community. That is the sense in which I call it an institution: he instituted it.

## Forgiveness, Reconciliation and Accountability

Matthew 18:15 says that if your brother sins against you, tell him his fault between you and him alone. If he hears you, you have gained him. If he will not hear, take one or two more so that the matter is established by two or three witnesses. If he still refuses, tell it to the church.

We often say we forgive before people repent, whether they repent or not. That is true in one sense. You can go to the person and correct him because you are not holding a grudge against him. You have forgiven him in that sense. But there is another forgiveness which restores fellowship, and that requires repentance.

In 1 Corinthians 5, the man who took his father's wife could be loved without the church holding a grudge against him. Yet there was a restoration of fellowship which could not rightly be given while he refused to repent. If you give that acceptance without repentance, you become involved in the wrong.

Even when someone refuses to repent, you still love him. Matthew 5 requires us to love our enemies, bless them, pray for them and do good to them. We can say, “I love you, but the relationship is not right, because you need to repent.” There is no contradiction. Unconditional fellowship or acceptance is not what the Bible teaches, and it can be dangerous.

Some people mistake walking in love for docility. They accept everything, then complain that God is putting them through it. Love does not prevent justice. You can love an abusive person and still seek justice. Do not let anyone gaslight you about that. Love for the people who may otherwise be harmed can also require consequences, so that the wrongdoing changes or is prevented from affecting others.

For example, where a terrorist has caused serious harm, I believe the government should carry out justice, including capital punishment in such a case. That does not mean I do not love him or wish he were saved. Even repentance does not remove the consequence of what he has done. There is a place for deterrence and retribution. We must not confuse love with the cancellation of justice.

Love does not take away discipline or displeasure. It has a way of expressing displeasure. The Lord's instruction is to bring up the fault, seek resolution, involve others when necessary, and finally bring the matter to the church. If the person will not hear, he must face the consequences of his action.

## Ecclesial Authority in Matthew 16, 18 and 28

When Jesus says to tell it to the church, church leadership is in view. He goes on to speak of binding and loosing, which concerns authority. This cannot mean only an invisible spiritual community. There must be a natural community capable of hearing and addressing the matter.

The people listening could understand taking a matter to the leadership of the synagogue to which they belonged. But Jesus also shows the application to his church: if two agree on earth concerning what they ask, it will be done by his Father, and where two or three gather in his name, he is in their midst. The gathering is in the light of his redemptive role, around the truth of the redeeming God.

Matthew 16 foreshadows the founding of the church as a spiritual community. Matthew 18 foreshadows the institution of its natural community. In Matthew 28, we see him institute it with a commission: “All authority is given to me in heaven and on earth. Go therefore and make disciples.”

Notice that he does not say, “All authority is given to me; therefore go and cast out demons.” This passage is primarily about ecclesial authority, the authority of the church and its leadership. Whenever this heaven-and-earth authority has appeared in these passages, it has concerned the church.

In Matthew 16, after promising to build his church, he gives the keys of the kingdom and speaks of binding and loosing on earth and in heaven. In Matthew 18, he speaks of hearing the church, then again of binding and loosing. In Matthew 28, he tells the apostles to go in the light of his authority and make disciples.

Verse 16 identifies the eleven who went to the appointed mountain in Galilee. The authority would extend to Matthias later, making the twelve. Jesus says that all authority is his, and they are to go in the light of it. There may be implications for believers' authority, but the direct issue is ecclesial authority and the commission of discipleship.

We are already God's family spiritually, whether we are physically gathered or not. We recognize others who share our faith and rejoice in that relationship. Yet the Lord also instituted a natural community, and he instituted it on the premise of making disciples.

The authority and the commission therefore come together. “All authority is given to me” leads to “go therefore and make disciples.” The Lord authorizes the apostles to act in the light of his authority. That is the basis for the natural community's work of discipleship, not merely an association people decided to form after believing.

## Discipleship and the Church's Mission

Acts 2 shows this in action. Three thousand received the word and were baptized. They continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine, fellowship, breaking of bread and prayers. They met daily in the temple and from house to house, eating with gladness and simplicity of heart, praising God and having favour with the people. Then the text says the Lord added daily to the church those who were being saved.

He calls this natural community the church. It is centred on the apostles' teaching and discipleship. The body of Christ is a family, but it is also an institution and organization based on the commission of discipleship. That commission has a mission, something the Lord intends the church to accomplish.

Acts 9:31 speaks of churches throughout Judea, Galilee and Samaria having peace, being edified and multiplying as they walked in the fear of the Lord and the comfort of the Holy Spirit. We see several such communities in different places.

A believer is therefore not only a member of a spiritual community. He ought also to belong to a natural community where he receives discipleship and walks in its effect, carrying out God's purpose and the mission of the church.

## Authority to Build Up, Not Destroy

Leadership is one of the major features, if not the primary feature, of the body of Christ as a natural community or institution. The Lord instituted it with leadership authority. We saw that in Matthew, and Paul refers to it in 2 Corinthians 13:10: he writes while absent so that when present he will not have to use sharpness, according to the authority the Lord gave him for edification and not destruction.

Sharpness can be used, but it is not the primary way we function. First, we explain persuasively what is wrong and why it is wrong. Then we remind correctively: “I told you that was not right. Why are you doing it again?” Sharp rebuke goes further. Expressions such as stiff-necked, hardened or hypocritical address the person's persistence in the wrong more sharply.

In the first case, you explain: “This is not okay, and this is why.” When the person does it again, your tone may change as you remind him of what has already been explained. Sharpness is a further step, not the way to begin every interaction. Even when the words are strong, the purpose has not changed from building the person up to destroying him.

Even then, be measured. A biblical use of “fool” does not make it a daily way of speaking. The authority is for edification, not destruction. Do not destroy people so they lose themselves in the process. First Corinthians 4:21 asks, “Shall I come to you with a rod, or in love and a spirit of gentleness?” The default expression is gentleness, rather than the rod.

Second Corinthians 10:8 again speaks of the authority the Lord gave for edification, not destruction. This is ecclesial authority. It is distinct from the authority belonging to believers as believers. We must recognize both without confusing them.

In verse 14, Paul says he is not overextending himself, as though his authority did not reach them, because he came to them with the gospel. If someone brings the gospel to you, you believe and submit to him for discipleship, there is an ecclesial authority in that relationship.

But define its scope. It is not authority over your entire life, telling you everything to do. It concerns the order of the church and discipleship. Questions of submission and accountability need to be understood in that light. The point here is that leadership with authority from the Lord is a feature of the church he instituted.

## Grace for Every Member's Measure of Leadership

This provision was not only for the first apostles. The Lord designed it across generations. Ephesians 4:7 says, “To each one of us grace was given according to the measure of Christ's gift.” Each of us. We must identify Christ's gift to understand the measure according to which grace is given.

Verse 8 says that when he ascended on high, he led captivity captive and gave gifts to men. He is speaking of people being given as gifts to humanity. The one who ascended first descended into the lower parts of the earth. Here we see an Exodus picture: passing through the sea, then coming up onto the mountain. He ascended that he might fill all things.

Then he gave apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers. What did Christ give? Leadership. Apostles are sent; prophets speak for him; evangelists announce or herald the message; pastors and teachers shepherd through teaching. These five or four terms describe the leadership Christ gives to the world.

We will develop the detail elsewhere, but rather than treating these as separate terms for different people, I am speaking of them as descriptions of every leader in the church. Each member has grace according to his own measure of this leadership gift. When you carry out the church's work, preach the gospel and people believe, you can lead them in discipleship in God's purpose.

Leadership is therefore built into the body of Christ. It is not merely an occasional discretionary appointment. There is room to discuss discretion and election, but the design already contains grace for each person's measure of responsibility. We need the whole picture.

If we only ask when Jesus handed authority to the first twelve, we can miss the continuing design. Ephesians says “each of us,” according to the measure of Christ's gift. The provision is not exhausted in those first recipients. As the mission continues and people are discipled, there is grace for the responsibility they are enabled to carry.

As someone exercises leadership and perfects you, you are enabled to do the work of ministry. Then, by the grace in you, you can perfect others. That is the design: each member functioning in the leadership gift according to his own measure as the church carries out its mandate to the world.

## Psalm 68 and Leadership Responsibility

Ephesians 4:8 draws on Psalm 68. Verse 17 speaks of God's chariots in their thousands and the Lord among them at Sinai, in the holy place. The preceding context is Israel's Exodus. They have come through the sea to Sinai, accompanied by the ministry of angels. This resembles the picture in Hebrews 12, with the innumerable company of angels among God's people.

“You have ascended on high, you have led captivity captive; you have received gifts among men.” There is a discussion about “captivity captive.” It can picture the former captors displayed as captives in victory. I have also leaned towards the people formerly in captivity becoming his captives, but I now think that argument is weaker in the light of other arguments. I am not prepared to develop that fully today.

The point here is receiving gifts among men, even the rebellious, so that the Lord might dwell there. God has brought these people out, and they are to be gifts among humanity, carrying his work even among the rebellious, so that he might dwell there.

“Blessed be the Lord, who daily loads us.” The supplied words “with benefits” can conceal the point about a burden or responsibility. He gives us responsibilities. When he brought Israel from Egypt to Sinai, he gave them responsibility as a kingdom of priests, the responsibility of leadership towards the world.

Ephesians 4 gives a similar picture. Each of us has grace for his measure of responsibility, serving so that God's dwelling is extended among people. In the Great Commission, there is potential for everyone to end up a spiritual leader. Leadership grace is available according to the measure of the gift and responsibility in which we function.

## A Good Desire Requires Qualification

Spiritual leadership is a goal we ought to have in our spiritual assignment. It is not the entire goal; our goal is to do everything God wants us to do. But if I faithfully carry out his will, I should expect to grow into spiritual leadership.

First Timothy 3:1 calls the desire for oversight a good desire: “If a man desires the position of a bishop, he desires a good work.” Wanting to push forward God's purpose and the mission of the church, even through greater leadership responsibility, is not wrong.

But the passage immediately gives qualifications: blameless, husband of one wife, temperate, sober-minded, of good behaviour, hospitable and able to teach. He desires a good work, but he must be qualified. Leadership is a possibility for every member as he carries out his spiritual assignment, and it is meant to unfold through discipleship which qualifies him, together with God's orchestrations.

Every believer ought to desire and expect leadership in the church. As I am perfected as a saint, I should grow to be able to function as a leader. The perfecting of the saints leads to the work of ministry, and that work leads to the edification and increase of the body.

This is why desire and preparation belong together. The desire for oversight is called good, and immediately the same passage says what the overseer must be. We should not condemn the desire as though wanting responsibility in God's work were itself wrong. Neither should we use the good desire to pass over the qualifications the passage gives.

## Doing Your Share While Focusing on Discipleship

Ephesians 4:16 describes the body joined and knit together by what every joint supplies, according to the effective working by which every part does its share. Every part has a share. In the work of ministry, we carry out the assignment God has allotted to us, our own inheritance and responsibility.

That effective working is God's grace. As I function in grace and do my share, people are joined to the body. The supply of the ligaments comes through the grace at work in each person's ministry, causing the body's growth and its edification in love.

Should leadership itself become my focus? No. My focus should be discipleship. If God has given me a spiritual assignment, I need to be discipled so that I can carry it out faithfully. I need to receive discipleship in the local church. Leadership is something to expect as I grow and fulfil my assignment, not a position to pursue apart from preparation.

## Qualification, Accountability and the Danger of Exploitation

If you put yourself into leadership before you are qualified, you will have to face what follows. If you know you are not qualified and someone offers you responsibility, you should say so and explain the particular reason. This does not mean rejecting leadership indiscriminately. It is good to desire and expect it. But desire does not remove the need for qualification.

If you know an immoral tendency could become a snare to the church or harm people, say, “I have this tendency; I am not prepared to do this yet.” If you accepted leadership and then discover yourself acting that way, be the first to acknowledge it and step away while you are prepared properly.

Perhaps there is a danger that you will steal money or exploit people sexually. Financial exploitation, sexual exploitation and exploitation of people's purpose, lives and time are very serious dangers. Time is a way of describing life. You must not misuse people's time, finances or sexuality. Do not treat these as small matters because you want a leadership position.

You may not be qualified yet, while believing you can become qualified later. Hold on and allow the Lord to mould, shape and train you. You can say, “I know you have called me to this, but because of my past or my present condition, I am not ready to do it properly.” Then submit to the preparation you need.

When you are ready, you can serve as a vessel prepared for the Master and useful for every good work. Jumping in too early and trying to become a spiritual leader at all costs is dangerous, especially without accountability. If you know you are not capable, do not play around with that.

The devil you are playing with is not playing with you. When things fall apart, you may look back and wonder whether it was worth it. You could have been patient and done what was right.

There is leadership in the body of Christ. You have leadership grace and your own measure. Be faithful to your measure. Receive discipleship. Unfold and grow and fulfil God's purpose.
