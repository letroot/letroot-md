# Discipline: The Drive of the Spirit

## Knowing God's Purpose and Seeking Its Fulfilment

Our Father, I am so glad that I am your child and a part of your family. In this meeting, gathered with my siblings, I receive of your word light, truth, revelation and growth by the knowledge of Christ and his work in all its fullness, and by the knowledge of him who is in me, in the name of the Lord Jesus. Amen.

We are looking at discipline, the drive of the Spirit. I have talked about this a couple of times, but I feel that my understanding of it is better, and it is something I want to share with you.

One of the earliest things we ought to know in the Christian faith is God's purpose for our lives. For some people, the journey begins with agreeing that there is God. Some of us went through that journey. Usually, the first two things you know are the truth of God's existence and the truth of redemption. You believe in the reality of God and the redemption he has accomplished in Christ. The next thing of great importance is the reality of God's purpose.

When you know the truth of God and redemption, you have answered the question of what this world is, what this existence you have found yourself in is like and how it came about. You have also begun to answer why it is the way it is, with this contradiction of good and evil, life and death, light and darkness. But the next question is, “Why am I here? Why did God make me in the first place? Now that he has redeemed me, what am I supposed to do?” Why he made us is what he has redeemed us to. When you know God's purpose, your greatest aim should be to fulfil it.

Paul explains in Ephesians 1:9 that God has made known to us the mystery of his will, what he wants, according to his good pleasure, what he likes, which he purposed in himself. God's purpose is what he wants that he likes. Think of something you ordinarily like and then begin to crave. Now you want it. Yet you can ignore that desire. Perhaps you are trying to budget and save, so you decide not to get it.

That should be a financial principle too. It is not everything you like, or even everything you crave, that you must get. You are not God; you are man. This is a serious matter for all of us. There are also times when you can say, “I can splurge,” and it is all right to enjoy. First Timothy 6:17 gives us that perspective. But God's purpose is his pleasure which became his will and his determination.

## Predestination Leads to Sonship

Ephesians 1:5 speaks of his having predestined us. Suppose you write the university entrance examination because you want to study law. Afterwards, you know you have done poorly and are unlikely to get the result you need. Yet studying law remains your good pleasure, will and determination. You decide to take A levels as another route.

You are predetermining a way to the goal ahead of a possibility. You are forecasting, not foreknowing. I am not entirely sure how that entry route works for law, but the illustration is about a response which preserves the original intention.

What God predetermined to do is not sonship; it leads to sonship. He predestined redemption. He predestined us to sonship by Jesus Christ, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love, as verse 4 says. Bringing us back into that blameless state through redemption is what he predestined.

Just as choosing an alternative admission route reveals what the student initially wanted, the destination of redemption reveals God's good pleasure. If the predestination leads to sonship, then his good pleasure, will and purpose itself is sonship.

## Sonship Means Being God's Child and His Heir

Romans 8:15 says we have received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry, “Abba, Father.” We emphasize sonship because Paul's idea was the placement of an actual child, whereas modern adoption makes us think of taking as a child someone who was not one's child. Sonship better expresses the point here.

Verse 16 says the Spirit bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God. We are born of God, to fellowship with him, relate with our siblings and walk in his nature and character, including his power.

Verse 17 goes further: if children, then heirs. God did not only want a child; he wanted an heir. That is what you must understand about being in his image. An heir receives what another person owns, with the responsibility belonging to it.

Think of a wealthy person such as Dangote making his child an heir. He does not merely give the child money. He can give ownership of the companies, so that the child becomes responsible for directing them. In the same way, being God's heir means receiving what he owns, the world. That should change your understanding of your role on earth. You begin taking responsibility for the world and the way it is.

## Our Responsibility in the Cosmos

Some people who talk about national transformation have part of this in mind. There is a way to approach it without giving the wrong idea about the gospel's mission. Our mission is not so much a national issue as a cosmos issue. Of course, you cannot talk about the cosmos without including the nation. We are supposed to manage the world in the direction of life, as much as it is up to us. We cannot do that exhaustively, because darkness will get worse even as light gets brighter until the end of the age. Nevertheless, we should understand that we are supposed to manage the cosmos.

There are problems on both sides of this conversation, and I want to handle it carefully. I want to address the individual side before the collective side. You are an heir of the world and should participate in the cosmos, even in your own microcosm: your community, nation or environment. That includes such things as voting. Once you accept that individual participation, you cannot necessarily reject the same concern on a higher scale. We should strive to manage the cosmos the way God wants and exemplify that.

You cannot do it perfectly. No matter how you get involved in government, you are still the beast. You must govern as a representative of the entire people, including unrighteous people, which means you must still sustain unrighteousness. The system the Lord will meet when he comes will still be a system of unrighteousness.

God's purpose is sonship, with these two main realities: child of God and heir of God. As heirs, we understand our assignment in two aspects, natural and spiritual. With natural assignment, I usually speak of academics, career, business and social causes. Here I would also include civil engagement. It is not quite natural assignment in the same sense, but we can put it on that side. Engaging with the cosmos in that way belongs to your place as a son of God, an heir of the world who is to inherit the earth.

Our sonship will do its full work when it brings the cosmos out of corruption into incorruption. That is the main thing. Nevertheless, we are still to engage with the cosmos today out of our philosophical persuasion, out of what some would call our religious belief.

## Religion, Devotion and Human Design

People say, “Religion has blinded people,” or, “This is not religion; it is the kingdom.” Often this is semantics. I used to think they meant self-righteous legalism when criticizing religion, but sometimes I cannot tell what they mean. Someone who says, “I do spirituality,” may still mean a methodical system of belief and practice.

Religion concerns devotion. Even people who do not believe in God find expressions of devotional life: causes they advocate, regular meditation, someone leading it and daily things to consider. Functioning metaphysically as well as naturally belongs to human design.

James speaks of pure religion in caring for widows and orphans. People who do not believe in God also care for the earth, animals or minorities. The point is not to criticize those concerns, but to recognize that human beings find devotional expressions.

We need not speak against religion simply as religion. If we mean self-righteous legalism, that is a problem. A metaphysical system, or even a natural one, built on undue pressure and forced compliance can produce hypocrisy, rebellion and frustration. Be clear about what is being criticized.

## What We Maintain and What We Pursue

God's purpose is sonship. You are his child and his heir, with a natural assignment and a spiritual assignment. Whenever people hear about purpose, they tend to ask, “What am I meant to pursue and fulfil? What am I meant to accomplish?” That concern for pursuit and fulfilment is part of human design.

It is almost as though that spiritual, metaphysical design also has a physiological expression. You see an old, frail person who is living for something and seems to keep going until it is done. I am not saying this happens in every case, but people often seem to survive while they have something to pursue. It is almost physiological.

When we speak of purpose in that sense of pursuit and fulfilment, it is our assignment, our place as heirs of God, that accounts for it. Being God's child involves fellowship with the Father. There is something to maintain there, rather than an assignment to finish. I maintain my relationship with my siblings and relate well with other people whom I want to become part of our family. There are things we maintain in purpose and things we pursue and fulfil in purpose.

I relate with people who are in God's likeness, desiring them to be saved and to be in his image. But what accounts for our pursuits and their fulfilment is our place as heirs, the assignments God gives us. I have things to do in this cosmos: people to preach to so they can be saved, people to disciple so they can walk in God's purpose. I have a natural assignment to fulfil, serving people, making things better and moving things forward as someone who has God's life should.

In that pursuit of purpose, one character trait is at the heart of it. That is what we are studying: discipline, the drive of the Spirit. We said in the teaching on the hidden man that our fulfilment of purpose rises and falls on the hidden man. More specifically, fulfilling our assignment rises and falls on discipline.

I am speaking about discipline from the standpoint of the new man. People can be disciplined without the Spirit; they can be disciplined by the flesh. There is a discipline which is the drive of the flesh, and a discipline which is the drive of the Spirit.

## Paul's Natural and Spiritual Assignments

Our main text is 1 Corinthians 9:24–27, but first we need to see what leads up to it. Paul makes a case for continuing his natural assignment even though he could put it aside. Why does he say that?

He begins, “Am I not an apostle? Am I not free? Have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord? Are you not my work in the Lord? If I am not an apostle to others, yet doubtless I am to you. For you are the seal of my apostleship.” He establishes his apostleship, then asks, “Do we have no right to eat and drink?” He was given to what people now call a fasted life. We do not know that he fasted always, but he said that he fasted often. You can learn that.

He also asks, “Do we have no right to take along a believing wife, as do also the other apostles, the brothers of the Lord, and Cephas?” Paul was so consumed with and committed to his mission that he set aside his right to marry. Then he asks, “Or is it only Barnabas and I who have no right to refrain from working?” That brings us to his natural assignment.

“Who ever goes to war at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Or who tends a flock and does not drink of the milk of the flock?” Normally, we should be paid in our spiritual assignment. Those to whom spiritual things have been sown should give material support. Paul and Barnabas still received support, but they did not stop working, and he was not demanding it.

This also shows that people who choose a different model are not necessarily wrong. There is a way to balance that model, and we will consider it at the right time. For some people, the nature of their commitment requires full-time work in the spiritual assignment. This calls for thinking on the truth.

Consider the scale of what pursuing the mission involves today. To disciple the nations effectively, you need to write books. You need people editing and doing many other things, some of which require full-time attention. Many things can be done part-time, but in my opinion, if you want to be effective, you need a group doing some things full-time. There is a righteous way to approach that.

## The Right to Receive Material Support

Paul has used natural examples: warfare, planting and animal husbandry. Then he asks, “Do I say these things as a mere man? Or does not the law say the same also?” This is what I often talk about: he has used natural truth, and now he uses revelatory truth.

The law of Moses says, “You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain.” Paul asks whether God is concerned with oxen or whether he says it for our sakes. He is taking the natural principle within revelatory truth to its conclusion: the one who ploughs should plough in hope, and the one who threshes should share in the harvest. “If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great thing if we reap your material things? If others are partakers of this right over you, are we not even more?”

The case is clear. Yet Paul says, “Nevertheless we have not used this right, but endure all things lest we hinder the gospel of Christ.” He does not want anything to create friction in freely communicating the truth of God's word. That should be the natural disposition of a minister. Nevertheless, if your eyes are on fulfilling the mission, you must consider how much attention your natural assignment can take, particularly when your election and calling require more of you spiritually.

Everybody has a spiritual assignment, but Paul's election and calling are not the same as those of any other disciple. Other people should also grow towards responsibility, becoming deacons and bishops, but the demands may differ. Paul says he has not used this right, and he endures so that he will not hinder the gospel.

He then points to those who minister holy things and eat from the temple, and those who serve at the altar and partake of its offerings. “Even so the Lord has commanded that those who preach the gospel should live from the gospel.” If we want to be honest with Scripture, I do not think we can escape the truth that people can earn fully and exclusively from spiritual assignment. There is good reason for it.

## Holding Together Right, Honour and the Mission

Paul says he has not used this right. Instead of enforcing it, he treats support as a matter of honour. We need to balance those things. Someone just starting a church work and immediately demanding support as a right is suspect, but that does not remove the truth of the right.

Pastoral work takes people's time, and time is work, life, result and reward. A pastor can plan a day's work, then spend an hour with someone's parent about a concern and another hour with the person concerned. Those are realities, not imaginary examples. More conversations follow, taking the time planned for other work. To say that such work should not be paid is not sensible. Some people make ungodly demands, but we must remain biblical.

Paul says he is not writing to demand that the Corinthians pay him, and would rather die than have this boasting made void. Yet a leader in that large church should consider whether his explanation exposes a failure in their own conduct, perhaps selfishness. His refusal to demand support is not a reason to ignore the truth he has explained.

Necessity is laid on him: he must preach the gospel. He also continues his natural assignment. Keep both in view. The main conversation leading to discipline is not about fasting or marriage, but his natural assignment alongside his spiritual assignment.

If he does the work willingly, he has a reward; if unwillingly, he is still entrusted with a stewardship. His reward is to preach without charge, without enforcing the right or abusing his authority in the gospel.

## Becoming a Servant for the Gospel

Verse 19 says, “Though I am free from all men, I have made myself a servant to all, that I might win the more.” Notice the movement. Paul has been saying, “I will do my natural assignment so that my spiritual assignment is not affected.” Now he describes his passion for that spiritual assignment: “I am free from all, but I have made myself a servant to all.”

Both points matter. When he speaks about discipline, we must realize that the discipline applies to his natural and spiritual assignments together. He is showing his engagement with both. There is another way we will see this, but that is the first.

“To the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might win Jews; to those who are under the law, as under the law, that I might win those who are under the law; to those who are without law, as without law,” although he is not without law towards God but under law towards Christ, “that I might win those who are without law.” To the weak he became weak, so that he could win the weak.

That is relevant even to writing books. If you know that people are weak in reading, and people in this generation are becoming weaker at it, you must give that serious consideration. “I have become all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.”

He does it for the gospel's sake, so that he may be a partaker of it with them. They can be fellow sharers in it, able to receive it without friction or something hindering their reception. That leads to verse 24.

## Running to Obtain the Prize

“Do you not know that those who run in a race all run, but one receives the prize? Run in such a way that you may obtain it.” Is his illustration spiritual or natural? It is a natural illustration. He applies it to spiritual things, but he begins with what happens in athletics and competition.

The reason he tells them to run so as to obtain is connected with verse 23, where he wants to be a partaker with them. What he is experiencing, they should begin to experience; they will be pursuing together. The level of their calling may differ, particularly in their spiritual assignments, but they should run to obtain the prize.

“Everyone who competes for the prize is temperate in all things.” The expression agōnizomai, rendered striving for mastery, means competing. The person who competes is self-controlled or disciplined in all things. That is what enables the athlete to win. “Now they do it to obtain a perishable crown, but we for an imperishable crown.”

This applies to our natural and spiritual assignments. Paul wants to continue working so that his spiritual assignment is not hindered, yet he must also fulfil that spiritual assignment to receive the crown. Engagement with both assignments is described as a race in which we are to obtain a prize. What is needed? Temperance, self-control or discipline in all things.

## Clear Goals and a Clear Plan

“Therefore I run thus: not with uncertainty. Thus I fight: not as one who beats the air.” What must be clear? Your goals. But there are two things here: a goal and a plan for fulfilling it.

If you are running towards a destination, you must know how to get there. If you are fighting to win, you must also know how you intend to win. A boxer does not train only with a goal, but also with a plan. “I need to land a punch, but the other person is defending himself. First I will do this to distract him; then I will do that.” There is a goal and a plan.

Once Paul speaks about acting without uncertainty, both things are involved. A plan is the process or order for getting something done. The adverb adēlōs means uncertainly. The underlying word joins the negative with dēlos, clear, evident or manifest.

What I want to do must be clear, and how I intend to do it must be clear. The goals and the plans are evident. This is part of discipline, the drive of the Spirit. It is not only that I have a general desire. I know the destination and the way I am going towards it.

## The Body Brought into Subjection

Verse 27 adds another important detail: “I discipline my body and bring it into subjection, lest, when I have preached to others, I myself should become disqualified.” The King James wording speaks of keeping the body under. There are two expressions: disciplining the body and bringing it into subjection.

The first, hypōpiazō, might make you think of training, dealing seriously with the body to prepare it for something. Have you seen what people put their bodies through in training? I saw someone performing an unusual balancing act. The performance looked elegant and normal when you saw it, but the person's legs showed the bruising endured while learning it. The body had been trained to do what was required.

But the second expression, doulagōgeō, bringing it into subjection, has the idea of slave-driving. That suggests that the whole framework here may be one of slavery. The first expression about beating the body into submission may not primarily describe difficult training to acquire an ability. It may describe a slave master bringing someone under subjection to execute a task.

“I discipline my body; I slave-drive myself, lest after preaching to others I become disqualified.” Why does he say that? Because they have become partakers in the same race. He has told them to run so that they obtain. Others might go ahead and obtain while he himself becomes disqualified from obtaining the reward of his natural and spiritual assignments. What would cause that? Lack of discipline.

Fulfilling God's purpose rises and falls on discipline. After naming discipline, Paul describes it. He runs without uncertainty and does not fight as someone beating the air: he is clear about his goals and plans. Then he disciplines his body and brings it into subjection.

Is slave-driving a new concept introduced only at that point, or is it a picture he has been working with? We can argue that he uses a slavery metaphor for how discipline works. The master makes the goal clear, subjects the slave to the process and drives him through its execution. “You must get this done.” The goal, the process and the doing all fit that description.

## Pharaoh's Taskmasters and the Daily Quota

Exodus 5 illustrates the relationship between a slave master, the goal and its execution. Moses and Aaron ask Pharaoh to let the people go into the wilderness to worship. Pharaoh refuses, asks who Yahweh is that he should obey him, and accuses them of distracting the people from their labour.

The initial request is expressed as going to worship. There are possible scholarly reconstructions concerning that request, the people's earlier crying to God and their eventual departure. Perhaps there was already movement towards worship, and the request represented an initial compromise. Such questions are different from dismissing the revelation. Critical scholarship can serve reconstruction rather than mere fault-finding, although some approach it with unbelief.

In our application, the Spirit is the master.

But Paul did not mean that every disciplined athlete necessarily has the Spirit. There is discipline which is not of the Spirit. Greed can be someone's slave master. That is why we speak of mammon being a person's god. Something is driving and ruling that person: “Because of this, you must do that.” It is not necessarily anything good. The existence of a strong drive does not itself tell you that its source is the Spirit.

Pharaoh orders the taskmasters to stop supplying straw. The people must gather it, while producing the same quota of bricks. I thought the quota was increased, but the text says it was not reduced. The additional work is gathering the straw. The goal remains, while another requirement is added to the process.

The taskmasters force the people to hurry: “Fulfil your work, your daily quota, as when there was straw.” The officers are beaten and asked why the required work was not completed yesterday and today. They appeal to Pharaoh because they must make bricks without being supplied straw.

The master determines the goal, orders the process and drives execution. When Paul speaks of slave-driving himself, he is both master and slave in that picture. In the application we are considering, the Spirit is the master driving the goals, plans and doing.

## A Forceful Drive, with Right Goals and Plans

Think about how lightly we can treat our own assignments. After the evening prayer watch, no one has beaten you, yet you say, “I can't kill myself.” It depends. Sometimes, if you could have done it, you should have done it. Sometimes you need to change your goal and your plan. Pharaoh's goal and plan here were wicked. I also thought of showing you the account of Solomon, another instance in which that forceful discipline was not the best thing.

Slave-driving is not a gentle expression. It describes a forceful drive. In the case we are considering, where does that drive come from? The Spirit. We are considering the Spirit as taskmaster, slave driver or master. That is the picture I want you to understand in Paul's description.

The goal is not separate from that picture. The master sets a clear goal, sets a clear plan and enforces the doing. The taskmasters forced the people to hurry, telling them to fulfil their work. The drive urges execution. So the master drives the goals, the plan or order, and the execution or diligence.

## Discipline as Strength Towards and Against

Let us examine the word translated being temperate. The verb is egkrateuomai, and the noun is egkrateia. Both come from the adjective egkratēs, used in Titus 1:8. The adjective describes being in strength concerning something. The first part has the sense of “in,” and the second concerns strength. The idea is a state or condition of being in strength about something.

Titus 1:8 describes a leader as hospitable, a lover of what is good, sober-minded, just, holy and self-controlled. Discipline is a vital qualification for leadership. A leader should have developed to that point. The one who fulfils his assignment must be temperate in all things, in strength concerning all things.

One might use the word masterful, but I think a useful way to understand it is being in strength towards something or away from something. Discipline can mean strength towards or strength against. You are in strength against, or away from, unrighteous things, and in strength towards righteous things.

Because Paul describes discipline through slave-driving, I will call that strength drive. He describes someone driving you to do something: setting the goals and driving you towards them, setting the process and driving you to follow it, then driving you through the steps of doing it.

Discipline is being in strong drive about all things, either strong drive towards or strong drive against. When you should be strongly against something but are not, the drive is weak. We say you are weak in discipline. Sometimes the strong drive against something means fleeing. There are things you should be strongly against and things you should be strongly towards.

## Beginning from the Lowest Point

When you wake up to the day's work, or wake up to prayer, there should be a strong drive towards doing it. I have often said that no matter how low a believer is, you cannot be lower than having the nature of God in you. If his nature is in you, whatever happens, you can speak the word. That is the least you can do. The Spirit of faith, the Spirit of God, is in you.

Suppose you have been missing prayer and feel very low. You can still say, “I am born of God. I have discipline.” You cannot be so low that you cannot say that. Keep saying, “I am disciplined. I am disciplined to wake up at the right time.” From there, begin praying in the Spirit about that same matter. You are setting yourself up to do what you should do.

If you do not do that, what alternative do you have? Are you resigning yourself to walking in the flesh? That must be the least response you can make: “I am disciplined.” You begin with what is true of you and build that drive.

## Discipline Throughout the Christian Walk

The noun egkrateia is used four times in three verses. In one of these uses, it can describe the entire walk in the Spirit. Someone can be strong in the drive to love, having disciplined himself to walk in love. Relational love cannot describe discipline in that same sense; saying, “I love to be disciplined,” means something different. Discipline can qualify any other gift of the Spirit. You need discipline in natural assignment, spiritual assignment and relationships.

In relationships, know where you should be strong towards people, showing up for them, and where you need to be strong in moving away or drawing boundaries. Do not be careless. You can be disciplined to be loving, kind and tender. You can train yourself to be full of tenderness and be strong in the drive to act that way. Discipline can describe the entire Christian walk.

Acts 24 gives an example. Felix came with his wife Drusilla, who was Jewish, and sent for Paul to hear him concerning faith in Christ. Verse 25 says Paul reasoned about righteousness, self-control and the judgment to come. Righteousness concerns God making us right; self-control or discipline concerns our walk; judgment concerns the end. He reasoned about salvation, our walk and the end. Discipline is used to describe the Christian walk.

Galatians 5:22–23 names the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, meekness and temperance. Against such there is no law. We can consider that statement from the standpoint of love, and also from the standpoint of discipline, because discipline encompasses the expression of these things.

Second Peter 1:6 uses the noun twice. Verse 5 begins, “For this very reason, giving all diligence.” Diligence itself can be understood as part of discipline. Your faith is the starting point, and you bring diligence alongside it. Add to your faith virtue, to virtue knowledge, to knowledge self-control, and to self-control perseverance. You bring in discipline.

The verb occurs in 1 Corinthians 7:9 and 9:25. We have been studying the latter, but let us also see the former. With a right understanding of discipline, we understand that the Spirit in us is meant to be our slave master. The Lord is the Spirit. The Spirit is an expression of Christ's lordship in our lives. And this is the Spirit with whom we are one.

## The Discipline Involved in Remaining Single

First Corinthians 7:8 says to the unmarried and widows that it is good for them to remain as Paul is. Then verse 9 says, “But if they cannot exercise self-control, let them marry. For it is better to marry than to burn with passion.” Exercising self-control is the verb here. If they cannot be disciplined in this way, they should marry.

There is a kind of discipline involved in remaining single, and not everybody has it. This is why, after teaching who we are in Christ, I sometimes say that there are things in which you should not try to strain the nature of God in you. Even God's word recognizes some things as an extra burden for which you may not be qualified. Believe God and trust him for favour where you know that this is your situation.

This is not the same as gambling or taking drugs. If a person is kept away from drugs, he may go through a lot, but he cannot use what he cannot access. The same can be said of gambling, alcohol and other vices. But you cannot be separated from your sexuality in that way. That is the reality. If you do not have the grace for this kind of discipline, go and marry.

We all have discipline, but here we are speaking of a particular kind of discipline. You know where you are. You can trust God and say, “God, you see me and you see my heart. I love you. We need to solve this matter.” That is different from pretending that there is no issue.

Someone says the wedding cannot happen until years from now. What are we doing? There should, of course, be a reasonable time of courtship in which you know one another. But there is also a point at which unnecessary delay does not make sense. You cannot merely say, “We are praying,” as though that removes every consideration the passage gives.

## Do Not Turn Faith into Presumption

When Jesus was tempted to jump down, Scripture was quoted about angels having charge over him and keeping his foot from striking a stone. Jesus answered, “You shall not tempt the Lord your God.” That is relevant when someone keeps saying, “You have discipline,” while inviting you to put yourself in unnecessary temptation.

Someone says, “Come and visit me,” in a situation where you know what the temptation is. You can answer, “You shall not tempt the Lord your God.” Even the Lord whom we are describing as your master, the Spirit, should not be treated presumptuously. Do not say, “The Spirit will hold me, keep me and drive me,” while choosing to test that in a situation you should avoid.

Do not let people delay you unnecessarily in matters where you should move forward, but do not misunderstand what I am saying either. We must keep the reasonable courtship, the discernment and the actual instruction of God's word in place.

## Build the Drive Daily

Once you know God's purpose, who you are and your assignment, you know that fulfilling it depends on the hidden man. One of the primary things to build there is discipline. You need love, joy, peace, kindness, meekness, gentleness and the supernatural, but you also need the Spirit's drive over your goals, plans and execution.

You can be strong or weak in that drive. People sometimes build it in a special meeting and accomplish things for the next few days, then expect it to sustain itself. When that does not happen, they feel condemned. Build it daily.

The master sets today's quota and drives the doing. When we pray about fulfilling God's purpose through discipline, we should know what must be done. The goals, the process and the actions all belong together.

If you see someone who has developed this, remember that he built it too. Start where you are, even lying in bed, unwilling to get up. Say, “I am born of God. I have the discipline of the Spirit. I am strong in the drive of the Holy Ghost to do all I am to do.” Start until you rise and begin doing it.

For the sake of God's purpose and your assignment, build discipline every day. Taskmasters do not appear only when they feel like it. Do not weaken the drive and expect the work to happen. When you notice your drive against something becoming weak, stay in prayer: “I am against this. I am not for this.” Build strength towards righteousness and against unrighteousness.

## Keep Discernment and Boundaries

I believe there is a great deal you can do by the Spirit of God in relation to sexuality. But I also believe you should do it within a reasonable time, because that is the point Paul is making. If you know you want to marry, do not try to strain the new creation and the reasonable discipline that should operate in you beyond that.

Trust God for his favour, and do not remove walking in God's word and discernment from these matters. Desperation can make you lose your boundaries, act foolishly and become permissive. Do not risk that. It is worse. Keep the instruction and the discernment together.

Walk and become stronger, or become stronger and walk, in discipline. It is the drive of the Spirit.

## The Spirit's Drive and the Flesh's Drive

I have not developed the drive of the flesh as fully as I intended. But Paul first uses discipline in a natural illustration, then applies it to us. Galatians 5:23 shows us discipline as an expression of the Spirit. There can also be a drive which comes from the flesh.

Man was ordinarily designed by God to know what is right. Otherwise, how would he become saved? If you hold that man is so totally depraved that he cannot know what is right, you will not be honest with Scripture. He can know what is right and choose to do it. But doing it from a place without redemption is itself sinful, because it is born of pride and is about self. I cannot explain that as fully as I would like here, but keep that distinction in view.

## Strong to Fulfil the Daily Assignment

I walk in discipline. I grow stronger in discipline. I am strong in the drive of the Spirit towards righteousness, and strong in the drive of the Spirit against unrighteousness. I am strong in the drive of the Holy Ghost to fulfil my natural and spiritual assignments.

Every day I am getting stronger in the drive of the Spirit, causing me to walk in all I am to do that day. I fulfil my daily quota. Every day I fulfil my daily quota in my spiritual assignment under the rulership of the Holy Ghost.

Your spiritual assignment: what you say you will do, do it every day by the Spirit of God. Your natural assignment: what you have set out to do, do it every day. Set the right plan. Have the right goals. Go after the day as you have laid it out by God's Spirit.

You are born of God. No matter how low you are, you can walk in faith for discipline. Start getting back on your feet and going after it.
