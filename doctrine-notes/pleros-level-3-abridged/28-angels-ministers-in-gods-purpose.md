## Angels as servants in God’s purpose

[0:00]

We are looking at angels, ministers in God’s purpose. “Ministers” here means servants, not ministers of the gospel. Often angels are taught only in connection with a need—favor, preservation or miracles. We want to understand them in their own right, through the role for which they exist.

“Angels” is a description rather than an exclusive name for these entities. Scripture uses different descriptions, including gods and sons of God, but angels is common in the New Testament, so we will use it. Our claim is that they are best understood as servants in God’s purpose. To understand that role, we must first understand the purpose itself.

Ephesians 1:9 says God made known the mystery of his will according to his good pleasure which he purposed in himself. His purpose is what he likes, wants and determines to have. Verse 5 says he predestined us to adoption as sons by Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will.

What he predestined is not identical with what the predestination leads to. The work by Jesus Christ is redemption; it leads to sonship. Consider someone determined to study law who plans an alternative admission route if the ordinary examination route fails. The alternative is predetermined because of the original desire to study law. Human planning is forecasting, whereas God foreknows. A chess player similarly plans responses to possible moves, although human knowledge is limited.

[6:34 — The Greek terms in the explanation of predestination are unclear in the captions.]

The destination reveals the original desire. God predestined redemption unto sonship because sonship is his good pleasure. Calling angels servants in God’s purpose therefore means calling them servants in sonship. What does sonship entail, and why does it involve servants?

## Children and heirs

[9:52]

Romans 8:15–17 says we received the Spirit of sonship, whereby we cry, “Abba, Father.” The Spirit bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God. Think of the two spirits hypothetically undergoing an examination: they give the same evidence because our spirit has been changed after God. First Corinthians 6:17 says the one joined to the Lord is one spirit with him.

If children, then heirs. Sonship involves both being God’s child and being his heir. Saying that he wants children alone does not complete the picture. He wants children who also take responsibility for what is his.

Galatians 3:26 says we are sons of God through faith in Christ. As many as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ. This putting on is not the adorning of the hidden man or consciousness that we have studied elsewhere; it concerns the spirit itself. First Corinthians 12:13 likewise says that by one Spirit we were baptized into one body and made to drink of one Spirit.

The evidence strongly points to spiritual baptism by faith. Ephesians 4 speaks of one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith and one baptism. This does not deny the practice or value of water baptism. But we cannot add to Christ’s redemptive work. By faith in his death, burial and resurrection, we are baptized into him and put on Christ.

Galatians 3 continues that Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female are one in Christ. If we are Christ’s, we are Abraham’s seed and heirs. There is joint heirship: we own the cosmos to order it after righteousness, which bears on civic responsibility locally and more widely. There are also individual allotments: natural and spiritual assignments given to each person.

## Heirs, servants, guardians and stewards

[18:07]

Galatians 4 uses an illustration that reveals other roles accompanying sonship. The heir, while a child, differs little in practice from a servant, though master of all. He is under guardians and stewards until the time appointed by the father. [18:52 — The caption says “Galatians 5:1,” but the surrounding discussion identifies chapter 4.]

We can use “servant” even for a son. A child entrusted with part of a parent’s business serves the parent in managing it, while also being an heir. Consider children of a wealthy business owner entrusted with divisions of the enterprise. [19:40–20:26 — Names and company details in the illustration are partly uncertain; the speaker presents possible examples, not verified appointments.] Their service differs from that of employees who are not heirs.

Believers are called servants or slaves of God and righteousness in Romans 6, and servants of Christ in passages about natural work. Yet servant is not our core identity in the same sense as someone who is not an heir. There is the role of the heir, the master, and the role of the servant.

Do not hear “servant” here only through the worst history of the slave trade. The role being discussed is dignified. Abraham’s chief servant held great responsibility, although he was not the heir. Guardians and stewards can exercise real leadership, training and management over what they do not own. The heir can therefore be under them while being trained.

This is the distinction we are drawing: sonship involves the heir’s role and also the servants’ role. Angels occupy that ministering role in God’s purpose.

## The law as preparation

[24:41]

Galatians 4 describes a period under the elements of the world and under the law, followed by the coming of God’s Son and redemption unto sonship. The expression “elements of the world” can be used in different ways: for the natural ordering of the world, in some contexts in relation to angels, and for law reflecting truth that can also be seen in nature. [25:04–26:29 — The Greek terminology and some sentences are unclear.]

Galatians 3:23–24 says the law was a tutor to bring us to Christ, that we might be justified by faith. Verse 19 says it was appointed through angels by the hand of a mediator, Moses. The system deliberately depicted angels, a mediator and man. The guardians and stewards of chapter 4 connect with that picture.

There was a training phase, a preparation for functioning as heirs. Through the system, people learned how they were to act in God’s purpose. We will examine the law, its content and its systems separately; the point here is that Paul has already connected the servants’ role with angels.

The first thing to say about man is not how many hands or eyes he has, but that he was made in God’s image, to be his child and heir. Likewise the first thing to say about angels is that they were made to be servants in God’s purpose. Descriptions of their composition and abilities follow that primary role.

## Ministers who do God’s pleasure

[30:57]

Psalm 104 describes God clothed with light, stretching out the heavens like a curtain, laying the beams of his upper chambers in the waters, making the clouds his chariot and walking on the wings of the wind. We are connecting this imagery with angels: the heavenly host through whom he acts. Verse 4 speaks of making his angels spirits and his ministers a flame of fire.

Their dwelling is in the spiritual world overlapping the material. “Above” is not merely physical elevation; the spiritual influences the material. They are unseen from the physical standpoint but affect what is seen. Whether described through water, clouds, wind or fire, their role remains service.

Psalm 103:20–21 calls them those who excel in strength, do his word, heed its voice and do his pleasure. “Excel” is comparative: their abilities exceed ordinary human abilities. Strength need not mean only what someone can physically carry.

The word for pleasure is also used for will, as in Psalm 40:8: “I delight to do your will.” That psalm speaks of doing what God desires, with his law in the heart, rather than merely offering depictions through sacrifice. Its primary redemptive fulfillment is in Christ, but David speaks it, and people walking in God’s will can speak that commitment too. We do not thereby replace the unique redemptive work of the Lord Jesus.

Man is to walk in God’s will, and angels minister in relation to that same will. Hebrews 1:7 repeats the description of angels as spirits and ministers as a flame of fire. Verse 14 asks whether they are not all ministering spirits sent forth for those who will inherit salvation.

They minister toward redemption, after redemption toward its consummation, and as man walks in God’s will age to age. If you are an heir in God’s purpose, you should recognize that God has also provided this role of servants. It is dignified, and the entities we call angels occupy it.

## The origin of angels

[40:26]

Angels are created beings, not eternal or self-existent beings. Job 38 places the morning stars and sons of God rejoicing in the account of the foundation of the earth. Someone can argue that the text says they rejoiced at that point rather than explicitly saying they were created at that point. That is why we do not rest the entire case on this passage.

Nehemiah 9:5–6 says Yahweh alone is God, and that he made heaven, the heaven of heavens and all their host, as well as earth and sea. The host of heaven worships him. The language therefore concerns more than visible stars. Angels are included in the created cosmos, just as man is included in what God made.

Psalm 148 calls on angels, hosts, sun, moon, stars of light, heavens of heavens and waters above the heavens to praise the Lord. I argue that verses 1–4 describe angels through this heavenly imagery, connecting it with Psalm 104. Verse 5 says he commanded and they were created. Then the psalm turns to the earth. Job 38 is the less explicit part of the case; Nehemiah 9 and Psalm 148 make the created status clearer.

## Genesis and the redemptive setting

[47:10]

We have argued that Genesis 1 is a redemption account. Darkness presupposes light and its withdrawal; the restoration of light and the ordering that follows are restorative. Heavens, earth and waters are already present in the account. What has gone wrong is being made right.

When we read the lights and stars in Genesis 1:14 onward, we should therefore consider the redemptive situation: darkness and light are present, and redemption has not yet reached consummation. The lights distinguish day and night, mark seasons and give light on earth. Their influence is felt on earth.

There are arguments to be made from that picture for angelic ministry, including the distinction between angels of light and angels of darkness, the devil and his cohorts. This is being offered as an interpretation within the redemptive reading, not as a separate account of self-existent beings.

That matters when considering claims that Jesus is a created angel, or pictures in which many beings were simply eternally there. The scriptural picture being argued is that Yahweh alone is self-existent, and the heavenly host is created and worships him. [50:21–50:50 — The references to Jehovah’s Witnesses and Latter-day Saints include unclear wording; the central contrast is between the self-existent God and created angels.]

## Hierarchy and their work in the world

[51:06]

Their role is ministry in sonship. Man is an heir; angels serve in that purpose. You cannot assume you carry out the assignment alone, without the help of those assigned to minister.

Their roles are hierarchical. Human society organizes at local, state, national and wider levels to get things done. Scripture uses the same kinds of words for spiritual ordering. Titus 3:1 uses rulers and authorities, or in the KJV principalities and powers, for civic government. Ephesians 1 uses principalities, powers, might and dominion in heavenly places, with Christ above them all.

Daniel speaks of the princes of Persia and Greece in a spiritual setting that relates to events in the natural world. We continue to contend with darkness as we carry out God’s purpose until the age’s consummation. [54:50–55:05 — Some of the explanation about successive rulers is unclear.]

Ephesians 6:12 distinguishes this struggle from flesh and blood and speaks of principalities, powers and rulers of the darkness of the age. I understand the final description, spiritual wickedness in heavenly places, as describing the spiritual nature of the preceding orders rather than necessarily adding a fourth rank. The imagery is of organized rule, including broad dominion like that of an empire. [56:38–57:14 — The Greek terms are inconsistently captioned.]

Ephesians 3:9–10 speaks of the mystery being made known and God’s manifold wisdom displayed through the church to principalities and powers. Angels see in action what they desired to look into. There is also an application to natural leadership observing the church’s oneness across race, class and gender. Such unity shows what the Spirit brings about. The church can do a poor job of displaying it, but that remains the intended witness.

## Prayer, perseverance and the angelic response

[59:42]

Daniel 10 shows the need to persevere. From the first day Daniel set his heart to understand and humbled himself, his words were heard. The angel came for his words, yet met resistance from the prince of Persia for twenty-one days, until Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help.

We do not command angels. There is no basis here for addressing them directly. Even Jesus spoke of praying to the Father, who would supply angels. Yet our prayers, petitions and words give voice to what they act on under God. Daniel was speaking to God, not the angel, but the angel came for his words. This fits Psalm 103: they do God’s word, heeding its voice.

Taking the picture in Daniel, I am arguing that angels are typically of equal strength, with more on the side of life, and that further help can be brought to bear. [1:02:09–1:02:33 — Details of this inference and part of the quotation are unclear.] The practical point is to stay through in prayer rather than abandon it because the response is not immediately visible.

Angels are ministers in man’s pursuit of God’s purpose. Those who rebel can instead minister in man’s rebellion; man then becomes a victim, and they appear as rulers. The serpent eating dust, with man described as dust, is part of the picture we have connected to this. The history behind that requires careful inductive and abductive reasoning, which we will distinguish from direct statements.

## Abilities, appearance, names and numbers

[1:03:54]

Psalm 103 says they excel in strength. Isaiah 6, Ezekiel 1 and 10, and Revelation 4 add imagery associated with seraphs, cherubim and living creatures. Those descriptions combine forms such as lion, calf, eagle and man to depict the accumulation of natural abilities. They should not automatically be treated as exclusive classes defined only by appearance; hierarchical responsibilities are a separate issue.

Cherubim were depicted in multiple numbers in the tabernacle and temple, while particular ones were attached to the mercy seat. The exact roots of the terms are difficult, and I have not checked all of those details for this teaching. [1:05:15–1:05:29 — Some terminology is unclear.] As far as names go, keep the teaching here to Michael and Gabriel. Other names such as Uriel and Raphael are being distinguished as coming from other historical materials. [1:06:02 — The particular work mentioned is unclear.]

The ability being claimed is anything naturally or cosmologically possible. Their knowledge concerns nature; this is not omniscience or knowledge of the future. If there were elements or natural possibilities beyond what we know, they would know how those work. The joking reference to imagined materials such as kryptonite illustrates the scope being proposed, not a claim that such a material exists.

Their ordinary appearance is described as that of men. Wings and multiple faces belong to exceptional, figurative descriptions. Daniel 10:15–16 describes the likeness of the sons of men; verses 5 onward describe a man with striking visionary features. Revelation 21:17 speaks of a man’s measure, that is, an angel’s. We should not turn every exceptional or figurative vision into the normal anatomy of an angel.

They do not need wings to fly, or immense size to excel in strength. Nor should popular pictures of chubby baby angels determine our understanding. Visions may have figurative purposes, but those pictures are not the normative description being established here.

What about numbers? They are described as myriads or innumerable. We have made calculations elsewhere that could suggest numbers in the trillions, but we do not know an exact number. That is projection, not certainty.

The reference to a woman describing David as wise like an angel is used in discussing natural knowledge and judgment. [1:13:42–1:14:08 — The precise reference and some wording are unclear.] Their technical capacity suits their ministering role. An heir coordinating an enterprise need not personally possess every technical skill of those who execute its work.

## Character and the limits of what we can say

[1:14:40]

Angels are spirits with minds. They can reason, remember, will, hold convictions and desire. Scripture speaks of angels who sinned, which involves decision. The citation is captioned incorrectly here but later clearly given as 2 Peter 2:4.

Can their consciousness vary as ours does? I do not know; we do not have enough evidence to say. We are not given an angel of light becoming annoyed one day and deciding to do evil. We have no basis for explaining Gabriel’s action toward Zechariah as an angel simply not knowing better, or claiming that an angel of light does evil merely because a believer commands it.

If someone speaks evil words, that is not a reason to assign their execution to angels of light. The devil and evil spirits already do evil. Psalm 103 presents God’s angels as bound to do his word and pleasure. The inference is that, having chosen their side, they are bound or covenanted to it.

We can argue for emotions appropriate to their conviction, since they desire, and for hatred or wickedness among the spirits described as wicked and unclean. But variable moods and departures from their chosen allegiance are not established. Expect consistency in their ministry under God. They are not bound to obey every human whim. [1:17:04–1:18:24 — Some wording about consciousness and consistency is unclear, but that qualification is explicit.]

## The history of angels: an argued reconstruction

[1:18:33]

We know first that they were created. What happened before sin? As far as we can tell, they were in consideration of God’s purpose, as man was before choosing. One can lean in a direction before making the decision. First Timothy 2 says the man was not deceived; that allows an argument about his prior consideration, but it does not give every detail.

Ezekiel 28 speaks about a human ruler with cherubic imagery, including trafficking. We have connected that with consideration and activity before the decision. [1:19:58 — The caption says “Isaac”; the surrounding discussion points to Ezekiel.]

My argument is that their decision concerning rebellion follows man’s setting it in motion. If man had not sinned, there would not yet be the fallen order in which they minister in rebellion. Could they still rebel afterward? I could argue yes. But the point at which the decision becomes settled is being related to what man sets in motion.

This reconstruction is by induction and abduction, not a detailed sequence directly narrated in one passage. Second Peter 2:4 and Jude 6 speak of angels who sinned or left their first estate. I acknowledge the argument connecting the former text with Genesis 6. My own argument is that their confinement and reservation for judgment describe a settled state: a limited window for determining their fate, unlike the period of embodied earthly life in which human beings decide.

The proposed stages are creation, consideration of the offered purpose, and decision at man’s sin. Their decision is then in the light of the knowledge of redemption. Angels are called elect and holy—terms used in redemptive settings. First Timothy 5 speaks of elect angels, and Revelation speaks of holy angels. Ephesians 1 connects election in Christ with being holy and without blame.

I am arguing that the effect of man’s sin concerned the entire cosmos, including them, and that their exemption or redemption is in light of Christ’s work. This is harder to establish directly than the fact that Jesus died and rose. It is an inference from the scriptural framework, not a story to be filled in from speculative accounts.

One objection is that Genesis 3 seems to show angels already sinning before they influenced man. My response is that leaning toward sin can be distinguished from the settled condition of death and darkness that man’s decision brings. On this account, those who aligned with redemption are holy and elect, while those who rejected it remain reserved for judgment. These are the conclusions being argued; the limitations of the reconstruction should remain clear.

## Their work before and after redemption

[1:24:26]

Before the accomplishment of redemption, the rebellious angels opposed its fulfillment and the others worked toward it. Exodus 23:20, with the angel keeping and bringing the people to the prepared place, belongs to that work. After Jesus’s redemptive work, the opposition is to the church’s mission toward consummation, while God’s angels aid that mission.

What is available in their ministry today? Orchestration and favor in purpose: arranging favorable situations for its fulfillment. Preservation and deliverance in purpose, as in Peter’s deliverance. Also miracles and supernatural activity, including revelation.

I sometimes argue that walking on water could involve angels bearing someone up, since the account does not say the water became hard. That is an argument about possible angelic involvement, not an explicit explanation supplied by that account.

## Walking in their ministry today

[1:26:14]

Our part involves faith and words. Speaking God’s word and the things of life sets activity in that direction, just as words of darkness and unbelief work in the opposite direction. Prayer and worship are also important. Revelation depicts angels active in relation to prayer, with prayer as incense. I do not want to call them mediators of prayer; that is not the idea being taught.

Walking in the Spirit matters too. Acts 10 connects the angelic visit with prayers and alms. Righteousness gives their ministry something to work with. Diligence in natural and spiritual assignment does the same. Conversely, walking in the flesh supplies the opposing work. [1:27:22–1:27:30 — Some wording is unclear, but the contrast between the two kinds of walk is explicit.]

It is not only faith-filled words, prayer and worship; your walk matters. We do not need to talk to angels or pray to them. We can receive revelation from them as we walk with God, but our faith, prayers and conduct remain directed as taught. Recognize that their activities aid what we do in God’s purpose under his instruction. We bring this teaching to a close. Amen.
