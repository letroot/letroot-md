# Understanding the Book of Revelation

## One Revelation and the Place of Eschatology

We're looking at understanding the book of Revelation. The first thing that needs to be mentioned is that it's not Revelations. If we read the material, it doesn't seem that John took a break throughout. It's one of the quickest materials you can read, aside from the epistles. It's not as smooth a sail to read Matthew in one go, maybe Mark, but Revelation is a book you can read in roughly an hour. You can read it well under an hour; it depends on reading speed. Revelation is not that long.

He saw it as a vision. You can read and imagine it, but I still believe the vision was a bit longer. It's not different revelations he had, taking a break and going into another one. It was a vision, with visions within that single revelation. He finished one scene, entered another, was taken by an angel into another, and on like that. It's the book of Revelation, a single revelation.

If we are going to fulfill God's purpose, we need the knowledge of God's word. There is an entire scope of doctrine we need. One of the things we identified that Paul talks about in the doctrine of our faith, Ephesians chapter 1, is the last days. We looked at that in the last teaching in level three, “Concerning the Last Days.” Eschatology is part of the doctrine of the church. It has an implication for our attitude, disposition, outlook, and worldview. If it is not present, we are going to lack that attitude.

Some instructions the Lord gave depend on your having eschatological truth on your mind. “Be faithful, for the master is coming at an hour you do not know.” If you don't have eschatological truth on your mind, you won't have that disposition. Think about church history even in a nation like Nigeria. Churches that tend to be more eschatological somehow have a particular godly disposition in their membership. We might say it's about teaching that character. On one hand, that might be true. On the other, without eschatological truth, you will lack certain dispositions.

There's a kind of anticipation we saw the apostles have that we're always supposed to have. Every generation of Christians must, in their time, have the disposition that it is close, near, about to happen. They work towards that in carrying out the mission of the church, their spiritual assignments, and in their attitude. He who has this hope will purify himself. Certain things will be present with you when you have the right eschatological belief as a Christian.

## Wrong Eschatological Beliefs Can Be Costly

We must say in the same breath that wrong eschatology can be very costly. People can be trapped. There was a known case back then where some people stopped going to class and went to a mountain because they were expecting the Lord. They had been given a particular revelation that he was coming so soon. This isn't new. It's almost like the Montanist movement. Should we talk about the one that was worse, that Jones man where they drank Kool-Aid? Wrong eschatological beliefs can be costly because of the force eschatological truth has. It can be compelling, and believing the wrong things can be expensive.

Interestingly, it seems that without the book of Revelation, the kind of tension people have eschatologically wouldn't be there. That makes it more important to pay close attention to it. They are not likely to have that tension from the epistles. But they start reading Revelation and see people receiving 666.

I remember before I was born again, someone was joking with me and wrote 666 on my hand. I wasn't a troublesome fellow; I was very gentle and didn't like all this. I wondered, “Am I the Antichrist? Could this be? Why did he get to write it?” I was very bothered, even though I wasn't saved.

There was another guy then, I think around JS2 or JS3. We had a white bus. My mother used to pick us up, and he followed us because he didn't live far from our house. He had read a book, and I think I later found out which book it was. He would tell us stories about the underworld. It was very gripping. The journey from Jericho towards Challenge was roughly forty minutes to an hour. We were always eager. He would stop somewhere in the story and continue later. But that guy was fabricating, adding things to it. It became a series we were eager to hear.

I wasn't saved then, and it was thrilling to think about. Some of these things, the kind of visions people see, are because of the book of Revelation. By the time you explain the book, you'll see they're somehow mistaken in how they approach some issues. Even if they see visions that way, maybe they should treat them the way John expected the audience he wrote to to treat what he wrote.

## The Spirit Received Now and the Redemption Ahead

Let's establish eschatology as an important part of Christian doctrine, then return to Revelation. Ephesians 1:13 says, “In him,” Christ, “you trusted after you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation; in whom, having believed, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise.” “Sealed” carries the intention that something is to be unlocked later. The Spirit is the earnest of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession.

He gives us a timeline: the earnest of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession. There is a timeline from when people begin to have the Spirit of God in them regeneratively. Remember, I made the case for the Spirit of God being with people in a non-regenerative sense before the accomplished work of redemption. We'll examine that in level four.

Today, when you have redemptive faith, the Spirit is in you regeneratively. Before Christ, people of redemptive faith were not regenerated as we are. They were still in a state of death, needing to be made alive. There was a temporary, interim provision of God for them until their redemptive faith was fulfilled in Christ's work. Now, when you have regenerative faith, there is still a distance. You have the Spirit of sonship, yet you wait for sonship, the redemption of your body. You have already received the Spirit of sonship in you.

Ephesians 4:30 speaks of this timeline: “Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption.” There is a time between the Spirit of God being in you regeneratively and what is going to be done. You were sealed for the day of redemption. It's a powerful text affirming what you might call the preservative role of the Spirit in a man. The Holy Spirit was given to you towards that day.

Romans 8:15 first says, “You did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but the Spirit of adoption.” That's something that has happened, not something we're expecting. We received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, “Abba, Father.” But verse 23 says that we who have the firstfruits of the Spirit groan within ourselves, eagerly waiting for adoption. In one sense, we already have the Spirit of sonship; in another, we wait for sonship.

There's something about our inheritance that isn't yet fully seen. We eagerly wait for what belongs to us. We have received the Spirit of those who are to receive an inheritance, but there is something of that inheritance we haven't received: immortality, incorruption, new heavens and a new earth. We groan within ourselves, eagerly waiting for the adoption, the redemption of our body. When Paul says we're sealed until the day of redemption, you can see he intends bodily redemption, the glorification of our body.

## The Last Days Follow Redemption

This timeline Paul points to is what is referred to as the last days. In “Concerning the Last Days,” we saw several mentions in the epistles and Gospels, but none clearly explained the last days. We could make an inference from some of them, like 1 Corinthians 15 calling Jesus the last Adam, or the statement that he was manifested in these last days for you. It seems Jesus' presence has something to do with the last days.

The text giving us a way to answer definitively is Acts chapter 2. Peter stood up and said this was what the prophet Joel had prophesied: “I will pour out my Spirit in the last days, says God.” But when we read Joel, he didn't use “last days.” He said “afterwards.” Peter used “last days.” We practically read through Joel in that teaching. Joel's “afterwards” is after people sinned, became condemned, had judgment meted out against them, repented, and were restored or redeemed.

After sin, condemnation, judgment, repentance, and restoration, he says, “Afterwards I will pour out my Spirit on them.” He's saying post-restoration, post-redemption. Peter takes it up in the same breath: through what Christ has done, upon his redemption, God pours out his Spirit. Putting the texts together, the last days refer to the days when the Christ is present, or his redemption fulfilled and his Spirit given. You can choose to count them from redemption being fulfilled or the Redeemer being present.

We saw that redemptive pattern of eschatology playing out repeatedly in Scripture, even down to the Olivet Discourse, which is for the church. Now we encounter Revelation, a book on its own. There are details I won't get into, such as which John wrote it and a stronger consensus leaning away from John the apostle. I don't want to get into that here. It's better for level five. For now, I want to address the substance of the book.

## Revelation Given from the Father Through the Son

Revelation is undoubtedly the most eschatologically focused material. After understanding it, you might argue whether it should be thought of only eschatologically, but it is strongly focused that way, almost entirely. The title comes from John having a revelation, apokalypsis. “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants things which must shortly take place.” Jesus gives a revelation to John.

You can find the eschatological pattern in almost every book of Scripture, arguably. In the Minor Prophets you see it a lot, and in Genesis. I gave about seven patterns running across Scripture. You can read your own life in your generation within God's eschatological pattern. But this book is heavily focused on it, and heavily contentious. We'll see why.

Revelation 1:1 says, “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants things which must shortly take place. And he sent and signified it by his angel to his servant John.” There are almost three levels of the message. There is a message intended to be delivered. God gave it to Jesus to show his servants. They are deliberately making a distinction between the Father and the Son. We are told the Father gave the revelation to the Son.

In the incarnation of the Lord Jesus Christ, his function excludes him from certain divine privileges, particularly eschatologically. He told us no one knows that day or hour. In his incarnation, in the role he carries out, he's not walking in certain divine privileges. As Philippians 2 says, there is an emptying of himself, a kenosis.

Matthew 24:36 says, “Of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, but my Father only.” Mark 13:32 makes it clearer: “Of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.” This framing at the beginning of Revelation is important. These are divinely granted privileges of things God wants us to know.

Who is giving the revelation? The Lord Jesus, with things he has by divine privilege, not only because he's God but even in his humanity, where he has decided to lay aside some privileges. He is now making those things known. “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants things which must shortly take place.”

## A Message Intended for the Servants of God

The first two questions we must answer after the general introduction are: what is the book about, what message is it communicating, and how was that message communicated? There is an intended goal. The Father gave the Son something to communicate to his servants, plural, before we're told it was communicated to one servant, singular.

The content is intended for believers. “Servants” is being used as in Luke 12, where you can speak of faithful and unfaithful servants, judged accordingly when the master comes. There are things he wants us to know. God wants several people to know the message. The Son, having received it from the Father, sent and signified it by his angel to his servant John.

The singular and plural are part of the original. They're not a creation of translators or a decision of textual critics. I doubt there's even a manuscript exception in this situation. It isn't some major debated decision. God wants his servants to know, so believers ought to know. We can argue he wanted believers at that time to know, but there are things useful beyond that time. By the end, you see the intention is for as long as this age continues. It's a book for us, though the message was communicated to one person.

He could have said he sent the message to his servant, but he says he “sent and signified.” That's a very key, deliberate word. From the beginning he's telling you how the message is communicated. We'll return to that. First, what is the message? Verse 1 says things which must shortly take place.

We can argue that it concerns immediate events, and as we get into it we see that. In terms of eschatological patterns, we can also argue that it concerns imminent events. “Shortly take place” can be read as immediate, rightly so, or as imminent: it will happen soon and can happen at any time. Everything necessary for it to happen is ready. It contains immediate events and imminent events.

John bore witness to the word of God, the testimony of Jesus Christ, and all he saw. “Blessed is he who reads and those who hear the words of this prophecy, and keep those things which are written in it; for the time is near.” You see the issue of imminence. Then, “John, to the seven churches,” and he goes on.

## Past, Present, and Future Within the Message

Another place showing what the material is about is Revelation 1:17–19. This is the Lord Jesus speaking. He came, told John things, and then gave his angel to signify them to John. “When I saw him, I fell at his feet as dead. He laid his right hand on me, saying, ‘Do not be afraid. I am the first and the last.’” The prōtos and eschatos. “I am he who lives, and was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore.”

He is the one who lives, yet came as human to die. Having accomplished redemption, in this new sense as human he is alive forevermore. He has brought immortality to light. “I have the keys of death and Hades.” Verse 19: “Write the things which you have seen, the things which are, and the things which will take place after this.”

You may argue that he means the short experience in the vision, the things just seen, what John is presently seeing, and what follows. That would also be right. But you'll find the material contains details of the past, present, and future. Keep that in mind. The message is things that will shortly take place, but it gives those things by mirroring or pointing to the past and showing the present and future.

He's sent to talk about immediate or imminent events God wants them to know. We must also know the context of those he writes to. These are Christians under Roman persecution, concerned by it, even though different locations had different experiences. Chapters 2 and 3 speak to churches where some issues were looseness, others oppression and persecution. It was written at a time when they were under Roman persecution and one empire, the Roman Empire, like a one-world order. That detail is necessary as we proceed.

This is a single level-three teaching, so I'll compress many things. I'll mention some without explaining them because there's not enough time. Some explanation will wait for level five. But expect references to past events, like the death and birth of the Lord Jesus, as well as present and future things. It's not everything you read that is something still to happen. It would be strange to read Revelation 12 and think some of it hasn't happened already. The tone says it has happened, not that it happens when John sees it or will happen later.

## “Sent and Signified”: The Reality and Its Sign

How is the message presented? Revelation 1:1 says he sent and signified it by his angel to John. The Father gives the message to the Son, and the Son sends and signifies it through the ministry of angels. The word “signify” is sēmainō. It's used six times in six verses, only once in Revelation, here in 1:1. Its noun is sēmeion, signs, as in signs and wonders. It means to show something indicating another thing.

Let's look at instances. It's used three times in John. If you assume this is the same John, that makes even more sense to examine. John 12:32 says, “If I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw all to myself.” Verse 33 says this signified what death he would die. He didn't mention death. He said “lifted up,” but that signifies the death he would die.

It probably points to crucifixion, being lifted up, or to dying and being raised, a death followed by resurrection. Whichever you're saying, he didn't mention death directly. “Lifted up” points to something else he's trying to say. When you use “signify,” there is the reality and the sign, the intended goal and the sign pointing to it. The reality you want to talk about is death, and then there's the way you express it.

John 18:31–32: Pilate tells them to take him and judge him according to their law. The Jews say it isn't lawful for them to put anyone to death, that Jesus' saying might be fulfilled, signifying what death he would die. Again, signifying.

John 21:18 says, “When you were younger, you girded yourself and walked wherever you wished; but when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish.” Verse 19 says he spoke this signifying by what death Peter would glorify God. Did he tell him directly what would happen? No. There's a reality, a more direct expression, and something else mentioned indirectly as a sign.

Church history keeps the record that Peter was crucified upside down. It wasn't what he wanted or did on his own. It was persecution. The Lord was pointing to the manner of his death. When we see sēmainō, we see that Revelation won't express things exactly or precisely as they are. It will use figurative descriptions. There will be a lot of figurative description in Revelation.

## The Signs in Revelation Are Not the Literal Reality

The noun sēmeion is used in Revelation a number of times, I think seven verses. It's used seventy-seven times in sixty-nine verses overall, but we'll focus on Revelation. Revelation 12:1 shows practically what the angel's signifying means. In these visions, someone was creating the scene for John. That's one role of the ministry of angels: figuratively describing things that have happened or are yet to happen in order to communicate a message.

“A great sign appeared in heaven.” That means this isn't the literal thing. “A woman clothed with the sun.” That isn't literal. No human being can be clothed with the sun. “The moon under her feet, and on her head a garland of twelve stars. Being with child, she cried out in labor and in pain to give birth.” He saw a sign, a pointer, not the reality. It's used to indicate or describe something.

The sign points to the reality. It isn't a clear expression of what the thing is; it is an indication. You must find out what the thing is because of the indication used. It's like a figure of speech. When someone says, “That guy is a lion,” you know what he means. You could write, “This he said signifying the boldness the guy had.”

Revelation 12:3 says another sign appeared in heaven, a great fiery red dragon. Was it really a dragon? The dragon described another entity. Verse 9 tells us: the dragon was cast out, that serpent of old. He uses another sign. When John initially saw it, he saw a dragon.

Similarly, people say if you see God's throne, you'll see four beasts and wonder how creatures like that can be around it. You don't have to see it that way. It depends on what the angels signify to you and how you receive. You can see awesome creatures, but it doesn't have to be one with this kind of head and another with that. That itself was a sign collapsing Isaiah's seraphim and Ezekiel's cherubim into one.

How the message is communicated is probably the most important point. This material is not literal. From the beginning it's clear he will communicate by signifying, and the use of signs is repeated. Some other uses of sēmeion are about the false prophet's signs and wonders, Revelation 13:13–14. Revelation 16:14 is also demonic powers performing signs, and 19:20 the false prophet. We don't need to focus on those.

Revelation 15:1 says, “I saw another sign in heaven, great and marvelous: seven angels having the seven last plagues.” Do they literally have seven last plagues in seven bowls? No. He saw a sign pointing to the unleashing of wrath. Revelation 12:1, 12:3, and 15:1 clearly show a figurative manner, already established by sēmainō in 1:1.

It's important that he said it in 1:1 because signifying starts in chapter 1. Early on you're told of the seven spirits of God. People go to “spirit of wisdom” and so forth. You could bring that thought in, but he's talking about the Spirit of God. It's difficult to recall every detail here, but such is the manner in which we have Revelation.

## Roman Persecution and the Purpose of the Outline

We have introduced eschatology as an important part of doctrine and Revelation as its most emphatic material. It was written during Roman persecution of Christians. Every place addressed was under the Roman Empire, considered the known world. Some were experiencing persecution; others knew their brothers and sisters were experiencing it. You must feel the burden of those experiencing persecution. We have to repent of not doing that.

At the beginning, Rome wasn't persecuting Christians. The first persecution was by Jewish authorities. Roman persecution was a later issue, around the period of Jewish revolt and its suppression, AD 66–70. Paul could appeal to Caesar with confidence. I'm thinking now that persecution started before AD 70, maybe around 64. It was long drawn out and sustained. It changed at different times, but it was centuries long, depending on how you look at it, before they had some relief or reprieve.

Keep that period in mind. They were under the Roman Empire and Roman persecution. The message is presented figuratively from the start. Now we come to the outline. It's impossible to exhaust every explanation of this book here. I'll give an outline and talk about it as we go.

## The Introductions in Revelation Chapter 1

The first part is Revelation 1:1–20, introduction. Verses 1–3 are the writer's introduction to the material, what led up to it, and the blessing on reading, hearing, and keeping the prophecy because the time is near. That's why I say the servants addressed are those in this age. As long as “the time is near” applies, as it does to us, the book is relevant for its eschatological truth.

Then there's the introduction to the letter itself: “John, to the seven churches which are in Asia.” Grace and peace from him who is, was, and is to come; from the seven spirits before his throne; and from Jesus Christ, the faithful witness, firstborn from the dead, ruler of the kings of the earth. The signs have already started with the seven spirits of God, which will come up later. John starts speaking that way himself.

“To him who loved us and washed us from our sins in his blood, and has made us kings and priests to his God and Father, to him be glory forevermore.” Then, “Behold, he is coming with clouds, and every eye will see him, even those who pierced him, and all the tribes of the earth will mourn because of him.” You can already sense the eschatological detail.

## Patterns of Coming and the Final Coming of the Lord

There's an argument about the coming of the Lord that I heard recently, and I'll present it. This matters because of people with Adventist kinds of theology, like Jehovah's Witnesses, and others whose leanings may come from this. There's an understanding that “the coming of the Lord” may not only refer to the second coming at the end. It can describe God coming to judge, and we can see several comings of the Lord in Scripture.

For example, the Lord came down with two other angels to Abraham before Sodom and Gomorrah. You can call that the Lord coming in the clouds with angels to judge. It's an eschatological pattern. I don't find anything wrong with that theology. What would be wrong is not clearly teaching the true, existential coming of the Lord at the end.

In that sense, can we say the Lord has come even in our time, in temporary acts of judgment? Yes, probably, but we must be careful. There are times when some justice hasn't happened. Where there's some justice or consequence today, maybe an army destroys another army as the victory of good over evil, you might call it a coming of the Lord. Evil, death, is being killed. The death of death is life, pointing to the death of death God seeks at the end.

There are also injustices. These instances of judgment can speak of the eventual justice that will happen. Every form of judgment you might call a coming of the Lord before the actual coming is, in comparison, weak and insufficient. What happened compared with what should happen justly is weak. What is supposed to happen should be much more severe.

I suspect some people read several comings into Revelation because of its several endings. I don't think that's the best reading. I suspect they're reading chronologically, seeing several endings, and taking them as interventions at different times. I don't think that's correct, and I'll make a strong case against it. We can read several comings of the Lord in the Bible, but mustn't mistake them for the one true coming. That's like reading several redemptive figures and thinking that is the actual redemption Christ accomplished.

We must not confuse patterns with the one true work of redemption, or with the final redemption and glorification of our bodies and the indignation and destruction at the end. I also think we should be careful when people experience judgments, perhaps an outbreak somewhere, and someone points to a vice or immorality there and says, “That's God's judgment.” There is a way Joel would use eschatological truth to interpret an experience of his day. We should allow those biblical letters their role in teaching truth through present experience, but be careful about approaching today's events that way.

The Lord had that carefulness when something fell on those people in Luke. Were they the only ones who had done evil? We should state the overall truth, that injustice will be judged, but be careful with specific examples. That approach can alienate people from God's love. They point to other evils where they see no justice, or to a child's situation. People must know God's justice, but we should be careful.

Matthew 10:14–15 says that when people refuse to receive you or hear your words, shake the dust from your feet. It will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city. Do you know how bad Sodom and Gomorrah was? Its people will still experience judgment. Indignation and consumption at the end will be worse. “More tolerable” is a severe comparison.

Matthew 11:20 onward rebukes the cities where Jesus' mighty works were done because they didn't repent. Chorazin and Bethsaida are compared with Tyre and Sidon, which would have repented in sackcloth and ashes. It will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon in the day of judgment. Capernaum, exalted to heaven, will be brought down to Hades. If Sodom had seen those mighty works, it would have remained; yet the day of judgment will be more tolerable for Sodom.

You could call what happened to Tyre and Sidon, Sodom and Gomorrah, or Noah's flood an advent, a parousia, a coming of the Lord. Jude and 2 Peter make these comparisons, bringing out Genesis 6 and such events. They are eschatological patterns. But the true one in the light of the existential truth of the gospel is at the end, indignation and consumption.

To say that the darkened moon and such language is just judgment when a war destroys people isn't a wholesome reading. You can see a pre-fulfillment application, but it doesn't remove the true day of the Lord. There is a true eschatological pattern with Jesus at its center, and his coming concludes it.

Revelation 1:7 speaks of coming in clouds. People argue that Jesus told Caiaphas he would see him, so the coming means judgment in AD 70, and therefore Jesus has already come and is with us. Be careful. Patterns playing out don't replace eschatological truth. You can see AD 66–70 as a coming in judgment against those rebellious to the gospel, but Christian persecution is the wickedness done to the good before the wicked face judgment. If judgment begins at God's house, what will be the lot of those outside? Keep that distinction in mind.

## Christ Among the Seven Lampstands

After the book and letter introductions, Jesus introduces himself as Alpha and Omega, beginning and end, who is, was, and is to come. John then calls himself their brother and companion in tribulation, kingdom, and patience of Jesus Christ. He was on Patmos for the word and testimony. Persecution is part of the context.

Signification appears early. In Revelation 1:12 he sees seven golden lampstands, and among them one like the Son of Man, clothed to his feet, with a golden band, white hair, and eyes like flames. Verse 20 explains the mystery: the seven stars are the angels of the seven churches, and the seven lampstands are the churches. He's in the midst of his church. That agrees with the epistles' teaching that Father and Son dwell in the church, as in Ephesians 2:19–22.

## The Seven Churches and the Severity of Judgment

The second part is Revelation 2:1–3:22, the seven churches. They receive rebuke, instruction, correction, and warnings affecting them in judgment. We know a judgment about salvation and damnation and another we call judgment of rewards. Let me be clear: the judgment of rewards is severe. Calling it rewards doesn't make it a relaxed matter. Scripture never gives that picture.

Don't speak as though you're going towards licentiousness, lasciviousness, or looseness. I won't go so far as those who devise an image of purgatory to reflect severity. I don't know how it will play out, but the matter is severe. Live your life right. Don't act as though you're unaccountable to God or truth, knowingly doing what's wrong. Walk in righteousness and love. Grow spiritually. Your intentions matter, so give yourself to growth. There is no room for living anyhow.

## Seven Seals and the Reign of Sin and Death

The third part, Revelation 4:1–8:1/5, is the seven seals. Chapter 4 shows God's throne. Chapter 5 asks who can open the scroll and seals. John weeps because nobody can, but the Lamb who died and rose has prevailed. The Lamb appears, the scroll begins to open, and what we see is judgment, what we can call the sevenfold reign of sin and death.

The first seal in Revelation 6 reveals a white horse, a rider with a bow and crown going out conquering. This is part of that reign. We have spoken of spiritual death and authority, including the devil and his forces; mortality of human life; corruption of matter; spiritual or worship disorder; governance disorder; economic disorder; and social or moral disorder. The conquering rider shows governance disorder under sin and death.

If Christ prevailed to open the scroll, why are all the things revealed bad? Because he is the one who will bring them to an end. He's unveiling the script to end it. The second seal shows a fiery red horse, its rider taking peace so people kill one another, given a great sword. We see mortality, evil, and perhaps social or governance disorder. They interweave.

The third seal brings a black horse and scales: a quart of wheat for a denarius, three quarts of barley for a denarius, with oil and wine unharmed. Economic disorder. The fourth brings Death, with power to kill over a fourth of the earth by sword, hunger, death, and beasts. Mortality and corruption of matter.

The fifth shows souls under the altar, slain for God's word, crying for judgment and vengeance. They receive white robes and wait for the time to be completed. That's persecution of the saints. I had wanted to make persecution a separate part of the sevenfold reign; the count became seven or eight. Put persecution of the saints there. We also saw it in the Olivet Discourse.

The sixth seal brings earthquake and cosmic signs, the sun blackened. Corruption of matter reaches its crescendo, its conclusion. From there we begin to see salvation or resurrection of the saints. You can say a general resurrection, but chapter 7 focuses on the saints, what we might call rapture on the day of the Lord. They sing and worship as the redeemed. This teaching assumes you've heard “Concerning the Last Days.”

The twenty-four elders say they're redeemed by the blood and made kings and priests on earth. They are humans, not angels, and must represent something. We aren't given enough to work with, but following signification, we would likely say they represent Jews and Gentiles saved before Christ. Chapter 7 shows the saints' resurrection, alongside those elders, celebrating salvation.

## The Seals and Trumpets End at the Same Point

When the seventh seal opens in Revelation 8:1, there is silence in heaven for about half an hour. Then seven angels receive trumpets. This introduces an overlap between seals and trumpets, one argument that the same end is repeated. Verse 5 finishes the seal before the trumpets proceed: the angel fills the censer with fire from the altar and throws it to earth. There are noises, thunderings, lightnings, and an earthquake.

Compare the seventh trumpet in Revelation 11:15–19. The world's kingdoms become the kingdom of our Lord and his Christ. This is the end of the age. The heavenly temple opens, the ark appears, and there are lightnings, noises, thunderings, an earthquake, and great hail. The trumpets end where the seals end.

That's why I give the seals as Revelation 4:1–8:1/5, and the fourth part, the trumpets, as Revelation 8:1/5–11:19. Verse 8:2 introduces the trumpets, then another angel with incense and the saints' prayers completes the seals' judgment. These include the prayer for vengeance seen in chapter 6. Verse 6 returns to the angels preparing to sound.

We're seeing another eschatological account. There will be seven accounts, different angles ending at the same place. Some argue the seven churches describe successive church phases until the last church; I don't really buy that. The first account here is the seals; the second is the trumpets, introduced in an overlapping way.

## The Seven Trumpets: Things Become One-Third Worse

In level five I argued there's a consistency: the trumpets show the reign of sin and death becoming one-third worse. The first brings hail and fire mixed with blood, burning a third of the trees. Corruption of matter worsens. The second brings a burning mountain into the sea: a third becomes blood, a third of creatures die, and a third of ships are destroyed.

The third trumpet brings a star like a burning torch, called Wormwood, falling on a third of rivers and springs. Waters become bitter and people die. Mortality and corruption of matter, one-third worse. The fourth strikes a third of sun, moon, and stars, darkening a third of day and night. Then come warnings of the remaining three trumpet blasts.

The fifth is one of the most confusing. I argue it's describing demonic powers. A star falls, receives the key to the bottomless pit, smoke darkens the air, and locust-like creatures emerge. I made other arguments in level five that I'm struggling to remember, honestly. Increased demonic activity, I think also persecution and destruction. I can't give the explanation here.

The sixth trumpet in Revelation 9:13 releases four bound angels and an army. Many are killed, an increase in human mortality. Verse 20 says the survivors still didn't repent of their works. Things grow increasingly worse. Chapter 10 has John eat a book, giving further revelation about the last days, then chapter 11 brings the two witnesses.

## The Two Witnesses and the Testimony Before Christ

Revelation 11 gives John a measuring rod to measure God's temple, altar, and worshipers, leaving the outside court to the Gentiles. You can see the Jewish depiction as church versus world: God's temple measured apart from the world. The holy city is trodden underfoot for forty-two months, three and a half years. Power is given to two witnesses to prophesy.

This likely follows what John ate in chapter 10, explaining mysteries previously unknown. Revelation 10:7 says that at the seventh trumpet the mystery of God will be finished, as declared to his servants the prophets. In level five I argued that the first three and a half years of the seven years of tribulation describe the pre-redemption period. The second half is increased, or great, tribulation.

In the eschatological pattern, sin and death reign, then increase at the instance of the Christ and his work. When Moses, the Christ figure, arrived in Egypt, did oppression decrease? It increased. The first half points to the pre-Christ period with saints, prophets, and the law.

Some call the two olive trees kings and priests. I don't think so. I've argued that the two olive trees and lampstands likely point to law and prophets, God's testimony before Christ. Fire coming from their mouths and shutting heaven against rain recalls Elijah. Turning water to blood recalls Moses. You see the law and prophets, as with those appearing to Jesus and the command, “Hear him.”

John either explains many signs or expects readers to know them from Old Testament texts or Second Temple literature. There's a strong argument that this three-and-a-half-year period is pre-redemption testimony. Those who read it as the very last days develop seven-year schemes and mid-tribulation rapture. I've argued against many of those things. There will be arguments for and against, but I believe our submissions are sound. I'm asserting here because I can't slow down to explain all of it.

When the witnesses finish, the beast from the bottomless pit makes war, overcomes, and kills them. Their bodies lie in the great city spiritually called Sodom and Egypt, where the Lord was crucified. Was he crucified in Sodom and Egypt? No. It is the world that killed him, where their testimony and persecution remain. Nations see the bodies for three and a half days and rejoice because they disliked the testimony.

After three and a half days, God's breath enters them, they stand, hear “Come up here,” and ascend in a cloud before their enemies. This would be like general resurrection after three and a half days, understood as three and a half years, the post-redemption period. He takes much liberty in description, but following his thoughts, I believe we should make those conclusions.

An earthquake follows, and then the seventh trumpet: the kingdoms of the world become the kingdom of our Lord and his Christ. Worldwide evangelism and discipleship reach fulfillment. The twenty-four elders worship. Verse 19 again brings the open temple, ark, lightning, noises, thunder, earthquake, and hail. Another end of the age, indignation, consumption, destruction, and judgment.

## The Woman, the Child, and Jewish Saints

The third eschatological account, the fifth part of the book, is Revelation 12:1–14:20: persecution of Jewish saints and the church. It begins with Christ coming from the Jews, depicted as a woman clothed with the sun giving birth. Some say Mary, which isn't a big problem, but Israel is the more accurate reading as you follow through. Those choosing Mary mostly have an agenda, understandably, but it's more Israel.

The dragon is the devil. His tail draws a third of the stars, angels, to earth. He opposes the child's birth; the Gospels show that opposition, some after the birth. The male child is to rule the nations with a rod of iron and is caught up to God and his throne. That should be post-redemption. The woman flees to a prepared wilderness place and is fed for 1,260 days, forty-two months, three and a half years.

This helps explain chapter 11's three and a half days. The prophets testified, were killed, then rose after that period, followed by the final trumpet. The seven years of sin and death, with increased tribulation in the middle, are figurative, not a literal seven years at the end. They depict the whole human lifetime from rejection of God's purpose to this age's end, with Christ's presence in the middle.

Was it Mary who fled to the wilderness like this? We don't know that. He's speaking of Israel, especially Jewish saints, scattered abroad around AD 66–70, preserved among the Gentiles. Then Michael and his angels fight, and the devil is cast down. This is upon redemption, which has a special implication for the devil's hold.

Revelation 12:12 says heaven should rejoice, but earth and sea face woe because the devil has great wrath, knowing his time is short. It's the last, post-redemption phase, with more intense activity. The dragon persecutes the woman who bore the child, a people rather than one woman. Behind-the-scenes activity leads to what happens around AD 70. She receives eagle's wings to her wilderness place for a time, times, and half a time, one year, two years, and half a year.

Three and a half years, three and a half days, 1,260 days, forty-two months, and a time, times, and half a time signify the same post-redemption period. The serpent sends a flood, and the earth helps her by swallowing it. Other nations help the woman. The dragon then makes war on the rest of her offspring, those keeping God's commandments and Jesus' testimony. The focus shifts to persecution of the church, especially Roman persecution.

That's why I say Jewish saints. Revelation 2 and 3 don't assume being Jewish automatically means salvation. Recognize their election for bringing forth Christ, a primary role Paul recognizes in Romans 11, and that some among them are saved. We are grateful for their working out God's redemptive plan to bring the Christ.

## The Beast as Governmental Order

You can read the accounts this way: the seals show Christ opening the script of sin and death to end it; the trumpets show it one-third worse; this account brings us to Christ in the middle and the increased reign after redemption. All end at final judgment.

Revelation 13 shows a beast rising from the sea, with seven heads and ten horns. Later the heads or horns are described as kings. The beast isn't just one entity, though it is also described as Nero Caesar. It represents governmental order, with the seven mountains and such descriptions.

It has a blasphemous name, an anti-Christ purpose that doesn't serve truth. That's the nature of government: it must serve the whole population, not only God's purposes. Even if a Christian tries otherwise, he will still walk in the flesh at the end, proving that point. Some descriptions refer to the Caesar then, his injuries and natural circumstances, but the general principle for us is governance order.

Notice the dragon gives the beast authority. The governmental order is sponsored by the devil and his forces under sin and death. The beast speaks great things and blasphemies and continues forty-two months, throughout the post-redemptive age. It makes war with saints. The blasphemous names refer to rulers claiming to be God.

## The False Prophet as Spiritual and Worship Order

Revelation 13:11 introduces another beast, later called the false prophet. It has two horns like a lamb but speaks like a dragon. Be careful with beliefs that everybody must be one, denying exclusivity, saying you are awakening to the light already inside you. It sounds good, like a lamb, but it is a dragon speaking. Some do it unintentionally, but it's still sponsored by the devil.

This second beast is spiritual order. “We are all saying the same thing; those who disagree haven't seen the light.” That is a false prophet, a dragon looking like a lamb, sounding nice while about to destroy people. It exercises the first beast's authority, causes people to worship that beast, performs signs, even fire from heaven, and deceives. Spiritual order is covered and sponsored by that authority.

## The Mark, Allegiance, and Gematria

In Revelation 13:15 onward, the beast's image speaks and those refusing worship are killed. Great and small, rich and poor, free and slave receive a mark on the right hand or forehead. It's a sign of allegiance. No one buys or sells without the mark, name, or number of the name.

Don't make a big deal out of this. You must use governmental order to function in the world's economic order. A blasphemous ruler claiming to be God was still represented in what you used to buy. It isn't describing an age when you'll refuse to buy and live on mangoes and coconuts. When people asked Jesus about taxes, he said to bring the coin and give the tax. Buying and selling through that order wasn't called wrong. John was clearly describing the king then, Nero.

The practice is gematria, using numerical values for letters, calculating a person's name as numbers. It was normal to refer to someone by that number. It was known to be Nero. He is directly used for things shortly to happen, but with the continuing implication for governmental order generally.

Chapter 14 confronts this with the Lamb on Mount Zion and his own seal. The 144,000 represent those saved among the Jews in the post-redemptive age, showing a full gospel reach. Paul's “all Israel will be saved” speaks of full reach among them as among Gentiles. Don't make it a literal quota with everybody else accompanying them.

They have the Father's name on their foreheads. Revelation 2 and 3 says overcomers, those who believe, have his name and seal. You need not worry about 666. People with that allegiance can repent. Jesus' number is 888. It's argued, depending on the approach, that people knew Jesus' 888 first and then drew a comparison with Nero's 666, or 616 in another language. I think the 888 is particularly in Greek. I'm not sure which language gives Nero 616 or 666, perhaps Latin and Greek.

## The Harvest and Judgment in Revelation 14

The Lord confronts darkness, and the account shows Jewish salvation. The first angel proclaims the gospel everywhere, Revelation 14:6–7, worldwide evangelism and discipleship. The second announces Babylon's fall. The third warns that anyone worshiping the beast and receiving its mark, allegiance against Christ and redemption, drinks God's wrath, poured at full strength into the cup of indignation.

They are tormented with fire and brimstone before the angels and Lamb, with smoke ascending forever and no rest day or night. There are arguments about annihilation or eternal torment. Whichever you think is worse is the right answer. Here is the patience of saints keeping his commandments. Blessed are those who die in the Lord.

Verse 14 brings the Lord's sickle to reap those who are his, followed by judgment at the end of the chapter. That concludes the third account: God's wrath and indignation on the world.

## The Seven Bowls Show the Final Unleashing of Judgment

The fourth eschatological account is Revelation 15:1–16:21, the seven bowls or vials. It reflects the last judgment. Unlike the progressive destruction before, this is the final unleashing, back to back. The first bowl brings foul sores on those with the beast's mark. The second makes the sea like a dead man's blood, and every creature dies. It isn't one third now; it's complete.

The third bowl brings the acknowledgment that God is righteous because they shed saints' blood. Persecution and judgment keep recurring. The fourth scorches people with heat, but they blaspheme and don't repent. The fifth brings darkness to the beast's throne, and they gnaw their tongues and blaspheme. It draws heavily on Egypt's plagues.

The sixth introduces frog-like unclean spirits from dragon, beast, and false prophet: spiritual authority, governance order, and spiritual-worship order. Demonic signs gather people to the great day's battle. This depicts people in rebellion against God before final indignation. People aren't literally gathering for God to show up and fight them. War is a typical description of the day of the Lord, people galvanized against him.

He warns that he comes as a thief; blessed is the one watching and keeping his garment. They gather to Armageddon. The seventh bowl is poured: “It is done.” Noises, thunder, lightning, and an unprecedented earthquake follow, like Revelation 8:5 and 11:19. It's the same final judgment.

## Babylon and the Judgment of Economic, Social, and Moral Order

The fifth account is Revelation 17:1–18:24. Babylon, the woman and harlot, depicts economic, social, and moral order: the pursuit of money and immorality against God. It describes how this pervades every place and nation, using the heads, mountains, and horns again. This economic, social, and moral order or disorder covers every geography, and final judgment comes against it.

## The Judgment of Governance and Spiritual-Worship Order

Revelation 19:1–21 is the sixth account, judgment of governance and spiritual-worship order. Verse 19 sees the beast, the kings of the earth, and their armies gathering against the rider and his army. “The kings of the earth” explains the beast's governance meaning. It resembles chapter 16 and will recur as Gog and Magog in chapter 20.

The beast is captured with the false prophet who deceived through signs, and both are cast into the lake of fire and brimstone. The rest are killed by the sword from the rider's mouth, his spoken judgment, and birds eat their flesh. Jesus appears again, as in chapter 14, because it's the same judgment. Treating it as several chronological comings is a mistake. One end is being described from several angles. This is worship order, not spiritual authority such as the devil; chapter 20 addresses that.

## The Thousand Years and the Present Reign of the Church

Revelation 20:1–15 is the seventh account: judgment of spiritual authority, including sin, death, the devil, and his forces. It tells the account from the devil's angle. Through the church's reign he is considered restrained, though at the tail end he has an overwhelming effect. Other accounts show increased persecution; this one presents restraint through the church.

After the thousand years, Satan is released to deceive nations, Gog and Magog, in earth's four corners. The thousand years describe the church age, the same period depicted as the final three and a half years. It isn't that Jesus has completed everything, the beast and false prophet are gone, and later the devil returns to disturb people left behind. It's the same account from another angle, the present reign of the church.

Revelation 20:4 shows thrones and judgment committed to those sitting on them, souls beheaded for Jesus' witness and God's word, who didn't worship the beast. They live and reign with Christ a thousand years. The rest of the dead don't live until those years are finished. Those who die in this period as believers are still alive. This is the first resurrection. Blessed and holy are those sharing it; the second death has no power over them.

Revelation 2:11 says the second death has no power over gospel believers. They have experienced the first, spiritual resurrection. Unbelievers in this thousand years are considered dead, believers alive and reigning with Christ. There's still a second resurrection. They are priests of God and Christ, reigning on earth, as kings and priests are repeatedly described.

At the period's end, Satan deceives nations and gathers them against the saints' camp and beloved city. There is intense persecution, or intended intense persecution, at the tail end. Fire comes from heaven and consumes them. The devil is cast into the lake of fire where beast and false prophet are, tormented day and night forever. Judgment follows, and death and Hades themselves are cast into the lake. Sin, death, devil, and his forces are judged.

## New Heavens and New Earth and the Book's Full Outline

The final part is Revelation 21:1–22:21, the end of mortality and corruption and the entrance of the new heaven and new earth. We have a ten-part outline: chapter 1's introduction; 2:1–3:22, the seven churches; 4:1–8:1/5, seven seals; 8:1/5–11:19, seven trumpets; 12:1–14:20, persecution of Jewish saints and the church, with judgment following; 15:1–16:21, seven bowls; 17:1–18:24, economic, social, and moral judgment; 19:1–21, governance and spiritual-worship judgment; 20:1–15, spiritual authority judged; and 21:1–22:21, new heavens and new earth.

One detail I didn't explain in Revelation 9: I believe we argued that demons were released to make people walk in the flesh, with fleshly and suicidal tendencies as part of agential forces, particularly on unbelievers. I remember something about five months, but I'm trying to remember the arguments. It specifically says those of faith were not affected. I mention it because that's the part I didn't really explain.

Our goal for a single teaching is complete, though your understanding of the whole book isn't complete. You need the full verse-by-verse teaching. But reading Revelation with this should begin to make sense. The author had immediate intentions, yet governance, economic, social and moral orders, and the devil's behind-the-scenes activity still operate today.

Remember the end is near. We will be accountable and judged. Live for God's purpose, fulfill his mission, and be inspired to live righteously. Revelation isn't saying weird things beyond the other Scriptures. The same truth is in the Olivet Discourse and epistles, expressed here graphically and figuratively for the Lord's intended purpose.
