## Psychology

### Two-Track Analysis
- Use a two-track analysis:
  - First, identify the factors that really govern the interests involved, rationally considered.
  - Second, identify the [[Subconscious Influences]] where the brain automatically reaches conclusions.
- One track is [[Rationality]]: work through the problem like an engineer would work through a bridge problem, evaluating the real interests, real probabilities, and real constraints.
- The other track is [[Psychology]]: evaluate the subconscious forces that cause automatic conclusions, many of which are useful in ordinary life but often misfunction.

## Microeconomics

### Economy as Ecosystem
- An economy is a lot like an [[Ecosystem]].
- Just as animals flourish in niches, people and businesses can flourish by specializing in narrow niches.
- People who specialize in the business world, and get very good because they specialize, frequently find good economics that they would not get any other way.

### Advantages of Scale
- Once you get into microeconomics, you get into [[Advantages of Scale]].
- This matters deeply for investment analysis because scale often determines which businesses succeed and which businesses fail.
- Advantages of scale can come from several different sources.

#### Experience Curve
- A major advantage of scale is cost reduction along the [[Experience Curve]].
- Doing something complicated in more and more volume allows motivated people to improve the process and make it more efficient.

#### Geometry and Physical Reality
- Some advantages of scale come from simple physical reality.
- If you build a large circular tank, the surface area rises with the square while cubic volume rises with the cube.
- As the dimensions increase, the tank can hold much more volume per unit of steel.
- This kind of scale advantage is not a theory layered on top of reality; it comes directly from the structure of reality.

#### Advertising Scale
- Television advertising created a huge advantage for large branded companies.
- In the early days of network TV, a few networks controlled most of the audience.
- A company like [[Procter & Gamble]] could afford network television because it sold so many cans and bottles.
- A smaller competitor could not buy the same advertising in useful partial units.
- When TV arrived, large branded companies received a huge tailwind and prospered for a long time, until some became fat and foolish from prosperity.

#### Informational Advantage
- Scale can become an informational advantage.
- If a customer sees [[Wrigley]] chewing gum next to an unknown brand like Glotz's, the known brand has an advantage.
- Even if Wrigley costs slightly more, the buyer already knows it is satisfactory.
- That familiarity lowers perceived risk and makes the customer less willing to experiment with an unknown alternative.

#### Social Proof
- Scale also creates psychological advantages through [[Social Proof]].
- People are influenced by what they see others do and approve.
- If everyone is buying something, we tend to think it is better.
- Sometimes this happens subconsciously; other times we consciously reason that other people may know more than we do.

#### Distribution
- Worldwide distribution can become a huge scale advantage.
- A small soft drink company cannot easily make its product available everywhere.
- A large enterprise that slowly wins a global distribution setup becomes very hard to dislodge.

#### Specialization Inside the Firm
- Large scale allows greater specialization within a company.
- As each person specializes, each person can become better at what they do.
- This is one reason [[Jack Welch]] pushed [[General Electric]] to be number one or number two in every field it entered, or else exit that field.

### Disadvantages of Scale

And there are also disadvantages of scale. For example, we—by which I mean Berkshire Hathaway—are the largest shareholder in Capital Cities/ABC. And we had trade publications there that got murdered, where our competitors beat us. And the way they beat us was by going to a narrower specialization. We’d have a travel magazine for business travel, so somebody would create one that was addressed solely at corporate travel departments. Like an ecosystem, you’re getting a narrower and narrower specialization. 
Well, they got much more efficient. They could tell more to the guys who ran corporate travel departments. Plus, they didn’t have to waste the ink and paper mailing out stuff that corporate travel departments weren’t interested in reading. It was a more efficient system. And they beat our brains out as we relied on our broader magazine.

So occasionally, scaling down and intensifying gives you the big advantage. Bigger is not always better.

The great defect of scale, of course, which makes the game interesting, so that the big people don’t always win, is that as you get big, you get the bureaucracy. And with the bureaucracy comes the territoriality—which is again grounded in human nature. And the incentives are perverse. For example, if you worked for AT & T in my day, it was a great bureaucracy. Who in the hell was really thinking about the shareholder or anything else? And in a bureaucracy, you think the work is done when it goes out of your in basket into somebody else’s in basket. But, of course, it isn’t. It’s not done until AT & T delivers what it’s supposed to deliver. So you get big, fat, dumb, unmotivated bureaucracies.

## Investment Takeaway
- Munger's point is not that one model explains everything.
- The point is to build a latticework of models from many disciplines: mathematics, statistics, engineering, psychology, and microeconomics.
- In business analysis, especially investing, these models combine. Scale advantages can be physical, informational, psychological, operational, or distribution-based.
- The best analysis asks which models apply, how they interact, and whether the business has durable economics because of them.
