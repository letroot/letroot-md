# Talk Two: A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom as It Relates to Investment Management and Business

## Core Disciplines

### Mathematics
- [[Compound Interest]]
- [[Permutation and Combination]]
- [[Probability]]

### Statistics
- Understand probability distributions, including the [[Normal Distribution]].

### Engineering
- The engineering idea of a [[Backup System]] is a powerful model.
- The engineering idea of [[Breakpoints]] is also powerful.
- The notion of [[Critical Mass]], which comes out of physics, is another powerful model.

## Psychology

### Two-Track Analysis
- Use a two-track analysis:
  - First, identify the factors that really govern the interests involved, rationally considered.
  - Second, identify the [[Subconscious Influences]] where the brain automatically reaches conclusions.
- One track is [[Rationality]]: work through the problem like an engineer would work through a bridge problem, evaluating the real interests, real probabilities, and real constraints.
- The other track is [[Psychology]]: evaluate the subconscious forces that cause automatic conclusions, many of which are useful in ordinary life but often misfunction.

### Pavlovian Association
- [[Pavlovian Association]] can cause people to dislike the messenger who brings unpleasant truth.
- Munger uses [[CBS]] as an example: if people tell you what you do not want to hear, there is an almost automatic reaction of antipathy.
- This is trainable. It is not foredestined that you must react this way, but you will tend to react this way if you do not think about it.

## Microeconomics

### Economy as Ecosystem
- An economy is a lot like an [[Ecosystem]].
- Just as organisms flourish in niches, people and businesses can flourish by specializing in narrow niches.
- People who specialize in the business world, and get very good because they specialize, frequently find good economics that they would not get any other way.

## Scale

### Advantages of Scale
- Once you get into microeconomics, you get into [[Advantages of Scale]].
- This matters deeply for investment analysis because scale often determines which businesses succeed and which businesses fail.
- Advantages of scale can come from several different sources.

#### Experience Curve
- A major advantage of scale is cost reduction along the [[Experience Curve]].
- Doing something complicated in more and more volume allows motivated people to improve the process and make it more efficient.

#### Geometry and Physical Reality
- Some advantages of scale come from simple physical reality.
- If you build a large circular tank, the surface area rises with the square while cubic volume rises with the cube.
- As the dimensions increase, the tank can hold much more volume per unit of steel.
- This kind of scale advantage is not a theory layered on top of reality; it comes directly from the structure of reality.

#### Advertising Scale
- Television advertising created a huge advantage for large branded companies.
- In the early days of network TV, a few networks controlled most of the audience.
- A company like [[Procter & Gamble]] could afford network television because it sold so many cans and bottles.
- A smaller competitor could not buy the same advertising in useful partial units.
- When TV arrived, large branded companies received a huge tailwind and prospered for a long time, until some became fat and foolish from prosperity.

#### Informational Advantage
- Scale can become an informational advantage.
- If a customer sees [[Wrigley]] chewing gum next to an unknown brand like Glotz's, the known brand has an advantage.
- Even if Wrigley costs slightly more, the buyer already knows it is satisfactory.
- That familiarity lowers perceived risk and makes the customer less willing to experiment with an unknown alternative.

#### Social Proof
- Scale also creates psychological advantages through [[Social Proof]].
- People are influenced by what they see others do and approve.
- If everyone is buying something, we tend to think it is better.
- Sometimes this happens subconsciously; other times we consciously reason that other people may know more than we do.

#### Distribution
- Worldwide distribution can become a huge scale advantage.
- A small soft drink company cannot easily make its product available everywhere.
- A large enterprise that slowly wins a global distribution setup becomes very hard to dislodge.

#### Specialization Inside the Firm
- Large scale allows greater specialization within a company.
- As each person specializes, each person can become better at what they do.
- This is one reason [[Jack Welch]] pushed [[General Electric]] to be number one or number two in every field it entered, or else exit that field.

### Disadvantages of Scale
- Bigger is not always better. Sometimes scaling down and intensifying gives the big advantage.
- Berkshire Hathaway's stake in [[Capital Cities/ABC]] exposed Munger to trade publications that were beaten by narrower competitors.
- A broad business travel magazine could be beaten by a publication addressed solely to corporate travel departments:
  - It could tell corporate travel departments more of what they cared about.
  - It did not waste ink, paper, or mailing costs on readers who did not care about that narrow topic.
  - It was a more efficient system because the niche was tighter.
- This is the economy-as-ecosystem model again: niches keep narrowing, and narrower specialists can become more efficient than broader incumbents.

#### Bureaucracy
- The great defect of scale is [[Bureaucracy]].
- With bureaucracy comes territoriality, which Munger sees as grounded in human nature.
- Incentives become perverse. In a bureaucracy, a person may feel the work is done when it leaves their inbox, even though the customer outcome has not been delivered.
- Munger uses [[AT&T]] as the example of a large bureaucracy where few people were really thinking about the shareholder or the whole system.
- Large organizations can become fat, slow, unmotivated, and internally focused.
- Life in business is an ongoing battle between two forces:
  - The advantages of scale.
  - The tendency of large organizations to drift into useless bureaucracy.

#### Walmart Versus Sears
- [[Walmart]] is Munger's example of fanatic execution defeating an older incumbent with scale advantages.
- [[Sam Walton]] started from a single store in Arkansas and blew past [[Sears]] in his own lifetime.
- Walton did not invent much. He copied what worked and executed it with more fanaticism, discipline, and better employee manipulation than competitors.
- The lesson is that scale plus fanaticism can be extremely powerful.
- Sears still had name, reputation, capital, and many real strengths, but it was weighed down by bureaucracy:
  - Too many layers.
  - Slow thinking.
  - Established ways of thinking.
  - Internal resistance when someone raised a new thought.
- In time, Sears' advantages of scale were not enough to prevent it from losing heavily to Walmart and similar retailers.

## Technology and Competitive Destruction

### Technology Can Help or Kill
- The great lesson in microeconomics is to discriminate between when [[Technology]] will help you and when it will kill you.
- A wonderful invention does not automatically create owner value.
- In a bad commodity business, productivity improvements often flow through to customers instead of owners.

### Textile Mill Example
- Berkshire was once in the textile business, producing low-end commodity textiles.
- When managers proposed buying a new loom that could do twice as much work, [[Warren Buffett]] said he hoped it would not work, because if it did, he would close the mill.
- His logic:
  - The textile business had substandard returns.
  - Berkshire was keeping it open partly to be decent to elderly workers.
  - It made no sense to put large new capital into a lousy business.
  - The productivity gains would not stay with the owners; competition would pass them through to textile buyers.
- The machinery seller and internal advocates usually show projections based on savings at current prices.
- They usually skip the second step: determining how much of those savings will stay with the business and how much will flow through to customers.
- The equipment may truly pay for itself in three years on paper, while the business still earns poor returns over twenty years because the benefits do not stick.

### Competitive Destruction
- Fast-moving technology creates what Munger calls [[Competitive Destruction]].
- A business can be excellent within an old technology and still be destroyed by a new one.
- The point is not just that new inventions arrive. It is that they can make the old business economically irrelevant.
- The owner must either get into a different business or be destroyed.

### Surfing
- New businesses can give huge advantages to early birds.
- Munger calls this the [[Surfing]] model: when someone catches the right wave early and stays on it, they can travel a long way.
- If they get off the wave, they can become stuck in the shallows.
- Examples include [[Microsoft]], [[Intel]], and [[National Cash Register]] in its early days.
- Munger points to National Cash Register's early reports under Patterson as the sort of opportunity an investor should look for: a business with powerful methods, clear objectives, and a large wave behind it.
- In a long life, an investor can expect to profit heavily from at least a few such opportunities if they develop the wisdom and will to seize them.

## Circle of Competence
- You have to figure out your own aptitudes.
- If you play games where other people have the aptitude and you do not, you are going to lose.
- You need to know where you have an edge and stay within your [[Circle of Competence]].
- Munger contrasts becoming the best tennis player in the world with becoming the best plumbing contractor in Bemidji:
  - The first may be impossible if others have far more aptitude.
  - The second may be attainable for many people with enough discipline, work, and accumulated local knowledge.
- People who cannot win in elite global competition can still rise quite high by slowly developing competence in the right domain.
- Competence comes partly from what you were born with and partly from what you slowly develop through work.

## Stock Picking

### Pari-Mutuel Thinking
- Munger moves from worldly wisdom into stock picking by comparing investing to [[Pari-Mutuel Betting]].
- Any rational person can see that a strong horse with a light weight, strong win record, and good post position is more likely to win than a weak horse with extra weight.
- But the odds may already reflect that. The bad horse may pay 100 to 1 while the good horse pays 3 to 2.
- Once the price changes, it is no longer obvious which is the best statistical bet.
- The racetrack also takes a large percentage off the top, so a bettor must overcome both the other bettors and the house take.

### Mispriced Bets
- Intelligence can create an edge, but frictional costs matter.
- A smart horseplayer may reduce the average loss from the house take but still lose overall.
- Munger knew someone who made a living betting harness races because that market was less efficient than regular races.
- The key was not constant betting. He waited until he found a mispriced bet, then acted.
- The market was efficient, but not perfectly efficient.
- The stock market is similar, except the house take is much lower if you avoid excessive trading.
- With enough shrewdness, fanaticism, and discipline, some people will do much better than average.

### Bet Heavily When the Odds Are Yours
- Human beings cannot know everything about everything all the time.
- But people who work hard, sift the world, and look for mispriced bets can occasionally find one.
- The wise ones bet heavily when the world offers the opportunity.
- The rest of the time, they do not.
- Munger argues that you do not need many great insights in a lifetime.
- At Berkshire Hathaway, most of the money came from a small number of major insights.
- Investment results can be remarkable if you think like a winning pari-mutuel player:
  - Treat investing as a game full of noise, nonsense, and occasional mispricing.
  - Do not expect to find thousands of opportunities.
  - When a true opportunity appears, load up.

### The Twenty-Punch Card
- Buffett's business-school thought experiment: imagine you receive a lifetime investment ticket with only twenty punches.
- Once the punches are gone, you cannot make any more investments.
- Under those rules, you would think much more carefully.
- You would be forced to load up on what you had really studied.
- Munger thinks the winner must bet very selectively.

## Incentives

### Investor Versus Manager Incentives
- What makes sense for the investor is often different from what makes sense for the investment manager.
- Munger illustrates this with the fishing-tackle story:
  - The lures were purple and green.
  - When asked whether fish really take those lures, the seller replied that he was not selling to fish.
- In investment management, the product is often designed to sell to clients rather than to produce the best investor outcome.
- As usual in human affairs, behavior is determined by the incentives facing the decision maker.
- Getting incentives right is a central lesson.

### Federal Express Example
- Munger's favorite business case on incentives is [[Federal Express]].
- The heart of the system was having all airplanes arrive in one place in the middle of the night and transfer packages from plane to plane.
- If that sorting operation was delayed, Federal Express could not deliver an integrated, reliable product to customers.
- The operation was repeatedly late despite moral suasion, threats, and other attempts to fix it.
- The problem cleared up when workers were paid by the shift rather than by the hour:
  - They received the shift pay when the work was done.
  - Once the packages were sorted, they could go home.
- The incentive changed the behavior immediately because the employees' interest finally matched the system's desired outcome.

## Investment Takeaway
- Munger's point is not that one model explains everything.
- The point is to build a latticework of models from many disciplines: mathematics, statistics, engineering, psychology, microeconomics, and incentives.
- In business analysis, especially investing, these models combine.
- Scale advantages can be physical, informational, psychological, operational, or distribution-based.
- Scale disadvantages often arise from bureaucracy, territoriality, poor incentives, and loss of contact with narrower customer needs.
- Technology is good for owners only when the economic benefits can be retained by the business instead of competed away to customers.
- Circle of competence matters because edge depends on aptitude, discipline, and domain fit.
- The best analysis asks:
  - Which models apply?
  - How do they interact?
  - Does the business have durable economics because of them?
  - Is the opportunity mispriced enough to justify a concentrated bet?
