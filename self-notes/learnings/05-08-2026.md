## Source
[Using the Needs Stack for competitive strategy](https://longform.asmartbear.com/needs-stack/) — Jason Cohen / A Smart Bear

## Main Learnings

### The Needs Stack
Customers rarely want the product they appear to be buying. They want a higher-level outcome, and the product is only one step in the chain of means that leads to that outcome.

A customer may appear to want:

- Infrastructure
- A WordPress site
- A personal website
- An audience and book deal
- Speaking opportunities

But the deeper need may be recognition, independence, status, creative expression, or financial success. The stack is a hierarchy of increasingly valuable outcomes, with lower levels serving as means to higher ones.

The strategic question is not only, “What problem does our product solve?” It is also, “What does solving that problem help the customer accomplish next?”

### Higher-Level Products Make Lower-Level Products Irrelevant
AWS, WP Engine, WordPress, Squarespace, Substack, and speaker marketplaces can all be competitors for the same customer, even though they operate at different levels of the stack.

A customer who wants a WordPress website may buy infrastructure directly from AWS. But if WP Engine makes the website easy to create and maintain, the customer may never enter the infrastructure market at all. WP Engine itself may still buy infrastructure from AWS, but it hides that lower-level need from the customer.

This is the important competitive insight: a company can be disrupted by a product that does not resemble it, because the competitor is satisfying a higher-level need and eliminating the customer’s need to think about the lower level.

### Up the Stack Means More Convenience; Down the Stack Means More Flexibility
Products higher in the Needs Stack usually help customers reach their end goal faster and with less effort. The trade-off is reduced flexibility, customization, and control.

Products lower in the stack usually expose more power and customization, but require the customer to do more work and understand more complexity. AWS offers more infrastructure control than WP Engine. WordPress offers more customization than Substack. The right choice depends on whether the customer values control or progress toward the higher-level outcome.

### Features and Benefits Depend on Where the Customer Is Thinking
“Sell benefits, not features” is incomplete advice. Customers do not always want to hear about their highest-level aspiration. They may currently be thinking about a concrete step lower in the stack.

Someone searching for “how to get a WordPress site” wants to understand features and implementation. But the product can still connect those features to the next level up: “Become famous online with a customizable WordPress website.”

A useful messaging formula is:

- Match the level the customer is actively considering.
- Explain why your product is the best way to accomplish that immediate need.
- Connect it to the next higher-level benefit.

### Position Lower Levels as Irrelevant
A product does not need to beat competitors on their strongest terrain. It can move the conversation to a higher level where the customer cares about a different outcome.

Substack should not compete with WordPress on customization. It can instead say, in effect, “You do not really want a website; you want to build an audience.” WP Engine can make infrastructure irrelevant by saying, “You do not want servers; you want a functioning WordPress site with support and security.”

The best positioning often reframes the category around what the customer ultimately wants, making the lower-level alternatives feel like unnecessary work.

### Moving Up the Stack Can Create More Value—and More Risk
Moving up the stack can create a more valuable product, but it usually means becoming a different business. The company may need a new product, brand, business model, expertise, and customer segment.

There are also practical limits:

- The next outcome may be difficult or impossible for software to guarantee.
- The higher-level market may be much smaller and more specialized.
- The company may lose the broad market that supported its existing business.
- The new offer may require services, partnerships, or performance-based pricing rather than software subscriptions.

A full pivot is not always necessary. Companies can move partially upward through vertical products, add-ons, education, partnerships, customer stories, and better marketing.

### Measure the Next Level Up
Operational metrics show whether the company is delivering its direct promise. Strategic metrics show whether customers are succeeding at the next level up.

For Shopify, creating an online store is the immediate outcome. But the customer’s higher-level need is to sell products. This is why [[GMV]] is strategically important: it measures whether customers are actually succeeding as merchants, not merely whether they created a store.

The next-level metric may be harder to control, but it is often the best indicator of long-term retention and customer value. A company should measure it, learn from it, and build features or education that help customers improve it.

### The Needs Stack Reveals Purpose
The higher levels of the stack can explain why the company exists beyond conversion rates, activation, and retention. A website may help someone build a career. A marketing tool may help an employee become a hero inside an organization. A publishing platform may help someone express their ideas and earn independence.

These outcomes provide a more meaningful source of [[Purpose]] for both customers and employees. The company should identify and tell those stories because the higher-level transformation is ultimately what makes the operational work worthwhile.

## Application to Building SaaS

The first version of a SaaS product is often positioned too low in the Needs Stack. It describes the mechanism—dashboard, automation, workflow, analytics, integrations—rather than the customer’s meaningful outcome.

For any product, map the stack explicitly:

1. What does the product technically do?
2. What immediate job does that enable?
3. What business result does that job produce?
4. What personal or strategic outcome does that result create for the customer?
5. What is the customer ultimately trying to become, achieve, avoid, or prove?

Then use the stack in three places:

- **Positioning:** Describe the higher-level result without pretending the product controls everything.
- **Product strategy:** Add features, services, education, or partnerships that help customers move toward that result.
- **Metrics:** Track the next-level outcome, not only the activity your software directly controls.

The main caution is to avoid making grand promises that the product cannot deliver. If the highest-level result depends on many external factors, position it as the direction or benefit while being precise about the result the product directly owns.

## Action Items

- [ ] Map the Needs Stack for your SaaS: mechanism, immediate job, business outcome, and customer’s higher-level aspiration.
- [ ] Identify the competitors operating one level above and one level below your current product.
- [ ] Rewrite your homepage headline so it connects the product’s immediate function to the next-level customer benefit.
- [ ] Choose one next-level metric that better predicts customer success and retention than your current activation metric.
- [ ] List one feature, service, partnership, or educational resource that would help customers progress up the stack.
- [ ] Write down the higher purpose your product enables for customers and use it to sharpen both positioning and internal motivation.
